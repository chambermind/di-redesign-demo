Priest ‘was source’ of RTE’s false allegation; Kenyan woman at centre of rape story says an Irish cleric made the allegation,

write Tom Lyons and Colin Coyle  4 December 2011 The Sunday Times
© 2011 Times Newspapers Ltd. All rights reserved
THE Kenyan woman at the centre of an investigation into how RTE falsely accused Fr Kevin Reynolds of fathering her child has claimed that a priest was the source of the Prime Time story.
In an interview with The Sunday Times, Veneranda Mudi said that she was approached by RTE to tell her story after a priest based in Kenya told Prime Time reporters she had been raped as a teenager by an Irish priest. She said the priest who passed on this information knew her for many years.
“He directed them [RTE] to our whereabouts, to Sheila’s [her daughter’s] secondary school,” she said. “They got her picture and came to Nairobi and my church. From there they came to our house and then Sheila’s place of work.”
The accused priest, whose identity is known to The Sunday Times, could not be contacted for comment.
Mudi said that she was reluctant to talk further about why she had falsely accused Reynolds of raping her and fathering Sheila. “I don’t want to push it further,” she said. “We have our own lives to live. I am married and Sheila also is married. We don’t want this story to disturb our lives.”
Mudi declined to name the real father of Sheila, who is lighter skinned then her halfsisters and brothers. The allegations she made against Reynolds, broadcast in a documentary entitled A Mission to Prey on May 23, were subsequently proved to be false following a paternity test.
Mudi confirmed Sheila’s school fees had been paid when she was in form three, the equivalent of fifth year in Ireland, and said she “did not know” who had done this.
During an interview at St Cuan’s church in Ahascragh, Co Galway on May 7, Aoife Kavanagh, a Prime Time reporter, told Reynolds that a local teacher named Mactilda “knew about the child”. Reynolds said he did not know a Mactilda.
Mudi believes that Mactilda referred to Matilda Amuhaya, a principal at a school that Sheila had attended.
She said she never discussed her daughter with Amuhaya, but admitted the principal may have heard “a story” from another teacher in Eregi Teachers’ College, where Mudi worked as a teacher’s housemaid in the 1980s.
Kavanagh had claimed in an email to Reynolds’s solicitor five days before broadcast that she had a “very credible third party source” substantiating her allegations.
In an email the reporter said she had “other independent evidence confirming that [Reynolds] contributed financially to the education of his daughter Sheila”. She asked Reynolds to reconsider his position given the “assertions of a number of people, independent of each other” that he was the father.
Mudi said she now regrets speaking to RTE. She lives in a tin hut in a slum in Nairobi. She shares the accommodation with her husband, two daughters and two sons, and makes her living selling vegetables outside her home.
Her daughter, Sheila, works as a nursery-school teacher and is the only member of the family with a steady job. Two weeks ago she lost her home when it was destroyed by the Kenyan government as part of a project to clear land and expand a road. Her husband has since suffered a suspected heart attack and is currently in hospital.
Mudi said telling her story had caused her huge stress, potentially damaging her reputation, and placing a strain on her marriage. “We have been living a difficult life of worries and sleepless nights,” she said.
