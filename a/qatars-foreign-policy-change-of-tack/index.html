Qatar’s foreign policy ~ Change of tack

Conferences & Commentaries

Jul 15th 2013, by N.P.| DOHA

 ECONOMIST

  Qatar’s emir, Tamim

EVER the astute investor, Qatar is beginning to worry about throwing good money after bad. The tiny but mega-rich emirate had poured $8 billion in Egypt since the revolution two-and-a-half years ago and perhaps another $9 billion to bankroll Islamists in Libya, Syria and Gaza, the Palestinian enclave run by Hamas, an offshoot of the Muslim Brotherhood. But from Qatar’s point of view these places suddenly, since the fall of Egypt’s Islamist president, Muhammad Morsi, no longer look like the winning horses they once did. Loathe to see more money go to waste, Qataris now speak of a new mood of pragmatism among their leaders. Words like “reassessment”, “recalibration” and “corrections” pepper Qataris’ discussion of their foreign policy.

 

A change at the top has facilitated the debate. Last month the two architects of Qatar’s Islamist-hugging foreign policy stepped down. Qatar’s emir, Hamad bin Khalifa al-Thani, abdicated in favour of his son, Tamim (pictured). The once voluble prime minister and cousin of the former emir, Hamad bin Jassem al-Thani, who had moulded Qatar’s punchy foreign policy, has been silenced, to be replaced by a shadowy military man who had been serving as deputy interior minister.

In his inaugural speech, the new emir softened his support for Islamist revolutions and called for an end to “arrogant” policies, suggesting he would curb his father’s foreign adventures. The new man made no mention of Hamas or of any of the other Islamist movements his father used to fund. And he joined his fellow Gulf monarchs, including those of Abu Dhabi and Saudi Arabia, who loathe the Brothers, in welcoming Egypt’s coup against the country’s first elected president.

The new regime also indirectly rebuked Sheikh Yusuf Qaradawi, the Islamist mentor who has used his pulpit on Qatar’s state-owned satellite channel, Al Jazeera, to promote the Islamists’ rise to power. Qatari newspapers published a letter, reportedly written by the preacher’s son, denouncing his father for continuing to support Mr Morsi. “We agreed with him [Mr Morsi] that he would install a participatory government, but he didn’t keep his word,” says the younger Qaradawi. “Qatar’s foreign policy is collapsing around it,” grins an Egyptian official, delighting in what he hopes is the Islamists’ loss of their major patron.

So far the new emir’s reserve towards the Brotherhood has only partially satisfied fellow Gulf leaders, who long blamed his father for fostering revolutions that might yet stoke uprisings against them. Elsewhere, those the Qataris once supported have struck back. Libya’s police have repainted the cars Qatar gave them, from maroon-and-white—the colours of the Qatari flag—to blue-and-white. Egyptian journalists recently chased Al Jazeera correspondents from a press conference in Cairo.

Some people suggest that, following promises of loans and gifts from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates totalling $12 billion, the Qataris could restore their reputation by switching their largesse from the Islamists to Egypt’s new rulers. As a further gesture, some call, among other things, for Mr Qaradawi’s expulsion from Qatar. But others suggest it is time for Qatar to take a more backseat approach and put its people first. “Why are we wasting our money on people who burn our flags?” asks Ali Khalifa al-Kuwari, an academic who, under the previous emir, used to criticise Qatar’s foreign policy.
