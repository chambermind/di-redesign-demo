THE BOY IN THE ATTIC by DAVID MALONE Review by Brady, Niall. Sunday Times 23 Oct 2011

BooksSatanism

Murder most forgotten:  An account of the savage killing of a young child by a teenager in 1973 and how it was barely reported is well researched but reveals frustratingly little new.

By Brady, Niall. Sunday Times 23 Oct 2011:

THE BOY IN THE ATTIC by DAVID MALONE Mainstream Publishing Pounds 12.99 pp223 Shortly before last Christmas, David Malone, a journalist and television producer, stumbled across a Canadian newspaper clipping from 1973 reporting the arrest of a teenager on suspicion of murdering a neighbour’s child in Palmerstown, Co Dublin. The body had been found in the attic of the teenager’s home, and the story quoted garda sources as stating that the dead boy’s hands were “nailed to the attic rafters in a crude form of crucifixion”.

Those same garda sources were apparently not talking to the Irish newspapers, which had reported the death but not the gruesome details. The mystery deepened for Malone when a trawl of the National Archives in Dublin failed to yield a death certificate for the murdered boy. The lost boy: Horgan; The culprit was Lorcan Bale, 16, a troubled youth obsessed by the occult. His victim was John Horgan, 7, who died in what appeared to be satanic ritual. Bale was sentenced to penal servitude for life but, after serving just seven years, was released at 23. Malone’s book tries to get behind these bare facts. How did Bale, the son of devout Catholic parents, get so involved with the occult that it led him to murder? Why was the incident, shocking by today’s standards but unthinkable in 1970s Ireland, barely reported? Despite extensive research, Malone fails to answer these questions. He exonerates the Irish media of self-censorship, suggesting instead that the gardai tried to cover up the sordid details.

“One can speculate that some of the more unpleasant aspects of the murder, most especially the satanic overtones, were omitted out of a belief that they may have caused some kind of moral panic,” he writes.

Could journalists of the time not function independently? Did they not speak to locals in Palmerstown, where lurid stories circulated freely after the murder? Did they not read foreign newspapers that, as Malone learnt 37 years later, were far more on top of the story? There is no evidence that the author tried to track down anybody working in a Dublin newsroom in 1973 for answers. Perhaps the Irish newspapers were playing it safe, waiting for the macabre details to be revealed in evidence during a murder trial, only to be frustrated when Bale pleaded guilty, and Horgan’s death was reduced to an open and-shut case.

Malone sets himself a risky challenge in attempting to uncover Bale’s motives because, without his co-operation, the answers are impossible. The extent of contacts between the two is not revealed until the final chapter. It is obvious that Bale had refused to tell all long before Malone was forced to conclude: “We still don’t know what Lorcan Bale was thinking and feeling as he sat in that attic staring at a dead child.”

To fill the gaps, he resorts to padding that adds little to the story — the history of Palmerstown, an IRA jailbreak from the prison next door to the institution where Bale was incarcerated as a juvenile, and murders in Dublin and Leitrim that had nothing to do with the Horgan case.

Malone deserves credit for his exhaustive research and for uncovering the state’s failure to issue a death certificate. This appears to have been an administrative mistake, and led to the inquest being hastily reconvened in May — almost four decades after the murder.

The book is respectful, avoiding the crude sensationalism that might have resulted from less sensitive hands. In particular, Bale’s privacy is always respected.

We learn only that he now lives abroad, is married, a committed Christian and has a secure office job. He has been tracked down by tabloid newspapers, though, which are far less timid than their 1970s predecessors, and now Bale must cope with a fresh investigation into a part of his past which for many years seemed to have been forgotten about.
