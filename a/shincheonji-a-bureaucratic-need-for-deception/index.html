Shincheonji – A Bureaucratic Need for Deception.

CultismShincheonji

Encounters with Shincheonji harvesters and leaves (recruiters) report that they will go to great, calculated lengths to deceive and win over recruits (AKA fruits), but are churches and universities in Ireland aware of Shincheonji’s activities targeting students and graduates and the damage they’re causing to individuals and their families?

Shincheonji’s alleged meeting place on Baggot Street, Dublin

(Source: Google Maps)

Any successful grift or scam requires withholding misinformation—or the provision of misleading information—which creates an imbalance of power that favours the grifter and disadvantages the victim, target, or recipient on the receiving end of the ruse.

Without some form of trickery, an attempt to manipulate behaviour or thinking towards an outcome providing favourable returns to the deceiver is impossible. Deception is the means and manipulation is the end, although in cultism contexts, the terms ‘brainwashing’ and ‘thought control/reform’ might be terms applied to the outcome.

And in the pantheon of cultic grift, Korean ‘doomsday/apocalypic’ group Shincheonji has earned itself more than just an honourable mention, taking a bureaucratic approach to deception in its bid to recruit members.

Harvesters (recruiters) will make contact with fruits through a variety of ways: dating apps, Christian message boards, university campuses during fresher’s week; however, they’ll never disclose their real identity or connection.

Lying is part of Shincheonji’s harvesting handbook. They’re trained to lie about their age and hobbies, to feign an interest in a topic, or claim to share some common ground to build a connection with potential recruits. During this process, they will also try to identify weaknesses and vulnerabilities:


The spreadsheets included personal details including whether potential recruits were Christian, how they were recruited and goals and personal information about their lives.

One note mentioned the “fruit” had broken up with her boyfriend and how another had a health issue they were concerned about.

Another entry, marked in red, noted one of the recruits had dropped out and had been “confirmed poisoned”.

Messages in a WhatsApp group detailed a meeting one member had with a “fruit” and added a list of goals including “dig deep into her loneliness”.

What may seem on the surface like polite small talk about a person’s background, or benign questions about education, work, and family, are deliberate attempts to collect information in the process of deciding whether an individual. Shincheonji encounters in Australia report:

Factors that might make someone a good target include being lonely, religious or open to religion, or newly moved to Australia and on the path to permanent residency.

These were all outlined in a training video for senior church leader recruiters, seen by Guardian Australia. The video tells recruiters to research potential recruits including their culture and interests and to pretend to share those interests.

Christian churches, I thought, at the very least, were supposedly welcoming places where a stranger might be able to find an open door and someone willing to help; extending kindness not just to their immediate congregation, but to their friends, family members, and to the broader community they support and serve.

And it should probably come as no surprise that a ‘church’ with such a reputation would regularly gather privately in a place with no sign. It has none of the usual decoration and symbolism you’d associate with a place of worship, and nothing to give away what happens inside during their clandestine meetings.

The location (according to sources and former members): An unassuming black door on Upper Baggot Street in Dublin 4, permanently locked, unwelcoming to non-members and the public, where the promise of ‘free Bible discussion’ begins for those lured in by the prospect of meeting new, like-minded people. In reality, what awaits is a carefully curated and micro-managed charade to avoid new recruits interacting:

Church leaders are told in training materials to accompany their recruit as much as possible, including to the toilet, while at church buildings to ensure they don’t interact with other new members, or try to leave.

“Do not let fruits leave the centre to go home alone, but accompany them to their transport and see them off, to minimise fruit-to-fruit contact,” a leader tells recruiters in one video. Offer to drive them home, or to get food with them after class, she says.

“Fruit-to-fruit contact is dangerous. Stick with the fruit. Be close. Close close.”

Keeping the tree imagery going, and adding yet another level of deception, a large proportion of the ‘new members’ present in these ‘bible discussions/studies’ are Shincheonji plants tasked with monitoring the activities of ‘fruits’ as well as helping the action appear ‘revelatory’ by pretending to be blown away by what they’re hearing.

The room will also be strategically organised to avoid any fruit-to-fruit communication and the possibility of them realising that something’s a bit off about their new ‘friends’:

Gloria may not have been familiar with the recruitment process when she was targeted as a ‘fruit’, but she soon learnt how existing members would minimise interactions between new recruits to control narratives.

“Each fruit has one to two leaves, sometimes three, but it’s pretty rare,” she explains.

“Imagine that there is a row of seats, so the fruits will be sitting in the middle while the leaf will be sitting on their right and the left side of the fruit, so that the fruit that’s in the middle won’t be talking to another fruit on the other side of the seat.

“The leaf will always follow the fruit wherever they go inside that classroom to make sure that the fruit doesn’t talk to another fruit.”

Fruits are discouraged from telling their parents and friends about their involvement; Shincheonji claims their duplicitous behaviour is necessary due to the constant negative press they receive in the media.

However, laundering deceit as a righteous and justifiable activity is a fundamental tenet of their ideology, and this Biblical ‘Wisdom of Hiding’ legitimises deceptive evangelising.

And without concealing their true identity, how could they possibly hope to infiltrate and convert—’Moving a Mountain’ is the Shincheonji term—another Christian church?

The complete flipping of another church and turning the congregation into Shincheonji followers may sound a little ambitious, but they are definitely ones for trying. When the usual subterfuge and skullduggery doesn’t seem to be working, financial incentives are used to achieve the same aim, as recruiters in Africa are discovering:

Across Africa, pastors and lay Christians who would love to attend Bible school but can’t afford to do so are being approached, and seemingly befriended, by strangers offering them the chance to do so for free.

Under the guise of ‘Zion Truth Bible Centers’ or the online course ‘Equipped Bible Seminars,’ African pastors and Christians are being encouraged to ‘change their sign‘:

SCJ offers special training opportunities (sometimes with overseas travel) and seeks formal agreements with unsuspecting pastors with the goal of capturing their churches and changing signboards to something that suits Shincheonji.

Christian churches and universities internationally have proactively responded to negative media coverage and concerns about Shincheonji recruitment within their congregations and on their campuses by sending out newsletters and updating their online materials to warn people of the situation.

(Source: Onslow Community Church)

Another New Zealand church has been very proactive in its efforts to educate and warn its congregation, while the University of Melbourne’s website also has a page warning students of Shincheonji’s activities on and around campuses:

‘They target young, lonely, trusting, faith-searching individuals – all of these terms were directly used to describe myself or others by recruiters and teachers,’ Jack says. ‘Once a relationship of trust is built by conventional studies, more dangerous, dogmatic, isolating and unbiblical teachings are integrated to uproot a person from all of their support systems and completely rely on, trust and work for the cult.’


With younger generations experiencing an epidemic of loneliness, this makes groups like SCJ all the more dangerous. When the desire for truth is manipulated, it becomes a tool of coercion as influence transforms into pressure.

With reports of increased evangelising in response to leader Lee Man-hee’s arrest, and his recent release from detention due to health reasons, and the new academic term underway, students/young Christians, faculty members, parents, and church leaders need to be extremely vigilant.

Dialogue Ireland has recently updated its 2022 overview of Shincheonji, which provides information about its deceptive recruitment practices and background on the group’s international activities.

Dialogue Ireland will be passing this information on to Trinity College, UCD, and churches in the Dublin area, so they can educate those in their charge about the dangers this group poses.
