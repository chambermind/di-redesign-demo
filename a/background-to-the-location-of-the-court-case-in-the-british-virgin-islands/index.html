Background to the location of the court case in the British Virgin Islands.

Tony Quinn

The oil company, International Natural Energy (INE), is incorporated in Nevis, hence the case is being heard at the Commercial Court in the BVI.

This involves a dispute between Sheila McCaffrey, a founder of the company, International Natural Energy (INE) and two directors Tony Quinn and Susan Morrice.

http://www.belizenaturalenergy.bz/news/video.html

http://www.belizenaturalenergy.bz

History

A statutory instrument established the West Indies Associated States Supreme Court in 1967, replacing what had been the Supreme Court of the Windward Islands since 1959.

The Order bringing the Court into existence was extended by Statutory Instrument to encompass the Virgin Islands. The West Indies Associated States Supreme Court was renamed the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court in 1983.

The Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court is comprised of nine Eastern Caribbean States and consists of the High Court and of the Court of Appeal. The High Court of the Virgin Islands is a member of the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court. In the Virgin Islands, the High Court Registry is one of the departments under the Office of the Deputy Governor, and is tasked with providing administrative support to the local Supreme Court and to assist in the dispensation of justice.

The Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court (ECSC) is a superior Court of record for the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS),[1] including six independent states: Antigua and Barbuda, the Commonwealth of Dominica, Grenada, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines and three British Overseas Territories (Anguilla, British Virgin Islands, and Montserrat). It has unlimited jurisdiction in each member State.

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Caribbean_Supreme_Court

The judge Edward Bannister is a QC appointed from England – he is the first ever judge appointed specifically to the Commercial Court in the BVI and has adjudicated in some very big cases involving companies incorporated in BVI, so he is very experienced but will obviously be looking at it entirely from a commercial point of view.  From this point of view, hypnosis is not an issue, if people bought shares, took decisions, signed contracts etc then that is what they did and they have to accept responsibility for this, whatever kind of relationship they had with the other parties.  However, we are pretty sure the law does take into account duress, that is if threats of violence, for instance, or material pressures such as withholding pay or threatening the loss of employment were brought to bear, for example.

The case the court is looking at is Sheila McCaffrey’s two alternative requests – one, that her shares should be purchased so that she receives the money they are worth or two, that the company be wound up so that all shareholders receive a proportionate share of the sale price achieved by the liquidators for the assets.  This is a very complex case – winding up a company of this size, which is profitable and actively trading, is an unusual undertaking and a huge responsibility for a court to decide – and is complicated enough without adding hypnosis and mind–bending to the mix.  Although, the fact that some shareholders gave up their share certificates in exchange for loans and gave undertakings not to say anything critical about the company and its directors could possibly be considered duress as they may have been in financial need and were being denied a dividend.  I believe part of Sheila’s case is that she was denied a dividend; this has come out in the newspaper reports.

On the face of it, the case sounds very similar to many cases of friends, family or colleagues falling out when there is a big windfall, but Judge Bannister’s concern is not the relationships involved but whether company or contract law has been breached in the various transactions that have transpired.  It will be very interesting to see what he concludes.  In Paul Marriott’s case, the court ordered the company to purchase his shares.  This would be the more straightforward decision for this court, to order the company to purchase Sheila’s shares, so the judge would have to take what he is hearing about how the company is being run very seriously indeed to order a winding-up.  It is clear that he is giving all the evidence he is hearing very careful consideration, so we will see what he makes of it when he gives his judgement.
