Dialogue Ireland response to the CHURCH OF IRELAND GAZETTE article on Willi Stewart.

Abuse by Church and Irish institutions

Dialogue Ireland response to:

“Last week, the since retired Archbishop of Dublin, Dr John Neill, said that, whilst extending pastoral support to Mr Stewart and his family when he resigned from CORE Church, he “consistently refused to give him clearance to apply for any further ministry in Ireland or overseas.””


It is interesting to note that most of the reports emanating from Church sources seem to do everything except report on the actual events surrounding Willi Stewart’s activities. They have been in the public domain since June 2011. They outline in great detail all the events surrounding this story. There is a court report in the absence of the media not reporting events following the court case at the Bridewell court in January 2005 which was edited with the Archdeacon representing the Archbishop of the time.

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/2012/11/21/willi-stewart-controversy-following-former-c-of-i-clerics-resignation-from-english-parish/

The Revd Will Stewart, formerly of the Church of Ireland’s CORE Church in Dublin until 2004, resigned at the beginning of this month as rector of the Chet Valley Benefice in Norfolk.

The Bishop of Thetford, the Rt Revd Alan Winton, said that there had been a campaign focused on “a matter” from Mr Stewart’s past. This concerned a sexual offence against a man aged 20, which had given rise to his resigning from CORE Church.

We note the bishop refers to a campaign and this is already addressed in our reply to him.

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/2012/11/08/reply-to-the-bishop-of-thetfords-statement-by-the-director-of-dialogue-ireland-mike-garde/

In a Dublin court, Mr Stewart pleaded guilty to the offence but, the judge ruling in accordance with probation law provisions, no criminal record ensued.

However, in order to understand this case we need to read the Court Report provided by DI.

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/2011/07/12/the-trial-of-rev-william-james-stewart-at-the-bridewell-district-court-on-friday-14-jan-2005/

When information about Mr Stewart was posted on the Internet, the Diocese of Norwich consulted Norfolk police who advised that the offence had not been against a child or vulnerable adult and that, therefore, the matter was to be regarded “as a diocesan responsibility to manage in accordance with its normal procedures”.

Here again we see that the definition of vulnerability is now quite outdated. It refers only to persons suffering from some form of disability. Read the definition below. It is quite clear that if one holds to the definition of vulnerable adult outlined by the bishop then the victim in this case does not fit that description.

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/safe_haven_for_vulnerable_adults_-april_2012.pdf

There are various definitions of what a “Vulnerable Adult” is. Here are two of them, the first being from the NHS and the second from the Church of England.

NHS definition –

Broadly speaking, a vulnerable adult is aged 18 or over, receives or may need community care services because of a disability, age or illness, and who is or may be unable to take care of themselves or protect themselves against significant harm or exploitation.

The Church of England definition is similar –

Any adult aged 18 or over who, by reason of mental or other disability, age, illness or other situation is permanently or for the time being unable to take care of him or herself, or to protect him or herself against significant harm or exploitation. (Promoting a Safe Church).

However, what is not recognised here is the person could be vulnerable not because of a disability but due to the imbalance of power and the inappropriate use of authority. In other words this person could have been highly intelligent but still vulnerable. Why did the Bishop ask the police to define for him what was obvious from the reports he had received from us, namely Willi had used his position of power in an inappropriate manner. The gospel which he claimed to represent was his interpretive tool, not the police.

http://www.nhssb.n-i.nhs.uk/publications/social_services/Safeguarding_Vulnerable_Adults.pdf

3.2 The current definition of abuse is …

‘The physical, psychological, emotional, financial or sexual

maltreatment, or neglect of a vulnerable adult by another person. The

abuse may be a single act or repeated over a period of time. It may

take one form or a multiple of forms. The lack of appropriate action can

also be a form of abuse. Abuse can occur in a relationship where there

is an expectation of trust and can be perpetrated by a person/persons,

in breach of that trust, who have influence over the life of a dependant,

whether they be formal or informal carers, staff or family members or

others. It can also occur outside such a relationship’

3.3 Forms of abuse can be categorised as follows:

• physical abuse (including inappropriate restraint or use of

medication);

• sexual abuse;

• psychological abuse;

• financial or material abuse;

• neglect and acts of omission;

• institutional abuse; and

• discriminatory abuse.

3.4 Incidents of abuse may be multiple, either to one person in a continuing

relationship or service context, or to more than one person at a time.

3.5 Any or all types of abuse may be perpetrated as the result of deliberate

intent and targeting of vulnerable people, negligence or ignorance.

In a statement, Bishop Winton said that while a number of parishioners had been supportive of Mr Stewart and his family, the rector had increasingly felt  The Revd Willi Stewart that “under the present circumstances” he was no longer able to continue his ministry.

The Bishop added: “In appointing William Stewart, the Diocese followed the normal procedure of seeking assurance from the Church of Ireland that he was a priest in good standing, and a CRB [Criminal Records Bureau] check was made and returned clear.”

I refer you to our reply to the bishop referenced  in the link above. Why there was a need to clarify his criminal record when it was clear from the terms of the Probation Act and the generosity of the victim that he would never have a criminal record.

“The Act allows judges wide latitude to dismiss a charge tried summarily against a defendant even when the court thinks it is proved, or to conditionally discharge a defendant (whether the charge is tried summarily or on indictment).”

 

What is doubt is not that he has a criminal record but whether he has actually entered into a process of reconciliation with the victim/s. That should have been the main concern of the Churchmen not legalities.

Last week, the since retired Archbishop of Dublin, Dr John Neill, said that, whilst extending pastoral support to Mr Stewart and his family when he resigned from CORE Church, he “consistently refused to give him clearance to apply for any further ministry in Ireland or overseas.”

The above statement by Archbishop John Neill is the first clear statement in regard to Willi which illuminates the logic of Dialogue Ireland in pursuing this issue. DI welcomes it and it now sheds light on Dublin and darkness on Kilkenny. Unfortunately his successor Michael Jackson refused to engage with DI in 2011 when we sought clarification of the views above. This then suggests Michael Jackson either had not familiarised himself with the case  or he is in agreement with Bishop Michael Burrows? Or there is some other explanation.

Nonetheless, some time after leaving CORE, Mr Stewart was issued with a licence by the Bishop of Cashel and Ossory; the Rt Revd Michael Burrows.

Willi Stewart  left for America in 2004, and returned to Ireland in 2005 for the trial. Because of the victim’s graciousness he was able to return pretty well immediately to the USA not having a criminal record. However, his plan to stay there fell apart when Archbishop Neill revoked his licence following the court case as he had promised to do. We are reminded that the Bishop of Cashel and Ossory before Michael Burrows was Peter Barrett from 2002 to 2006 and I am reliably informed he maintained the position outlined by Archbishop John Neill.

I contacted  Michael Burrows in 2008 and he kindly replied to me but saw no reason to stop issuing Mr Stewart with a limited licence.

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/2011/07/25/visit-to-st-canice’s-cathedral-kilkenny-may-2008/

In a statement supplied to the Gazette, Bishop Burrows said: “The Revd William Stewart was permitted to exercise a limited and local ministry in the Diocese of Cashel and Ossory in a non-stipendiary capacity from 2008 to 2011, after having obtained extensive and appropriate professional advice following the conclusion of the court case in Dublin in 2005.

Could we ask the bishop did he as part of seeking ‘professional advice,’ seek to ascertain did Mr Stewart seek to reconcile himself with the victim in this case or any other one involving inappropriate behaviour?

“When his move to England took place last year, there was full disclosure to the Norwich diocesan authorities of the nature of his ministry here and in particular in respect of the full facts relating to the court case.

How did the bishop acquaint the Norwich diocesan authorities in respect of the full facts relating to the court case when there is no record of the case because he had pleaded guilty?

 “Nothing was, as far as I am concerned, concealed or unacknowledged. This naturally included the outcome of his vetting prior to departure by the Irish police.”

This could of necessity only involve any earlier case or subsequent case as the case involving the court case in 2005 had left no criminal record by definition.

Asked to confirm, whether or not he had sought advice from the previous Bishop under whom Mr Stewart had served, namely the then Archbishop of Dublin,

This after the statement of former Archbishop Neill is most revealing in that it fails to answer a straight question and diverts into an answer not asked: We have to assume he flagrantly disregarded the advice of his predecessor and that of the Archbishop? We would be happy to publish a refutation of this point if this is not the case.

Bishop Burrows told us: “I sought extensive and appropriate professional advice concerning Mr Stewart, and with his [Mr Stewart’s] co-operation. I naturally sought other informed pastoral advice as well, and full details concerning this have to remain confidential.

How convenient? I asked Willi Stewart on a number of occasions have you made peace with the victim. I asked him to not officiate at communion until he was reconciled with him. This would be the kind of questions one expects from a bishop not hiding behind ‘professional advice?’

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/2011/07/18/church-discipline-in-the-church-of-ireland/

“Having taken extensive pastoral and professional advice into account, I had to make a decision concerning the appropriateness of permitting a limited, local and mentored ministry in the context of the lapse of time since the completion of the court case.”

In other words he totally ignored Archbishop Neill and sadly refused to engage with Dialogue Ireland, and as a pastor engaging with a priest who had been permanently debarred from ministry he did him no favours by not addressing the victim.

The Gazette was informed by a Norwich Diocese spokesperson that Mr Stewart was not available for comment.
