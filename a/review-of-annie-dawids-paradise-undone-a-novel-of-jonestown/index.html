Paradise Undone: A Novel of Jonestown—A Discussion.

Uncategorized

One day perhaps, when the records of the twentieth century AD have grown as fragmentary as those of ancient Rome, a history of the Second World War will be written which relies solely upon the broadcasts of Hitler and the memoirs of Churchill. It will be one cut off from whole dimensions of experience: no letters from the front, no combatants’ diaries. The silence will be one with which the ancient historian is all too familiar, for, to twist the words of Shakespeare’s Fluellen, ‘there is no tiddle taddle nor pibble pabble in Pompey’s camp’. Nor in the peasant’s hut, nor in the slum dweller’s shanty, nor in the field slave’s barrack. Women, it is true, can sometimes be overheard, but only the very noblest, and even those invariably when quoted – or misquoted – by men. In Roman history to search for details of anyone outside the ruling class is to pan for gold.

—Tom Holland (2003). Rubicon: The Triumph and Tragedy of the Roman Republic.

According to Annie Dawid, her sixth book, Paradise Undone: A Novel of Jonestown, published on the 45th anniversary of the 1978 Jonestown Massacre is her most important book to date. 

Blending hard facts and meticulous research with fiction, Dawid animates the lost and overlooked stories of the real people of the Peoples Temple and guides our attention away from the man behind the massacre; a man who, in Dawid’s own words, ‘Already gets too much air time.’ 

Dialogue Ireland director Mike Garde (MG) and DI colleague Peter Johnson (PJ) discuss Dawid’s book, how the death of 918 (917 if you exclude the Rev. Jim Jones) American citizens in the humid jungle of the recently sovereign Guyana brought the issue of cultism to the attention of the world, the importance of shifting the spotlight off the perpetrator, and why it’s a murder-suicide and NOT a mass suicide.

PJ: Before we begin, I’d like to say a big thank you to Annie and her publisher for kindly sending Dialogue Ireland copies of Paradise Undone: A Novel of Jonestown earlier this year.

Back in April, we published a short post about the book’s publication in the UK last year on the 45th anniversary of the Jonestown Massacre (18/11/2023). 

Photo: Annie Dawid

Right, so where to begin? I thought I’d start with a little bit of background about the book before we get stuck into a discussion. 

Annie started the book in 2004, and the book was ready for the 30th anniversary of Jonestown (if my maths is right, that would have been 2008). However, and quite surprisingly, it sounded like it took her another fourteen years to find somebody to publish it. (She may have even said she received hundreds of rejections from publishers over the years.)

Annie has no personal connection to Jonestown or the Peoples Temple, but during her research, she met survivors and family members of the people involved.

In the Indoctrination podcast, Annie mentions that during high school, she was friends with two sisters who joined a cult (not Jonestown) for a significant amount of time. According to her, these sisters were ‘independent, smart, and creative,’ and ‘not followers.‘

What seems to be of interest to Annie is the media fallout depicting the members of the Peoples Temple who died on that November day as ‘mindless zombies,’ and the same media exalting Jim Jones to almost anti-hero celebrity status with the amount of attention they gave to him.

It seems, therefore, that Jonestown, the reason why people join cults, and the media handling of the perpetrators and the victims was something she was trying to understand, and redress, in her research.

In Paradise Undone, Annie switches our attention from the ‘cult leader’ to tell the stories of the real people: The people who dedicated their lives to a man who forced them to leave their homeland and loved ones, follow him to Guyana, and die at his command.

I’ll drop a couple of quotes in from the book as we go along to frame the discussion or draw attention to certain themes, and I’ll refer to podcasts to get a bit more of Annie’s perspective. 

However, as a starting point for us, it might be a good idea to begin with a bit about the incident itself. The 1978 Jonestown Massacre, as it is known, was a bit before my time; however, it’s one of those pivotal moments when it comes to the study of cultism.

Mike, was the ‘massacre’ your first encounter or knowledge of Jones, the Peoples Temple, or Jonestown, or were you aware of what was happening pre-1978?

MG: No. Like most people, it was when the controlled environment was breached that the lid came off. Jones had been seen as a kind of white Martin Luther King in San Francisco, but as his behaviours got out of control the lights went out and he had to move to Guyana to be away from witnesses and to have the environment under control and himself not subject to scrutiny.

My guess is the first time I really focused on it was when I went to the Danish Dialog Centre in Aarhus, Denmark. There, in the pre-internet days, we brought suitcases from all over Europe with material on every type of cult and the photocopiers were on all the time. We had an extensive conversation about Jonestown and at that time I would have focused on beliefs and not processes. 

Then it was all about the evil Jones, the suicide, and that false narrative about the Koolaid which managed to distort what had happened. We got that Leo Ryan was extraordinary in flying down to check out how his constituents were getting on and we did not think he was coming to die.

Also, we were used to instant communication so it was amazing how quickly news spread and how many videos and audio I collected on the subject. It is hard now to play these media but this one has been part of the Dialogue Ireland archive from the beginning.

https://dialogueireland.ie/dicontent/resources/video/paradise.html

Aerial photo of Jonestown after the massacre (Photo Credit: WikiCommons)

PJ: The American public before 1978 was no stranger to the concept of ‘brainwashing’ in a religious or political context: The Manson Family murders and Patty Hearst’s involvement with the Symbionese Liberation Army made both national news, but in your opinion, what was it about Jonestown that made the public start to take notice of the issue of cultism?

MG: Well, I think that what happened is that the concept of a cult in 1978 was very much a concept that was something beyond every one of us. So when we talked about cults, they were very specific things, but they were very kind of outside our reference point.

However, around the time of Jonestown, more and more people were having the direct experience of friends, family members, or colleagues who were quite rational and normal and suddenly showed signs of no longer being in touch with reality.

There was the case when students I knew joined the Moonies (The Unification Church)…but it was like they had been taken by aliens, except it was these strange Koreans. He’s “Gone with the Moonies,” as they would say.

I was just talking last year to a friend about a woman who seemed to literally change overnight. In those days it was someone who read a pamphlet and they became what we now call “radicalised,” but this would normally take up to a few months.

Now with social media and the internet, this can take place immediately and we now call it self-radicalisation.

But this can also happen at a societal, or cultural level. Here in Ireland, everyone is unaware of how the “Cult of 1916” is foundational to our view of things, and this manifested itself at the Irish music festival Electric Picnic this year, where the famous rebel band The Wolftones sang Up the Ra (IRA), and in 2022 when the Limerick Hurling team singing Sean South of Garryowen (a call to invade Northern Ireland and force it into the Irish Republic).

PJ: One of Annie’s goals in the book was to give the victims a voice and dispel the myth that the members of the People’s Temple were mindless zombies and not worth further discussion. A quote on page six reads:

What I was saying was that the news made out all of Jonestown was psychos and sickos and brainwashed, brainless zombies. So far from the truth, Kenyatta.

Mike, why is there still this blame-the-victim mentality? That it’s somehow the person’s fault if they get involved with a cult. 

MG: Victim blaming is something that is now so embedded that we take it for granted. It’s not unique to cult contexts—it exists in all realms where there’s a victim-perpetrator dynamic.

But recently, we do seem to be improving our concept of victims, and we are beginning to recognize the fact that the victims of child sexual abuse need to be recognised, the #MeToo movement recognises what happened to women. They are now being seen and heard.

The rape allegations in the UK about Harrods boss Al Fayed (who died last year at 94) make this clear.

There’s an awareness of victimhood and at the same time, there’s also a movement from victimhood to survivorship. From “poor me” to we are not going to take this anymore. Will the US election next week be the last gasp of patriarchy or will women have control of the narrative for the first time?

And so that sense of being a victim, if you don’t get out of that state, that’s a very serious situation where we get stuck in the past or frozen in the present and can’t move from being a victim to having a future as a survivor.

Take the events of October 7th last year (The Hamas attack on Israel), as an example.

Many of those young people who were kidnapped and later released can’t survive in the current Israeli state because there’s no space for them, the government will not take responsibility for what happened during the events of October 7th so it has no strategy, so it is like the Beckett play Waiting for Godot.

People don’t want to take responsibility or face what has happened, so it can be easier to ignore or blame the victim, which adds to the trauma.

PJ: It can be difficult to discuss Jonestown without it becoming a character analysis of Jim Jones, and I think many of us fall into the trap of viewing the whole thing as if it is his story; as if he’s the main character in all of this.

(There’s a good chance we’ll be guilty of that today.)

MG: That was my original view when my lens caught Jones in direct view. That made him look strong and the man in control. What became clear was he was a weak man who could not control things, and he was beginning to lose it as the drugs and his loss of boundaries took hold. He was not a man of faith in Christ but rather overwhelmed by the ideology of Soviet communism.

In the end, it got so bad that he had to move (to Guyana) because the authorities were beginning to surround him and he was losing control and wanted to control history by not allowing the truth of what was happening to come out.

Then a new insight brought greater awareness as we saw his pathetic inability to die as he set off a murder-suicide. He could order others to kill themselves, but he did not have the bottle [courage] to shoot himself and he had to be ‘assisted’ with his own demise.**

**Jim Jones and the nurse Annie Moore died by gunshot that day. It is believed that Jones ordered Annie to shoot him first.

PJ: I think Annie does a great job of shifting the spotlight off Jones in Paradise Undone. While he features in the book, he appears more in interactions with, or the recollections of the other four main characters: 1) An African-American, former drug addict ‘saved’ by the Peoples Temple and survivor of Jonestown; 2) the Guyanese ambassador to the US, 3) Jones’s wife Marceline, and 4) a young lady called Truth (who goes by the name given to her by Jones).

All of these characters seem to represent a cross-section of society that Jones was able to infiltrate, groom, and influence. 

Annie uses real quotes from Jones to begin each chapter, which I felt became increasingly sinister as the book progressed and intentionally showed his deteriorating mental state. One chapter begins with the following: 

YOU CAN CALL ME AN EGOMANIAC, A MEGALOMANIAC WITH A MESSIANIC COMPLEX. I DON’T HAVE ANY COMPLEX, HONEY, I HAPPEN TO KNOW I’M THE MESSIAH. IT’S ONLY A COMPLEX WHEN YOU’RE CONFUSED. IT’S ONLY A COMPLEX WHEN YOU HAVE NEUROTIC COMPULSIONS. HONEY, I DON’T HAVE ANY CONFLICT ABOUT IT AT ALL. I KNOW I AM GOD THE MESSIAH. THERE WASN’T ANY GOD TILL I CAME ALONG.

However, Annie mentions in a podcast that the function of these Jones quotes is to show how Jones’ followers still feel his influence and presence many years after Jonestown. 

If I remember correctly, the character Truth still ‘communicates’ (or at least receives instructions) from the spirit of the deceased Jones long after his death. 

A lot of the book focuses on how people deal with the aftermath of Jonestown, which makes the very poignant observation that cult involvement does not end when you escape or leave—the residual effects and damage linger on for many years afterwards.

MG: Well, you have to think that at that time, the concept of leaving a cult involved such things as deprogramming and kidnapping.

Somebody who has been physically deprogrammed, is similar to a rape victim: They’ve been spiritually raped by the “group/cult” and then they go through a similar process to get out of the group, which can lead to a switching off of the mind, and living a kind of automatic life.

Someone may then find a resolution by some therapeutic means if they recognise the trauma they’ve been in. But some people will repress these memories, and there’s also the chance of them having false memories.

It’s an extremely vulnerable period of time, and there’s the risk of them leaving one bad group or situation and walking straight into another as they have not addressed the original departure.

PJ: One of the main characters in the book is Jim’s wife, Marceline Jones, who married Jones in 1949 and was there from the beginning of the Peoples Temple (in 1954) when it was just a small Pentecostal church in Indiana, to the very unfortunate end in Jonestown.

To my detriment, I didn’t know much about Marceline Jones before reading Paradise Undone, and don’t think a great deal of attention has been paid to her in the years since. I’d gone along with the line that she was with Jones all the way; therefore, in my mind, she was complicit in what happened at Jonestown.

Paradise Undone gives Marceline an existence, and Annie describes her as a ‘superwoman’ who juggles her duties between full-time nursing and the Peoples Temple. In the book, she’s a caring, selfless nurse who lives in the service of others, mainly the children and the elderly of the Peoples Temple.

Occasionally, it was true. Some days, Marceline looked around and felt her prayers had been answered, that Jesus was still walking on the earth, for she could see His footprints in Jonestown when she watched the seniors working in their vegetable gardens, or sitting on their porches, fanning themselves as their own elders had back in the South, with their extended families all around them. To Marceline, the care of the elders, their feelings of complete safety and security, was their most important accomplishment at Jonestown. Nothing could take that away. Even Jim’s paranoia and drug abuse couldn’t ruin the simple, daily pleasures in the lives of the eldest citizens of Jonestown, who had their vitals checked every morning by a nurse.

In Paradise Undone, Marceline unfortunately can never find the courage to derail or speak out against her husband’s final plan, but we see a tormented internal monologue as things begin to unravel.

I’ve watched him nearly thirty years and said nothing. Now’s the time to act, Marceline. When he started fooling around, I did nothing. Any other woman would have left him when he started up with Jocelyn. If I’d done that, there would be no Jonestown. No murdered Congressman. No dead children. No frenzied suicides.

In our discussions with Ian (who used to work with Dialogue Ireland), he wondered if Jonestown could have worked if the insidious influence of Jones had been removed, and somebody like the character of Marceline was in charge.

I haven’t seen the 2018 documentary Jonestown: The Women Behind the Massacre, which discusses the role of other female members of Jones’ inner circle, but Paradise Undone definitely has me thinking more about the involvement of the other women in Jonestown.

While on the subject of the women of Jonestown, I want to mention that the book is dedicated to the memory of Christine Miller, the sixty-year-old African-American lady who stood up to Jim Jones on the day of the massacre. She’s the only person known to have spoken out that day, and there are recordings of her interactions with Jim Jones on the 44-minute ‘Death Tape.’

The dedication to Christine in Paradise Undone reads:

For Christine, who refused to submit.


MG: Like you, Peter, my initial read of Marceline was that she was “Just a cypher, she wasn’t a real person.” She didn’t even register on my dial as it were, because she was just a part of the march of death, like those who entered into the gas ovens of Auschwitz.

It appears that Marceline was, in a sense, just an “attachment” to Jim Jones, rather than having her own thoughts, and I think Paradise Undone addresses this issue.

PJ: As you say, Mike, there was this idea that Jim Jones was an almost ‘White Martin Luther King’: a philanthropist and social activist way ahead of his time when it came to inclusivity and racial tolerance.

The black bottom of the Peoples Temple. The base. The place he got all that Social Security money and collection money from to run the operation–poor old black ladies giving him their last dollars and the ratty houses they saved for all their lives because so-called Dad healed some auntie of cancer or cataracts. Of course those healings all fake.


One criticism of Paradise Undone I’ve read elsewhere is how some African-American characters (maybe the character of Watts) seemed a little ‘caricature’.

Listening to the IndoctriNation podcast, I discovered Watts was a composite character based on multiple real people. Personally, I thought Watts was just a pseudonym for one of the larger-than-life interviewees in the Jonestown: The Life & Death of Peoples Temple documentary. 

From your perspective, Mike, was Jim Jones just targeting African Americans because he saw them as a vulnerable group? Was his altruism completely fake and self-serving?

Jim Jones receives the Martin Luther King, Jr. Humanitarian Award – January 1977. (Photo Credit: Wikicommons)


MG: Jim Jones was viewed—because of all his work with people of colour who are vulnerable and drug addicts, as well as having an inclusive congregation—as a “great guy.” That he’s somehow a “white” Martin Luther King-type figure in San Francisco.

I don’t know if you’ve read the reports, but there are accusations that Martin Luther King, like Jones, engaged in, or was involved in problematic sexual incidents.

I can imagine Jones did not start out with bad intentions of exploiting a vulnerable group. I could imagine him starting out as an evangelical who was unusual in coming from Indiana (a very conservative state), and he had genuinely altruistic motivations to begin with.

In the transition from a normal pastor to a cult leader, I could imagine that it was the point where his power and his visible effect on people went to his head, so it was an ego transition from helping people toward one where he became more important than the people he was working with.

It wasn’t darkness from day one with him, and the young man originally from Indiana, was not the same man who orchestrated Jonestown. However, as his power began to grow and his drug addiction started to maintain his momentum, exploitative behaviour started to emerge.

The addiction, the power, the finances, all that lead to the evolution of a guy who was seen as the Martin Luther King of San Francisco to becoming the killer, and the guy who did what he did in the jungle.

He wanted a place where he could not be observed, the great man compared to Martin Luther King suddenly was seen to have feet of clay and had to get out of the United States in order to keep himself from being investigated because things were beginning to turn on him.

PJ: Sex in cult contexts is often an incentive to be controlled, or a tool that’s used to control others. Jones’ sexual encounters and exploits are well documented; however, in Paradise Undone we see firsthand the effect this has on the characters as they begin second-guessing the fidelity of their partners, which begins to break down the trust of the community at a familial level (and causes trust issues long after Jonestown).

The quote here is from the Guyanese Ambassador to the US.

What to call Jones? The Reverend–though revered for what, I couldn’t say. In Guyana, he dubbed himself the Bishop. Psycho. Devil. Charlatan. Megalomaniac. Monster. Madman. Sociopath. Monomaniac. Ahab. Messiah. Prophet. Revolutionary. Christ re-born. Lenin reincarnated. Guyana’s best American friend. Human Rights Leader, as Indianapolis did in 1960. Man of the Year, as the Black Press of America did in 1969. Self-suggested intermediary between the Symbionese Liberation Army and the family of kidnapped heiress Patty Hearst in 1974. Humanitarian of the Year, recipient of the Martin Luther King Jr. Award in 1977. A combination of Martin Luther King, Jr., Angela Davis, Albert Einstein, and Chairman Mao, as California State Assemblyman Willie Brown called him. The homosexual arrested in a Los Angeles cinema toilet for propositioning an undercover police officer. High school teacher. Head of the San Francisco Housing Authority. Friend to First Lady Rosalyn Carter and the Governor of California. Key vote-harvester for San Francisco Mayor George Moscone and Supervisor Harvey Milk. Healer of the sick. Resuscitator of the dead. Father. Dad. Monkey salesman. The man who fucked most female members of the Peoples Temple inner circle. The man who fucked my wife. My wife is calling me, asking when I will come to bed. Soon, I tell her. Soon.

The inclusion of a Guyanese main character is important because the effect of Jonestown on what Dawid—if I remember correctly—described in a podcast as a ‘fledgling country’ is often overlooked or not considered at all.

(It’s worth pointing out that moving almost 1000 people—many of them without passports—to South America to build a town in the jungle is an incredible logistical feat.)

MG: Guyana had just found independence after many years of British rule. Jonestown was not a colonial project to take over the state. Rather it was the opposite a place to escape from the regulation of the USA and the weak scrutiny of the Guyanese state.

Jim Jones is not just a guy with an ideology; he was a preacher with fantastic charisma. And this charisma would have been able to bring people to Guyana if he had not been successful at doing it in San Francisco.

But what he was not successful at was covering up the fact he had a dual model: he was sexually abusing women, taking money, and accruing power to himself, and he had to do it in Guyana.

He’s now so addicted to opiates and everything that I don’t think that he was doing anything other than just trying to protect his nest.

PJ: Mike, you’ve mentioned several times that the idea that everybody that day ‘voluntarily committed suicide’ is the wrong interpretation of what actually happened. Annie also picks up on this in the book. Here’s an example on page 74:

Rather than let the children be brought up by the oppressor, the parents killed them, then committed suicide. A brave act, not one committed by cowards. In Israel, Masada was a shrine, the Hebrew ancestors commemorated as noble patriots. Why were Peoples Temple members regarded as crazy? Duped zombies.

Truth stopped. Babies couldn’t drink by themselves. They had to be injected. Or have the liquid forced down their tiny throats. She shuddered. Always, thinking about the infants gave her pause. How had Lionel put it? “They killed their children.”

Lionel went on: “The Jones people, though, they were the craziest of all. They killed their children! That lady in Georgetown killed her three children with a knife! She slit their throats. Then she slit her own. Don’t you think that’s crazy?” 


MG: What I think happens is the power of the charismatic leader is such that it gets you to say one thing on the one day and the opposite thing on the next day.

So, it’s not just about the technicality of suicide, it’s murder-suicide based on the charismatic influence, so to use the normal criteria to judge his wife or those people is to misunderstand the nature of charisma, the power of charisma is very important to understanding this.

By getting the parents to kill their children first, the women were then kind of ‘disconnected’, and then men saw no purpose in living because their wives and children were gone, so then they might as well join the ritual death.

They had lost the power by that stage to resist. It was too late to resist.

So it’s a murder-suicide that starts, first of all, with the act of giving cyanide to the children.

PJ: Annie’s writing style demonstrates great respect for the subject matter and the characters, and for me, it’s her attention to the small details that re-humanises the people of the People’s Temple:

“Can’t sleep, Marcy. Like you, I guess. You okay?” Edith was one of Jim’s first rescue projects, a white widow with a good heart and no money, lots of pride and her own children dead. They took her in Indianapolis in 1957. She insisted on housekeeping, though her arthritis made most tasks painful, and the results were often inadequate. The peanut butter sandwiches in the kids’ lunchboxes were half-smeared against their tin insides because she could never quite get the bread completely inside the waxed paper bag.” (pg 163)

‘Members of Peoples Temple including Richard Parr, Wesley Johnson, Barbara Hickson, Ricky Johnson, and Sandra Cobb attend an anti-eviction rally at the International Hotel, 848 Kearny Street in San Francisco, January 1977. Photo by Nancy Wong'(Credit: Wikicommons)

If we had to say something negative about Paradise Undone, the only one thing that springs to mind —and was something our colleague Ian noticed—was the quick perspective switch in Marceline’s narration (from third to first person) towards the end of the book. This was momentarily disorientating but could have been intentional as it was the moment things were going very wrong in Jonestown. However, the perspective shifts for different characters worked to good effect throughout the rest of the book.

Annie’s very aware of the political and social context of the era (issues surrounding race, class, female empowerment, and women’s rights), but also connects Jonestown to modern-day American society and how death and violence at scale seem senseless and hard to comprehend. The following quote references the 1999 high school shooting that left 15 dead (if you include the perpetrators) and 24 injured. 

“How can you put it into context?” She looked up at him. “I have no idea. My son’s only ten, but they had the TV on in his class yesterday, if you can believe that. I wanted to help him think about it, but I froze. It’s just horror, as you said. Pure violence without any kind of rationale. How could there be anything political to it?”

“The fact that middle-class high school boys in the American heartland can equip themselves with enough material to make bombs and hold off a SWAT team for an afternoon … don’t you think that’s political?” She shook her head. “I don’t know. All I know is that I’m really, really scared.” She looked into her teacher’s eyes. “Scared for my little boy.” 

Just as everyone understands that the shortened Jonestown means the 918 dead American citizens in the Guyanese Jungle, the word ‘Columbine’ is now a byword for school shootings. However, if you want to use their official, unabbreviated titles, you’ll find both events share the same surname—massacre. 

How society remembers such events can be an issue, and the final chapter focuses on the real problem of Jones’ name being included on the memorial monument:

Beyond the word “KILLER” is one more phrase: EXPUNGE HIM. On her clipboard, retrieved from the giant canvas satchel she carries everywhere, Kenyatta notes the particular word choice: not “expel” or “remove.” There are many who believe Jim Jones should not be memorialized along with the other 917 dead on November 18, 1978, in Jonestown, Guyana. But there he is: James Warren Jones, sandwiched between James Arthur Jones, one of his grandchildren and Jesse Weana Jones, an elderly black woman from Louisiana.

“917, not 918!” a woman shouts.

Mike, should his name be anywhere near that monument? And would you also include Marceline’s name?

MG: He should be definitely excluded and there should be a sign saying very clearly he was removed because of the fact that it was totally inappropriate for him to be connected to this. It’s like the equivalent of a murderer being added as if he’s a casualty.

I wouldn’t have any problem with Marceline’s inclusion, I believe she may have been a victim of her husband, and from what we’ve seen from Annie’s book, she’s been in some ways exonerated and her complicity questioned.

So I don’t see any reason why she, as a woman, who is herself caught up in the dynamic, shouldn’t be part of the group being remembered, because she is also a victim.

‘One of three new headstones placed at Jonestown memorial service in 2011, at Evergreen Cemetery in Oakland, California. Names of all who died are written on the stones.’ (Photo credit: WikiCommons)

PJ: I’d like to return to a point Annie made in the IndoctriNation podcast, and that’s how ‘Cult Madness’ is still happening today.

In the decades after Jonestown, other cultism-related incidents had similar international media coverage: Heaven’s Gate and Waco were, unfortunately, significant media incidents and received a lot of international attention. Annie reminds us on page nine that Jonestown is still the deadliest of all these incidents:

What do you think accounts for Reverend Jones’s success–perhaps that’s a strange word to use–maybe I should rather say his ability to convince so many people to stay with him, and in the end, to take their own lives? In all the similar events that have happened since–in Waco, for instance, or Heaven’s Gate here in California–we’re talking about a few dozen people. In Jonestown, we’re talking nearly a thousand.

Annie mentions the 448 dead and 613 missing in the Shakahola forest incident, which is often referred to as the Kenyan starvation cult. In the almost fifty years since Jonestown, has anything really changed?

MG: The list goes on, unfortunately, and it’s frustrating and tragic to see events like this still happening internationally, so it might seem like we haven’t progressed in terms of where we’re at.

Before Kenya, there was Uganda and The Movement for the Restoration of the Ten Commandments.

The interesting thing is that there were Irish Catholic missionaries called the Legion of Mary, a very interesting movement here that cleaned up the brothels of Dublin after the 1916 Rising, and they had their own missionaries that went to places like Uganda. They weren’t directly connected to The Restoration but would have been the group from which it emerged.

So again this demonstrates a similar situation: conning people, misinforming them, and then the undue influence being exercised by a charismatic leader to such an extent that people allow themselves to be burnt alive.

What seems to be changing is our understanding of cultism, and it’s helpful to understand it as a spectrum rather than a fixed point.

What I mean is there was this seeing it as a kind of separate entity where people said,” He’s in a cult.” I think that the notion of cultism is a much better construct to be using because I think the danger of using the word “cult” is that it defines and solidifies in a way that puts it in concrete rather than leaves it open to new dimensions coming out.

You know, to talk about this being “Just the cult of Jim Jones” freezes it at that moment.

What Annie’s doing by using fiction is to broaden the categories of cults, where the tendencies and attitudes that make up cultism are very much at play and, therefore, more dimensions come out.

So it’s like the onion, you are peeling off different layers and then reinterpret another layer that you may have given too much preference to. For example, the notion of Jim Jones as the central figure, whereas now the tendency is to remove him completely.

The field of cult studies still doesn’t get the support or understanding. For instance, I was recently talking to a social studies teacher from England, and every time I was brought up a subject she was intrigued and would say “That’s something I need to bring up with the kids.” But, when I mentioned the fact that in Ireland, we don’t have any radicalization courses, she was amazed.

So it can still be a hard thing to get people to understand. Have you noticed that I’m banging my head with journalists, I can’t get through to the government people, and they don’t reply? It’s so just unbelievably frustrating in terms of things not going anywhere.

PJ: To finish up, I just want to say that I really enjoyed the book, and there’s definitely something in it for anyone interested in modern American history, cultism, or who enjoys a pop history approach to significant events. 

I haven’t had the chance to ask Annie about why Paradise Undone was rejected by multiple punishers, but if I was going to have a guess, maybe they thought there was nothing left to say on the topic, or by taking the focus of Jim Jones would be removing something integral to the Jonestown ‘story’.

But, as we’ve seen in this discussion, Paradise Undone has us thinking—sans Jim Jones (I hope)— about Jonestown and, more importantly, the real people who made up the Peoples Temple.
