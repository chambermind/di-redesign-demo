Church should be offering confessions, not excuses by JUSTINE McCARTHY

Abuse by Church and Irish institutions

The Sunday Times   15 May 2011

He was phoning from somewhere in the middle of Ireland, he said. He was alone, and lonely enough to confide in a stranger whose name he had seen in a newspaper. His voice was dead with sadness.  He had been raped by two priests when he was a child, he said. It happened again and again, at the presbytery near his home and on organised trips away. Now that he was an adult, he felt like an outcast. He used to have a girlfriend but she was long gone. The only people who would have anything to do with him were lawyers. They dealt with him on behalf of the Catholic church. What they said, in so many layman’s words, was: “Get lost.”

He did. I found out later that he left the country, carrying with him an insulting amount of “get lost” money. God knows what has become of him.

He was just one of many. A name in a newspaper provides a flimsy lifeline after Holy Mother Church has chewed them up, spat them out and squashed them underfoot with its lawyers’ expensive size 10s.

Another lived in a dank flat in Cardinal Seán Brady’s archdiocese of Armagh. He sat on a threadbare couch amid a pool of empty lager cans and a cloud of cigarette smoke. The first time a priest raped him, he was still in short trousers. After the second priest, he went on the game. A third priest was one of his clients.

The border-straddling archdiocese settled out of court in relation to the first priest who had, by then, shot himself dead at his kitchen table. His victim got a paltry sum. The church sealed the deal north of the border, where blind-faith Catholicism still thrives, compared with the punch-drunk south.

Then there is the man from the mid-west who painstakingly emails copies of his correspondence with church authorities. He is trying to complete a mission that killed his younger brother. Two days after a meeting in a hotel with church lawyers, the brother ended his life in despair. All he had wanted was acknowledgement that, when he was a seven-year-old altar boy, the parish priest regularly raped him before donning his golden vestments to say mass.

Last week, the National Board for Safeguarding Children (NBSC) in the Catholic Church in Ireland, revealed that the bishops and superiors of religious orders had been up to their old tricks again: excuses, excuses and damned lies.

In February, church leaders approved a national training strategy for child protection — then promptly cancelled the funding.

They withheld information about four-fifths of the allegations of clerical abuse they received last year, notifying the NBSC of just 53 new allegations. Only on a final pro forma check before signing off on the NBSC’s annual report did they own up that, actually, there were 272 new allegations.

Most disturbing of all, they fashioned a brand new fig leaf to continue covering up their failures and arrant incompetence. Despite commissioning it themselves in December 2009, 23 bishops refused to co-operate with the NBSC’s review of child protection practices because, they said, they were worried they could be fined under the Data Protection Act.  And these are the men who deign to preach about morality.

Church leaders’ intransigence begs the question: what other skeletons are they hiding in the cupboard? After two decades of media revelations and four landmark state inquiries (Ferns, Ryan, Murphy/Dublin and Murphy/Cloyne), they could be expected to come out with their hands up. But they don’t, because they feel the tide turning. They detect signs of abuse-fatigue in the media and a wider fatalistic sense that society has done as much as it can.

The old arrogance is creeping back. Since Pope Benedict’s letter to the Irish faithful last year — perversely blaming the Celtic tiger for clerical child abuse — the hierarchy has regained its strut. There has even been excited speculation about a papal visit next year — meant as a treat, not a threat.

It is as though church leaders were taking a calculated gamble that the most seismic ructions over their behaviour are in the past.

The pontifical visitation ordered by Benedict was a choreographed line in the sand.

Faoiseamh, the counselling service set up as part of the church’s compensation deal with the state, has been rebranded as new and improved. In reality, it will increasingly refer abuse victims to state-funded services, cutting costs for dioceses and congregations. Last week, the St John of God-run Granada Institute, the only organisation dealing exclusively with abusers, put up the “closed” sign, citing lack of money.

The untruths told to the NBSC, the church’s own watchdog, follow the Vatican’s refusal to cooperate with the Murphy commission’s investigation of the Dublin archdiocese. It is no coincidence that the prefect of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith in Rome, the curial office responsible for handling abuse complaints, is Cardinal William Levada. When he was the bishop of San Francisco, he established a review of child protection procedures in his diocese but the layman he appointed to chair it, James Jenkins, resigned in frustration. Jenkins accused Levada of stymying the investigation’s work. Sound familiar? When the NBSC in Maynooth exposed the hierarchy’s obduracy, abuse survivors in the rural Cork diocese of Cloyne must have listened aghast. The Murphy commission’s report on how their complaints were handled has been in the Department of Justice since last December. It was supposed to be published in the second week of May. That deadline came and went without a single church voice advocating that the survivors be put out of their agony and the report be published. The delay, needless to say, is caused by lawyers wrangling over what parts may not be published.

The Cloyne report was commissioned by the state following an investigation by Ian Elliott, the chief executive of the NBSC. The bishops who appointed him must wish that Elliott, like the survivors, would disappear.

He, however, refuses to quit. He deserves a halo. But there comes a time when the bishops have to be brought to heel and abuse survivors allowed to live their lives. One such person is a former nun who gets in touch from time to time. She was raped by a visiting priest in her convent. She has settled her case with the church for a nominal amount. The lawyers said that was all she was getting because she was an adult when she was raped. She was 19, and innocent as a child.
