26 September 2010

Students quit the ‘cult-like’ Tabor Centre, Sunday Times by Colin Coyle

Tabor Institute

More than half the students at a psychotherapy institute have walked out, writes Colin Coyle.

STUDENTS at a psychotherapy institute have staged a “walk-out” following accusations of unethical and “cult-like” behaviour. More than half of the 60 students at the Tabor Institute, which has centres in Dublin and Mullingar, did not return to complete their studies following the summer break. Some claim they were intimidated by Jeanette Rigney, the institute’s founder and director.

Tabor Sunday Times (c)

Some students, who pay €4,500 a year in fees, are unhappy that they are expected to pay additional charges for “skills training”, with a number paying up to €30,000 over the four years of the diploma course. A number of former Tabor staff have also expressed concerns on an internet forum about the centre’s operations.

Rigney, who runs a series of courses in “transpersonal psychotherapy”, would not comment on the accusations this weekend. A number of former students have defended her on the internet forum.

Four therapists at the institute have been dismissed or have resigned over the last four years after clashing with her.

Last week The Sunday Times discussed the operations of Tabor at length with Rigney, but she insisted that none of her comments be published. She is believed to be seeking legal advice in relation to the publication of claims on the forum, which is hosted by Dialogue Ireland, an organisation which monitors “new religious movements and cultism” in Ireland.

Mike Garde, the publisher of the website, said he set up the forum after being approached by former staff and students who were “afraid” to go public.

A former therapist at the institute said students told her they felt like “captives”, unable to leave Tabor because they had borrowed large sums of money to complete the course.

Critics of Tabor says its core course, a diploma in Humanistic, Integrative and Transpersonal Psychotherapy, is not accredited with any of the representative bodies in Ireland and blurs the lines between psychotherapy , religion and spiritualism. Students explore shamanism and other new-age therapies as well as traditional psychotherapy. They participate in rituals such as ceremonial feet-washing, walking on coals and hugging. In images of a “cairn ceremony” on Tabor;s website, students are wearing tribal dress.

Tabor, which was established seven years ago, admits on its website that its course is yet to be accredited but says it is consistent with the guidelines laid down by the representative bodies for psychotherapists in Ireland and meets European standards. Rigney is accredited as a therapist with the Irish Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy (IACP).

One former student said she had spent more than €30,000 during her four years at Tabor, when she was “obliged” to pay extra for skills training and residential weekend course.

Some students report they were encouraged to repair and maintain the institute’s facilities, even painting the centre’s building in Mullingar on one occasion. Others were asked to help fix a leak when pipes burst, while another built a ceremonial cairn at the Mullingar base, located in Ballinafid.

It is alleged that students, who must personally undergo 40 hours of personal therapy each year while training, are encouraged to use therapists at Tabor for their own counselling. Many are also believed to have been unhappy at being asked to pay €200 for tickets to their graduation, up from €50 the previous year. There have been accusations that the failure rate at the Tabor institute is higher than on other similar courses. Those who fail must pay to repeat the course, although Tabor does not charge the full cost of tuition.

The IACP and Irish Association of Humanistic and Integrative Psychotherapy (IAHIP) which oversee therapists, said they had yet to receive complaints from former staff or students of Tabor who were willing to put their names to accusations. Neither professional body will investigate anonymous grievances.

Psychotherapy is unregulated, with no legal obstacles to anyone wishing to set themselves up as a practitioner.
