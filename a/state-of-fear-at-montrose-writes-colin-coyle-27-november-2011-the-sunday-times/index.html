STATE OF FEAR AT MONTROSE writes Colin Coyle, 27 November 2011 The Sunday Times

Abuse by Church and Irish institutions

STATE OF FEAR AT MONTROSE; A Mission to Prey was billed as an exposé of sexual abuse but all it reveals is RTE’s failings in its pursuit of an innocent priest. The station now faces investigation itself, writes Colin Coyle

27 November 2011 The Sunday Times  © 2011 Times Newspapers Ltd. All rights reserved

Prime Time

It was three days before the broadcast of Prime Time Investigates: A Mission to Prey, a heavily promoted exposé of alleged sexual abuse by Catholic missionaries in Africa. Aoife Kavanagh, the programme’s reporter, had a final email to send.

At 1.49pm on Friday, May 20, she sent a message to Bishop Philip Sulumeti, the head of the Catholic diocese of Kakamega in western Kenya, from her RTE email account. It was marked “urgent”.

Sulumeti had been Fr Kevin Reynolds’s bishop when the Irish priest worked at a teacher-training college in rural Eregi. Kavanagh told him that she had recorded an interview with a woman who claimed that Reynolds had had “non-consensual sex” with her when she was a teenager, resulting in the birth of a baby daughter.

“We have very credible information that a number of years later, when his daughter was in school, you were made aware of this fact,” Kavanagh told the bishop.

“I understand that you yourself were struck by the resemblance between Fr Reynolds and his daughter. Our information is that you then went on to encourage Fr Reynolds, though a third party, another Irish priest, to pay money towards her education. The girl’s mother was never made aware the money came from Fr Reynolds, though she was very grateful to receive it.”

Kavanagh said she had “a number of sources” who had confirmed these details, and then asked the bishop to confirm the claims. “We intend to include this information in our programme,” she said.

At 10.29pm local time, Bishop Sulumeti replied, addressing the reporter as Dear Kavanagh. He rejected the allegations. “I am hearing them for the first time today from your email,” he wrote. “Indeed Fr Kevin Reynolds worked in the dioceses of Kisumu and Kakamega. I can vouch that [he] was an exemplary priest who worked very hard to evangelise our people.”

With only days to go before RTE’s broadcast, the bishop had denied a key piece of evidence underpinning Kavanagh’s belief that Reynolds was the father of a child in Kenya.

Nine days earlier, on May 11, Reynolds’s solicitors in Galway, had written to RTE, addressing the letter to the “Head of Broadcasting”.

They set out Reynolds’s denials of a series of allegations made by Kavanagh at St Cuan’s church in Ahascragh four days earlier, after a Holy Communion ceremony.

When confronted by the camera crew, comprising Kavanagh and three others, a smiling Reynolds had mistakenly believed they were there to cover the ceremony.

Instead Kavanagh accused him of raping a maid named Veneranda 30 years ago, and fathering a child by her who had been named Sheila.  Reynolds replied: “I never heard the name Veneranda. That’s the gospel truth.”

Believing that he had refuted the allegations, the parish priest visibly relaxed. As the camera crew was about to finish recording, he blithely remarked that he hoped he would “look good on the picture” and told them to “take care”.

During the on-camera interview, Kavanagh had put it to Reynolds that Sulumeti, his former superior in Kenya, was aware he had fathered a child. She said she knew the bishop had spoken to Reynolds about the child. Not so, Reynolds’s solicitor insisted. In fact their client had a letter of reference from the bishop. Kavanagh had shown no interest in seeing a copy, they remarked.

On May 18, at 4.46pm, five days before broadcast, Kavanagh replied to Fair & Murtagh’s legal letter in an email sent from her personal account. An individual journalist responding to a threatening letter from a solicitor personally would be regarded as unusual. Kavanagh did not keep the contents of the email from her team, however. Brian Páircéir, the show’s producer, was copied in.

The email stated that RTE intended to go ahead with the broadcast, despite Reynolds’s rejection. “Your client should know that we have been given an account by a very credible third-party source substantiating the allegation that Father Reynolds is the father of Sheila,” Kavanagh wrote. “We have other independent evidence confirming that he contributed financially to the education of his daughter, Sheila, and was made aware that she was his daughter.” Kavanagh signed off with a warning: “We would strongly urge Father Reynolds to reconsider his denials even at this late stage.”

Immediately prior to the broadcast, Fair & Murtagh wrote directly to Kavanagh telling her that Reynolds had left his parish while the allegations were being investigated by his order. They repeated Reynolds’s denials of the allegations.

They also enclosed a copy of the reference from Sulumeti, saying they hoped it would not be used “in a negative manner”. Any broadcast of the allegations against Reynolds would result in defamation proceedings, they warned.

By now Reynolds had offered to take a paternity test to prove definitively he was not the father of the child. It was an opportunity he was never given.

“SHE lives in a shack at the end of a slum in the capital, Nairobi,” 529,000 viewers heard Kavanagh intone as she introduced the story of Reynolds’s alleged victim in the documentary.

“Her name is Veneranda. When she met Fr Reynolds, she was working as a maid in a teacher’s house in the parish of Eregi in the west of the country. Fr Reynolds was based there, too, and would often call around. One day when the teacher was away, he forced her to have sex with him.” An interview followed with the woman. “I tried to refuse him but he said no one would be told what has happened here in this house,” she said. “He told me no one had saw him coming in.”

Soon after the alleged encounter, the woman realised she was pregnant. She claimed to have confided in another teacher who subsequently confronted Reynolds. Veneranda was said to have given birth alone, without help. “I was in so much pain. There was no light, no paraffin, just God to help me.”

Viewers were then introduced to Sheila, now aged 29. The girl’s lightbrown complexion contrasted noticeably with her mother’s darker skin. Sheila said she had been teased as a child about having a white father because of her complexion.

“People are talking stories,” she said in broken English. “Your mother had an affair with priest. It was very bad. I used to run away. Look at that girl, she was born with priest. These are white, and such like this.”

In a voiceover, Kavanagh said Fr Reynolds went on sabbatical for a year after the girl was born. “Prime Time understands he was told he had a daughter and was asked for help with her education,” the reporter claimed.

Sheila pointed out that she was not expecting any financial help from her “father”. She said: “I’d like to meet him, to know who he is. That would be all. I don’t want too much more — just to know who he is.”

The day after the programme, RTE Radio’s Morning Ireland broadcast a report on the documentary.

In a statement, Alan Shatter, the minister for justice, expressed his “revulsion at the unspeakable catalogue of abuse against children it revealed”.

“While the behaviour took place abroad, we have a solemn duty to do all that is within our power to ensure that perpetrators of this predatory abuse of children are brought to justice wherever it takes place,” he said.

Shatter said the law allowed for those who commit sexual offences abroad to be prosecuted for their crimes in Ireland. He had asked the garda commissioner to investigate whether prosecutions could be taken. Last Friday, Shatter withdrew this statement.

RTE carried a news report on its website later that day, stating that gardai were liaising with the Health Service Executive about any child protection issues “that arise in this jurisdiction from the presence here of a number of persons identified in the programme”. This suggested Reynolds would now be subject to a criminal investigation.

THREE days after the broadcast, on May 26, Kavanagh finally responded to the legal letter sent by Reynolds’s solicitors prior to the programme. The reporter, again in an email from her personal account, was not backing down. “Prime Time is satisfied that it can stand over each and every allegation made against your client in its programme,” she told Fair & Murtagh.

Kavanagh also reiterated that she would not be naming Reynolds’s accusers. “We will not be making the name and address of any person interviewed during the course of the programme available to your client.”

Three weeks passed and with RTE showing no signs of retracting the allegations or agreeing to a paternity test, Reynolds approached Fr Sean McDonagh, a leading member of the Association of Catholic Priests. McDonagh brought Reynolds to meet Robert Dore at the solicitor’s office, in Bridge Street, Dublin, on June 17. Dore had begun to represent priests aggrieved by how they were being treated following allegations of a sexual nature.

“About eight months ago, two friends of mine at the [Law Library] asked if I could give advice to priests referred to me by the Association of Priests,” he said last week. “They were all priests against whom allegations had been made. [It was] on the basis that they would not be in a position to pay for advice. I agreed and I met several priests in that context.”

On the day Reynolds met Dore, the solicitor advised that they institute proceedings immediately. A plenary summons was served on RTE later that day. A week later, Dore wrote to RTE saying Reynolds was “prepared, and indeed is most anxious, as a matter of urgency, to undergo a paternity test”.

Dore said his client was prepared to travel to Kenya, if necessary. He warned RTE if Veneranda and Sheila refused to participate, it would beg the question why. “A paternity test is now the sole definitive means left to Reynolds whereby he can definitively vindicate his character, which was impugned with such ferocity by your programme,” he wrote.

On July 7, RTE wrote to Dore saying they had hired Ormond Quay Paternity Services to carry out the tests, which begin by taking a swab from the inside of a person’s mouth. A doctor in Nairobi would perform the test on Sheila in the company of a representative from RTE, while Reynolds should fill out a DNA profiling request form and visit a GP to conduct his test.

On July 14, Reynolds travelled to Dublin and gave a DNA sample at Dame Street Medical Centre. He then awaited confirmation that Veneranda and her daughter, Sheila, had taken similar tests in Nairobi. The samples were then sent to Anglia DNA Services Ltd in the United Kingdom to be analysed. Both parties agreed that the results would be revealed to them simultaneously by Ormond Quay Paternity Services.

As Reynolds waited for confirmation that Veneranda and Sheila had taken their tests, Dore grew impatient, sending a rapid series of legal letters urging RTE to expedite the tests. It was then the bombshell dropped.

ON JULY 7, Sheila walked into the Mill Hill Missionaries headquarters in Nairobi and handed a letter to Fr Patrick T Ryan. “On behalf of my mother and I, I would like to make an apology towards the issue at hand concerning my biological father,” it said. “I have done various deep investigations and found out the truth about myself. I humbly request this matter to come to an end because Fr Kevin is not my biological father and I have found out who he is.” Sheila said she was not willing to take a DNA test or conduct any more interviews.  The letter was forwarded to Fr Anthony Chantry, the superior general of the Mill Hill Fathers in London. Reynolds got a copy on August 2. Dore forwarded it to RTE.  On August 25, the station wrote back to say Sheila had in fact taken the paternity test, despite the reservations expressed in her letter. The results would take two weeks. Despite Sheila’s letter absolving Reynolds, RTE was still not satisfied of his innocence.  On September 6, Reynolds was called by a journalist from the Irish Independent and asked whether he wanted to comment on news that the paternity test was negative. This, Reynolds was told, had been disclosed by sources at RTE. So Reynolds was definitively innocent, although the discovery by way of a “cynical leak” only “contrived to further aggravate the grotesque wrong done to him”, Dore told RTE.  The broadcaster’s solicitor responded the same day, saying they “were advised by Ormond Quay Paternity Services there was an issue with the test results and they would be recommending a retest”.

The second test was needed because Sheila had not brought photographic ID when she went to the GP in Nairobi to deliver her sample. So later that month a second sample was taken from Sheila and retested. Dore pressured RTE to apologise and to stop “clutching at straws”. On September 20, after receiving the results of the retest, RTE finally caved in.

Reynolds has since been paid damages of about €750,000. The legal fees, also paid by RTE, will add considerably to that total. RTE has used John O’Sullivan, a media insurance broker, to cover itself in the event of defamation but the broadcaster has refused to say whether it had insurance for A Mission to Prey.

Dore remains critical of RTE, especially of the “rushed” apology broadcast after Prime Time on November 17. Acknowledging this, RTE rebroadcast it on Friday night. Ken O’Shea, the editor of news and current affairs, and Ed Mulhall, the director of news and current affairs, have decided to stand down pending the outcome of a Broadcasting Authority of Ireland (BAI) investigation. Kavanagh and Páircéir will be off the air until the inquiry is concluded.

Dore is adamant his client would still be in court but for the paternity test. “If [the programme] was confined to an allegation of rape, it would have been difficult to disprove,” he said. “If Sheila could not have been found and we could not get a paternity test, I don’t think RTE would have backed down. They were gung-ho in their defence. They would have gone to the High Court, I believe, if the paternity test hadn’t materialised. They were convinced their story was true and that they had Fr Reynolds bang to rights, which begs the question, who was the thirdparty source who was so convincing?” An RTE internal investigation, as well as the BAI inquiry, will now ask why the programme was broadcast despite the strong denials from Reynolds and his bishop. RTE says it will publish its reviews by mid December. Dore said the outcome of the BAI inquiry should also be put into the public domain. “I look forward to reading it with interest,” he said.

Susan O’Keeffe, a Labour senator and former investigative journalist, is baffled as to why the Prime Time team did not allow the paternity test to go ahead first. “If you were trying to prove definitively an allegation like that and someone agreed to take a paternity test, I would have thought you would be celebrating,” she said. “Here was a chance to get 100% independent proof, pre-broadcast, which is so difficult to achieve.”

Kavanagh has insisted she did not rely solely on Veneranda’s testimony for her story. So who were her third-party sources, and were they simply mistaken or were their motives more sinister? WHEN Reynolds was first “doorstepped” outside St Cuan’s church, Kavanagh told him that a teacher named “Mactilda”, who taught with the priest at Eregi teachers’ college in Kenya, knew about the child and believed she was called Ursula. Reynolds said he did not know anybody by the name of Mactilda.

The teacher had allegedly told Kavanagh that Reynolds left Eregi after the child was born. This part of the interview was never broadcast. A former principal of Eregi teachers’ training college is named Matilda. Was she the “Mactilda” being alluded to? She is married to another local teacher, Thomas Amuhaya. The family could not be contacted this weekend.

Veneranda and Sheila could not be contacted either. According to his then bishop, Reynolds’s decision to leave the school and work in a parish one hour’s drive away had nothing to do with the birth of a child.

RTE is expected to publish the findings of its parallel inquiries, one of which is being conducted by John Horgan, the press ombudsman, by mid December. The BAI inquiry is due to be completed within two months but few media commentators believe the agency will turn around its first full inquiry in such a timescale. A BAI spokesman said last week the compliance committee had yet to meet to discuss who would even carry out the inquiry. The BAI may well be met by journalists eager to protect their sources, even if those sources provided flawed information.  Media guidelines differ from one institution to another, but the usual rule of thumb is that a source’s protection ends if a journalist establishes that they lied. If the source simply got it wrong, or made an honest mistake, a journalist is dutybound to protect them.  “While I’m sure RTE will co-operate in every way, a journalist is entitled to protect their sources, especially if they have agreed to speak on the basis of confidentiality,” O’Keeffe said.  The former Granada and BBC journalist knows this better than most, having been threatened with imprisonment for contempt of court after refusing to reveal the name of a key source for a World in Action exposé that led to the establishment of the beef tribunal in 1991.  “There are two victims in this,” she said. “Principally Fr Reynolds, but also the reputation of Prime Time Investigates, which has been doing really good, important work for a decade, and public opinion on investigative journalism in Ireland generally. For that alone, someone should pay the price, and that someone should be senior and in a position of authority.”  ‘We strongly urge Fr Reynolds to reconsider his denials’

This is the email that RTE reporter Aoife Kavanagh sent to Fr Kevin Reynolds’s solicitor just five days before the broadcast of Prime Time Investigates:

May 18, 2011, 16:46 Aoife Kavanagh to Fair & Murtagh Solicitors Dear Sir,  As we put it to your client already, we have recorded an interview with a Kenyan woman called Veneranda who asserts that, when she was a teenage girl, working as a maid in Eregi, she had a sexual encounter with Fr Reynolds.  Your client subsequently warned the girl never to tell anyone about what had happened. She became pregnant and gave birth to a baby girl and named her Sheila.  Furthermore, your client should also be aware that we have other independent evidence confirming that he contributed financially to the education of his daughter Sheila and was made aware that she was his daughter.  (a) Even if he cannot remember her name, did Fr Reynolds ever have sex with a teenage girl during his time in Kenya?  (b) Is your client denying any sexual relations, or encounters of a sexual nature, consensual or otherwise, with any member of the local or expatriate community during his time in Africa?  (c) Does Fr Reynolds continue to insist — despite the assertions of a number of people, independent of each other — that he has never been informed as a matter of fact, or even the possibility, by any person, directly or indirectly, that he fathered a child in Africa?  (d) Has your client ever discussed the issue of whether or not he fathered a child during his time in Africa with any other person, at any other time …?  We would strongly urge Father Reynolds to reconsider his denials, even at this late stage.  Regards, Aoife Kavanagh Fair & Murtagh’s reply on behalf of Fr Reynolds Our client reiterates that he did not have any sexual encounter with a Kenyan woman called Veneranda and did not have a child with this or any other woman. Our client does not know any child by the name of Sheila and was not told about any pregnancy.

(a) Our client denies having had sexual relations with any teenage girl during his time in Kenya.

(b) Our client did not have any sexual relations, consensual or otherwise, with any member of the local or expatriate community during his time in Kenya. This is a new allegation which … was not put during the course of your interview with him at St Cuan’s church on May 7. If you have specific details, please let us have [them] as, again, our client is anxious to deal with each and every allegation which is being levelled against him.

(c) Our client denies that he was ever informed by any person that he fathered a child in Africa, other than by yourselves.

(d) Our client never had discussions with any person on the issue of whether or not he fathered a child during his time in Africa, save where he had discussions with yourself.

The committee investigating the investigators

Who investigates the investigators? When questions arose in 2007 over the veracity of the sources used by journalist Justine Delaney-Wilson to justify a claim in an RTE Prime Time documentary that a priest, a pilot and politician snorted cocaine, the broadcaster conducted an internal inquiry. The findings were never published. The station refused to release them under the Freedom of Information act, claiming “post-transmission internal reviews” were exempt.

Following the defamation of Fr Kevin Reynolds, RTE again ordered an internal investigation. The station also asked John Horgan, the press ombudsman, to conduct a review of editorial procedures.

As public disquiet grew over RTE’s botched handling of the aftermath of the case, Pat Rabbitte, the minister for communications, announced that the Broadcasting Authority of Ireland (BAI) would hold an independent inquiry. This will be the first test of the BAI’s appetite and ability to investigate forensically the making of a programme, under powers granted to the quango under the Broadcasting Act 2009. The two-year-old organisation has been given a timeline of two months to report back to the minister. The results of its investigation will be published, although legally sensitive material may be taken out.

The organisation’s compliance committee, chaired by Professor Chris Morash, head of NUI Maynooth’s School of English, is being tasked with choosing an investigator and signing off on the BAI inquiry. Four members of the committee are appointed by the government, with the BAI picking the other four. The committee’s usual role is adjudicating on complaints from the public.

Other members include Paula Mullooly, a partner with Simon McAleese solicitors in Dublin. Mullooly, whose firm provides legal advice to Independent News & Media’s newspapers, was a member of the government’s working group on defamation. Another member of the compliance committee is John Waters, a media commentator who has already written critically about RTE’s handling of the Fr Reynolds affair, and who believes there is an institutional bias in the media against priests.

The investigator can be chosen from among the ranks of the committee, or can be an outsider. Whoever is chosen will have the power to compel RTE to hand over all documentation relating to the broadcast, and can require RTE officials to attend an interview.

If RTE is found to have breached a range of statutory regulations — such as on objectivity, impartiality and fairness — the broadcaster can be fined up to €250,000 by a court. RTE could also be fined for not co-operating with the inquiry.

The station has, of course, said it will co-operate fully, but what if the investigator begins to probe the identity of the confidential “third-party sources” used by the Prime Time Investigates team? Senator Susan O’Keeffe, herself a former investigative journalist, said it was important that the person chosen to conduct the inquiry had experience of the dynamics that arise prior to broadcasting a report that may have taken several months to compile. “They need to be familiar with the nuts-and-bolts process of making and broadcasting a piece of investigative journalism,” she said.  An experienced media lawyer believes the inquiry may yield information on what legal advice RTE journalists received.  “There is always a necessary tension between an editor, whose instinct will always be to run a story, and a lawyer, who wants to be careful,” she said.  An RTE source pointed out that it still does not know whether the BAI review will be “investigatory” in nature, particularly given the two-month deadline. “It may not be an investigation into all aspects of how the programme was made,” he said. “It may simply involve stating whether the programme breached the BAI guidelines on fairness and objectivity, and setting out how it breached them. By that stage, RTE’s own reviews will have been completed and published.”  Clearly, the station is hoping that, if it takes decisive action itself next month, on foot of the reports, the public outcry will subside and the emphasis on the BAI process will be diminished.  Last week the compliance committee had yet to meet to discuss the investigation. The RTE staff members who have stepped aside until the investigation is over may yet have a long wait before they return to their desks. ‘
