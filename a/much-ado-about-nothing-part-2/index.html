21 December 1978

Much ado about nothing? (Part 2)

Conferences & Commentaries

or: The New Religious Wave

By Ole Riis

The new religious wave does not indicate a sudden change in the trend of secularisation. The institutional position of the church has not changed. Rationalisation and mystification still continue hand-in-hand along the road of history. The point is rather that the new religious wave contains a revival of a religious orientation within a segment of society hitherto unaffected at any depth by religion. Whilst institutional religion in Western Europe has tended to become the bullwark of the old middle class, the new religious wave builds – mainly – upon the children of the new middle classes. (I do not like the concept of class in this connection, but use it nevertheless as shothand)

The new demand for religion

Before we come to the religious renaissance among the jetkids I would like to comment upon the concept of “need for religion”. Some participants in the debate hold that religion lingers on as an underlying primary need. Unsatisfied, this need gives rise to perverted projections, e.g. the fascist displays of mass-hysteria and Fuhrer- idolization.

An argument of this kind can never be disproven, since any effort to point to a sane person without any demonstrable religious inclination can always be cast aside with statements like: “oh, just wait….something will show” or “He is very religious after all, but does not show it outwardly…” etc.. Behind all this argument lie the problems connected with the vagueness of the very concept of religion. Just as the rock-fan holds his heroes and their music as sacred, and the tifoso adores “gli azurri” on about the same level as his madonina, and most members of a capitalist society hold Mammon and-its obscure powers in awe, in the same way the religious need can have so many and such diversified outlets that the whole discussion slips from our hands.

Another tradition maintains that the religious “need” is acquired and that it is self-inflating and self-defeating much like the craving for opium, or sexual perversions or super-duper cars, quad-equipment etc. First induced, the • need then grows and more of the thing does not lead to satiation but to demands for even more and more again… till you bust. More central to the debate: the channelling of the demand-structure in a monomanic direction makes people blind to basic problems concerning the real primary needs. This formulation will, I hope, serve to cover the marxist critique of religion and some other significant critiques. Such a critique does not, however, strike at religion in all meanings of the concept, but only at those magical forms which deflect social actions from a rational course, and at mystical trappings which prevent a person from perceiving the world he or she is a part of. It could be hypothesized that another kind of religion might exist which is without magic, mysticism and fetishism, which does not isolate man in his own thoughts and pacify him. It has even been argued that true Christianity is such a religion. This is of course acceptable as a hypothesis. However, the real test would lie in a historical demonstration of the social responsibility in Christianity.

To many sober critics it has nevertheless been proven that Christianity can be more than a mythology for the legitimization of the powers that be in society.

A position in between that of the universal and primary need for religion, and religion as a perverted demand, is religion as an acquired, but positive kind of demand. It has seldom been in the foreground in our culture, whereas it is very common in oriental religions. What I am thinking of is religion conceived of as resembling the connoisseur’s refined demand for vintage wine, Mouton Rothschild, of Henry Clay cigars and Furtwanglers version of “the 9th”. The connoisseurs form a religious elite, or maybe an aristocracy of religious life. They are not born with total sensitivity, but only with a rudimentary capacity for the acquired taste. Under the right conditions, however, the religious sense may be developed to the full. With the right teacher, or guru, one might reach higher levels of religious fulfilment. This conception is thus very far from the first idea of a universal and innate religious need. In this last sense, the need may remain at a rudimentary level, if the person in question develops only a part of his potential, and thus remains a lesser human being. Neither is there any question of a clearly felt need for religion.

The position mentioned above also differs from a dualistic one, where a pre-ordained minority possesses the charac­teristics necessary for developing the religious sense -for salvation, that is. And it differs from the critical position by regarding the religious need as basically po­sitive. These two might, however be combined. We might start from the same understanding of religious needs and interpret the critique of religion as similar to religion’s own critique of superstition. Then a residual element remains: “true religion”. The definition of this residual element, however, in very vague indeed, and varies a lot from one Religion to another. We might use three crude indicators: subjective feeling, subjective and intersubjective knowledge, and the functions of actions. In our own pietist tradition, the subjective affective affirmation is a common mark of salvation. In the oriental tradition, the knowledge seems important. Both these are hardly accepted by the materialist critics. The third indicator often regresses to action for itself, or ritualism. Some kind or set of activity is established a priori as the right one, and rigoristic adherence to the rules confirms a person’s status within the group. Such a position can be met among brahmins and pharisees. But the materialists will probably choose the third alternative in the functional sense: It is a necessity not that you want what is good, but that you do what leads to what is good. This demands a further explication of what “the good” really is. To most materia‑

lists, some kind of Christian altruism is fundamental. But the discussion may easily become entangled with problems of Pareto-optimality, and buddhist notions of obedience to the world and its secular powers.

To return to the main track: the religious debates in our culture are usually polarised a way that corresponds to the first and second interpretation of religious needs, the one

talking about universal needs for religion (or for Calvinists, among others, about selectively distributed pre-natal needs) and the other about false needs, the newer interpretation among the new religious movements seems to follow the third line indicated.

I should like to illustrate this point by a quotation from a former adherent of a neo-hindu guru. She talked about her experiences with drugs and hallucinogens, leading to a new level of cognition, but also to black trips, “bummers”. She tried religion as a drug, which could keep her high without bummers and negative after-effects. In this perspective, which is not uncommon, we can hardly talk about an innate demand for religion. We are, instead, confronted with persons who look upon religion as a means and not a goal in itself and who thus resemble connoisseurs in their need for elated sensory gratification through eating, seeing, smoking, drinking, etc.

From bohemians to psychedelic hippies

The modern industrial culture seems to emphasize two anti-systemic tendencies. We have on the one hand an idealistic, mystical, world-fleeing position, and on the other a materialistic, world-fighting, committed position. The one seeks a “true self”, unspotted by an absurd and horrible world, -the other a basic change in society, and thus the possibility of becoming a true human being. This means, that the latter position at depth has been self-negating, whilst the former has been self-enhancing in the first instance.

The first position can be likened to the bohemian cultures of the fin-de-siecle. The world is viewed as being as absurd as the percieved world in the artistic micro-cosmos. The culture emerges in night-long debates on nihilism, in drinking absinth or opium, in speculations about the values lost in asiatic mysticism, and in attempts to revolutionise the culture of the occident. Bourgeois aesthetics are smeared with disharmonies, with painting “noir-sur-noir”, with strindbergian dramas. This sub-culture was at one and the same time despised and admired by the bourgeois: despised for its immorality, more or less secretly admired for living the life of the individualist to the full. “Laissez­faire-et-merde-a-vous”.

This world in a way unites with the materialists in their critique of society, but it does not fuse organically with materialism. It does not want to become uniform and disciplined in the fight against capitalism. It doubts the possibilities of formulating socialist values or any value system at all. It is a culture for the hopeless and cynical… unless they gain hope through mystical contemplation. The elitist position, and the rejection of the collectivist and materialist positions may become an easy way to the Oriental or mystical alternative. Mystical enlightment is a goal easily understood by the fin-de-siècle artist and intellectual.

In a chaotic world the fixed point seemingly must lie in the true self, the cosmos in the inner depths of the individual. From an expressionism standing against the chaotic system to a deep impressionism there is a very short road indeed. There is – in my view – a direct descent from the bohemians to the beatniks, and further to the hippies and the mystics of the new religious movements (I shall not debate the new fundamentalism in this section, since it is a revival of rather old traits, and not a genuinely new phenomenon). Very little has changed, indeed, from the bohemians of Montparnasse to the new hippie cults in Christiania, except, of course the clothing – and the people who are the basis of the cult.

The latter point is the crucial one. The bohemian cult was clearly elitist. It was intellectually demanding to obtain admittance to the Circles of the self-appointed avant-garde. It was also existentially demanding. But the new hippie cult and the new religious movements are MASS movements. The intellectual standards are quite low. I have not heard of anyone rejected by the new gurus. By contrast only a very few are accepted as pupils of the classical gurus of hinduism. In the mass movements one is asked to obey, while the deeper exstential demands are quite limited. There is no demand for awareness of an inner pyre which torments and purifies the adept with inextinguishable demand for insight, knowledge, wisdom, sanctification.

The new religious movements remind me of Walt Disney’s version of Grimm, Hollywood versions of Roman gladiator fights, Italian plastic miniatures of Michelangelo’s works, and easy-readers for the rich-folks-low-brow-college-kids. Here the point is the dilution and popularization of thousand of years of philosophical wisdom from other cultures. Hinduism-an­easy-introduction-for-young-folks. With cartoons.

Confrontation with a new concept of the world

Since the relating of Oriental wisdom to the Occidental world-view is very demanding for serious intellectuals, the very effort is, of course, overwhelming and paralysing to those of lesser capacities. The confrontation with a quite new angle of vision, can be likened to the experimental situation, where one’s perception is distorted by putting on special glasses. Until one is able to orient oneself by correcting the new perceptive field, one is quite confused, maybe even fearful and in need of someone to offer support.

The guru is a kind of crutch for the young westerner, for whom the whole idea of the world is deeply questioned for the first time. Since we are talking of mass movements, where the adepts are or a rather average intellectual standing the new gurus often become permanent necessities. Mediocre gurus can make it. For in the kingdom of the blind, the one-eyed man is king.

What I am talking about here is really a very special situation. Most of us, myself included, live by a half­understood and unquestioned paradigm of the world and society we live in. We are more or less conditioned by Newtonian ideology of the universe. A few of us even have Einsteinian ideas. All these are in a rather vague form, and we are by no means concerned about the substance of light, the existence of black holes in the universe or the extent of space. We muddle through with some limited working-model of the universe. A few philosophers, artists and scientists in our history of culture have been confronted with the big question of the adequacy of their fundamental paradigms. Some just went out of their minds. Some found new answers. Some even had their new answers accepted in wide circles. Such as Galileo, Newton, Einstein.

Try, for a short time, to dwell upon the basic question of what you really know. See, then, how much of your knowledge can be questioned. When the first questions arise, you become very confused and fearsome. The world seems to slip from beneath you.

Now the hipsters are in a way alienated from the standard bourgeois culture from which they came. This alienation does not take the same form as the general reification processes of late capitalism. We are discussing a different phenomenon. Probably very few of the hippies have been subject to a lasting job in mass industry. The cultural alienation may stem from a lack of integration in the grey-flannel-suited, nice-middle-class commercial­technical-job and homes. The young people in question may be subject to parental divorces, or just separated by lack of interest from their parents. This separation makes relating to the fictitions world of mass-media more common and in the new generation more crucial.

We are confronted with a new generation, which has had VERY much contact from early childhood with television, and its confusion of facts and fiction, its pseudo-personal-relationships and dream-worlds.

I have not seen any long-term studies of the life-careers of the most television-prone young people. I should not be surprised if many of them end up searching for mystical and culturally alienated answers to the basic existential questions. Nor should I be surprised, if the least socially integrated of these became very self-searching, that is, subject to the answers given by the new religious movements.

The new religious movements

These can be typified by:
1. Syncretism, combinations of western (christian) and o­riental world-views
2. Ego-centredness, searches for the true self
3. leader-orientation, divination of a visible authority.
4. Idealistic concept of the world – in contrast with empiricist, materialist, pragmatic.
5. Strong group-attachment

The latter point seems difficult to link with the second until one understands that group practices may be other-directed, or group-directed. In the first form we have mutual, personal relationships. In the second, we have one versus group-as-a-totality relationships. The leader, or guru, is often the visible point of focus for the group as a totality. This indicates that the integration of the group really conceals a star-shaped pattern of me-and-the-common-guru-relationships. The group is integrated by the common leader, but atomized at the person-to-person-level. This is very often the case in the new religious movements.

The formation of personal subgroups is very often prohibited. The reasons are many. But the function is among other things to limit sectarianism, fragmentation, and organized criticism from below of the leadership. It can be likened to the divide-and-rule-dictum.

To return to the central question: Why did the new mysticism get such a mass basis – and why did the religious undertones become the main thema of the hippie cult? There seem to be three major roads to the new religious movements. One is for the few who actually seek new mystical insight. The broader one is for the many who just want a shower which might freshen their life, improve their self-reliance and self-management. And then we have a back-road, for the band-wagonners- those who do, what it is “in” to do, in their special sub-culture, whether it is smoking pot, reading Marx or meditating.

The first category covers very largely the most culturally alienated. People with very varied former experiences, including drugs. The second are more integrated, but not quite successful in the bourgeois world. They are individualistic, but insecure about themselves. Being ego-centric, insecurity about oneself is the same as insecurity about the world. So a new world-orientation is still the end product. However while this product is actually wanted by the first category, it comes more as a by-product for the second. These want a technique, a rather simple mental gimmick with which to stabilize themselves. Many come to understand, however, in the process of obtaining the gimmick, that much more is needed, that the problems are deeper-rooted.

Many come in with a very vague background: A little curiosity, a little desire to reduce personal fears. Many leave again without becoming further enmeshed in the spider’s web. But many become incoherent, confused, surrounded, devoured, when confronted with major questions about the world and their own existence, and, being intellectually middle-range persons, they find they have taken on a bigger task than they can cope with. They cannot evade the questions posed, nor can they answer them on their own. So they must deepen their involvement. Since the alternatives are subjugating one’s ego to the definitions of the movement, or breaking away and living in chaos and fear, the answer is given: What could you choose when given the choice of wandering blindfold along the cliff edge or of following with obedience and humility the possibly harsh but reliable paternal figure? You might of course reject THIS movement and this special guru. But such a rejection often leads to further subjugation to another movement with more emphatic answers and a guru with more authority and with a more authoritarian style.
