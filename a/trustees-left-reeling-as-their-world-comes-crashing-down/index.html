Trustees left reeling as their world comes crashing down

Victory (Faith Movement or Prosperity Gospel) Archive Abundant Life

Maeve Sheehan: Evangelical church

trustees left reeling as their whole

world comes crashing down

Receivers called in as Victory Christian

Fellowship owes bank €18 million.


http://www.independent.ie/opinion/analysis/maeve-sheehan-evangelical-church-trustees-left-reeling-as-their-whole-world-comes-crashing-down-29348192.html

Maeve Sheehan – 16 June 2013
IT is Friday night and the trustees of Ireland’s most talked about evangelical church are reeling from a tumultuous couple of weeks.

Their church, the Victory Christian Fellowship, owes €18m to Bank of Scotland. Receivers sent in to take possession of the church’s three prime properties were blocked by staff and some of the congregation. And when the trustees failed to show up in court to answer accusations that they had ignored court orders, a High Court judge directed gardai to hunt them down.

IT EMERGED IN COURT THAT THE CHURCH HAD LOST ITS CHARITABLE TAX – EXEMPTION STATUS BECAUSE OF ALL ITS COMMERCIAL ACTIVITIES.’

Brendan Hade, a dapper businessman, his elegant wife Sheila, and their friend, Gerry Byrne, narrowly escaped jail on Friday when they agreed to facilitate a peaceful handover of the church’s three Dublin properties to the bank’s receivers at 2pm tomorrow.

They include Kilmacud House in Stillorgan, 39 Westland Row and a state-of-the-art prayer centre in Firhouse that was built five years ago.

Victory Christian Fellowship is now in the news for all the wrong reasons.

The church has come under media scrutiny in the past for encouraging people to donate a tenth of their earnings to the church, and other money-generating schemes. Last week’s events turned a sharper spotlight on its activities.

The burning question now is how an evangelical prayer group that started in the Hade front room in Rathfarnham 25 years ago turned into a multi-million euro enterprise with debts of €18m?

It emerged in court that the church lost its charitable tax-exemption status because of all of its commercial activities. Meanwhile, Mike Garde, of Dialogue Ireland, which monitors religious groups, has claimed that people were urged to donate money that they could not afford.

It has separately been alleged that the Hades and Gerry Byrne use the church to fund their lifestyles.

It’s no surprise that the trio felt the time had come to give their first ever interview. Sitting in a spartan apartment in Sandyford Industrial Estate, they are fresh from their high-profile courtroom outing on Friday. They deny the accusations of milking their congregation for money, of dubious financial dealings and of profiting personally from the church’s many money- spinning activities. They say they live off incomes from their own private businesses.

“We took nothing. As trustees we cannot take a salary,” they said.
Most of their problems seem to have sprung from their ambitions to spread the gospel – evangelist-style – through a new building and prayer centre with enough attractions to entice people inside.
“We put up a state-of-the-art facility that attracted people in. Normal people won’t come into a church. People came there for Starbucks and they ended up going to church. People came there to see Jedward. It’s an attraction, to showcase the gospel. It’s a modern church,” said Gerry Byrne.
The building in Firhouse has left the Victory Christian Fellowship nursing an €18m debt, an enormous turnaround for a church that evolved as a casual prayer group 25 years ago.
The Hades are self-made business people who ran several shops and businesses, some of which are now run by their children. Gerry Byrne met them when his printing business ran off some gospel material for their prayer group. At the time, he was sinking under the recession, his businesses were in difficulty. “I got interested and it literally changed my life,” he said.
The prayer group became immediately successful. Numbers grew and members were encouraged to donate one tenth of their income. They later landed a lucrative State contract for housing asylum seekers, and a Revenue scheme that allows charities claim back tax paid by donors provided another six-figure source of income.
“We teach tithing . . . but it’s voluntarily, we don’t insist on it,” said Brendan Hade, denying Dialogue Ireland’s claims that people were pressured to donate large sums. “I think way back we had a person who gave us money and who wanted it back. We gave it back. If people give us money and then they feel they shouldn’t have done it, we give back the money. We are not in this to get money off people. We know 100 per cent that no one went to Mike Garde to complain about us.”
Their most lucrative activity was in housing asylum seekers. The trustees got a bank loan to buy Kilmacud House in Stillorgan in 1996 and later expanded to run two other accommodation centres for asylum seekers. Over the decade or so that it operated, it generated €1m a year for the church.
When that lucrative State contract came to an end, the trustees applied for planning permission to turn Kilmacud House into apartments, to help fund a dedicated prayer centre that they wanted to build at a site in Firhouse.
According to Mr Byrne, they got a loan of €13m and planned to sell Kilmacud and Westland Row for €10m. That would have left the church with a “manageable” debt of €3m. But the property crash left them with two buildings they couldn’t sell and a crippling loan of €18m.
According to Byrne, they were banking on Kilmacud House to help the church out of its financial difficulties. As trustees, they had secured another lucrative State contract with the Health Service Executive in recent months to redevelop the property as a primary care centre.
A planning application – which includes a three-storey building and a penthouse apartment – went in on May 1 and has attracted six objectors, including the Carmelite nuns. Nevertheless, Byrne believes that it has the potential to “solve all of our financial problems”.
Their plans were scuppered by Bank of Scotland Ireland which last month called in the loan and appointed receivers to take possession of the three properties at Kilmacud, Westland Row and Firhouse.
On May 29, the bank-appointed receivers began trying to take possession of the properties.
According to Sheila Hade: “The people are the church and they decided to protect their church. We didn’t hire in specialised guys that the bank hired in.”
They insist that they did not know that a High Court judge had ordered them to appear in court after the receivers complained that they couldn’t get on to the properties. They also claimed to be unaware that an attachment order had been issued to have them forcibly brought before the court.
When gardai called to their homes in Rathfarnham at 6am last Wednesday to haul them before the High Court, Brendan and Sheila Hade and Gerry Byrne were not at home. “We were away,” said Sheila. Where were they? “I can’t tell you, because of all that’s going on,” she said. “But it was church business, to prepare for an international prayer conference this weekend.”
Gardai did arrest their sons, Niall and Brian, who were brought before the court last Wednesday morning. The judge ordered gardai back out to track down the parents. In the end, the trio showed up in court voluntarily on Friday, flanked by a small group of supporters, where they agreed to abide by the High Court’s orders.
The charity recently lost its valuable tax-exemption status. They said they only recently learned that the Revenue had suspended its charitable tax status because they hadn’t declared a commercial restaurant would be operating in the Firhouse prayer centre.
They are appealing, and while they wait the church is missing out on another lucrative source of income.
According to Byrne, the charity is due around €200,000 from the State in a charitable tax rebate but the repayment is on hold pending the appeal. The scheme has brought in astonishing sums: €208,000 in 2010 and €220,000 in 2009.
Byrne said they have not received any money from the Government since 2010. It is a lot of money. So what did they do with it? “The money we built up went into the new project and then the world recession caught up with us.”
