Uncertain future for the Legionaries of Christ following Holy See investigation

Abuse by Church and Irish institutionsLegionaries of Christ

The Universe, Sunday May 2, 2010

EX-MEMBERS HAVE GOOD REASONS FOR INTEREST IN THE FATE OF THEIR FORMER ORDER

By Sarah Mac Donald

Universe by Lennon

“THE smoke of Satan has entered the Church,” Pope Paul VI stated in 1972. In the wake of the clerical sexual abuse revelations, was the Pope’s statement a prophetic denunciation of the fissures of corruption which are now unravelling? Take Fr Marcial Maciel, the deceased (2008) founder of the Legionaries of Christ and exponent of a brand of conservative Catholicism which endeared him to many prelates in the Church, including Vatican Secretary of State, Cardinal Angelo Sodano, as well as John Paul II’s personal secretary, Cardinal Stanisław Dziwisz. Sadly, the Mexican priest, who was considered a paragon by many, has been exposed as an artful chameleon who led a grotesque double life. These shocking revelations have left many wondering if his foundation should be disbanded. The misdeeds of ‘Nuestro Padre – as Legion members used to call him – include the sexual abuse of teenage seminarians, the sexual abuse of his own children, whom he fathered in illicit relationships with various women, drug addiction and financial corruption. The eight-month Vatican- instigated visitation of the Legionaries is awaited with baited breath. The order had previously been considered the most “successful” in the Church, numbering 800 priests and 1,300 seminarians. There are also 65,000 lay members in the Legion’s affiliate, Regnum Christi. Having delivered their reports on March 15, the five Vatican-appointed visitors were due to present them to the Holy See on April 30 when a decision would be made as to the future. Among those interested in the verdict is Paul Lennon. He spent just over 23 years, between the ages of 17 and 41, as a Legionary priest before disagreements with the founder over the mistreatment of members forced the Irishman to leave. He is now a psychologist and is involved in the website www.regainnetwork.org which is operated by a small group of ex-members, as well as concerned parents and friends of active members. He has detailed his experiences in the Legion and of Fr Maciel in his book Our Father Who Art in Bed.

Lennon was one of the first eight Irish-born members of the order. In 1961, following a vocations talk by a member of the order, which was just 20 years old at the time, the Dubliner signed up along with two others in his class at St Vincent’s Christian Brothers in Glasnevin. Fellow student Fr John Devlin later become personal secretary to Fr Maciel, while the other member of the trio, Fr Thomas Moylan, held mid-ranking positions within the organisation, though it seems he too has now left. As part of the “first batch of Irish guys” to begin novitiate in Spain in 1961, Lennon describes himself and the others as “very idealistic and very generous”. Recalling the context in which he made his decision to join, he told The Universe: “The Legion recruited as a missionary group. “In Ireland at that time there was a lot of fervour around missionary groups. The whole idea of Mexico for those of us who had barely set foot outside the country won us over.” Studies in Salamanca in Spain followed but Lennon was also learning his first lessons about appearance and reality in the Legion.

Surprised

“When we joined, we were gung-ho about being missionaries in Latin America,” he said. However, while studying in Spain, it began to dawn on him that “the Legion was not really a missionary society. It was an organisation which was targeting society’s elite”. This surprised him because that had not been his understanding of the apostolate. Shortly after ordination, he was assigned to study at Southern Connecticut State College. He hoped to become a counsellor at a high school. “Then, because of something I put in one of my letters to Maciel, all of a sudden he told me: ‘We are going to send you to the missions’.” For Lennon, “part of being able to stay in the Legion is how you assimilated to these shifts which kept you off balance”. These shifts, at Maciel’s whim, were compounded by the contentious vow of silence which was taken by members in addition to the usual vows of poverty, chastity and  oedience. The vow of silence prohibited members from making criticisms of the order or the founder and required them to report any members who did. In 2007, the Vatican ordered its removal. The vow, in Paul Lennon’s view, was symptomatic of the lack of transparency which has dogged the Legion from its early days. “Fr Maciel used to say that the Legion had to learn from the Communists in that each religious community was a cell and each cell was isolated. One cell did not communicate with another cell,” he recalled. “Fr Maciel was very dictatorial. There was a lot of control. When you study sects and cults, it is apparent that information control is a big thing. It is incredible how this man seemed to do all this stuff naturally because he had never studied anything. “Some people believe he didn’t even finish his studies for the priesthood. He was so good at conning everybody, he even conned his uncle into ordaining him. At the time of his ordination at 24 years, he had already started the Legion.” Today the Legion is estimated to have assets worth €25bn with an annual budget of €486m. “The way he crafted this secrecy was marvellously well done. Even when I started having disagreements with Maciel, I still hadn’t the slightest idea that he was up to any other sort of stuff,” Lennon said. This comment refers to his abuse of more than 20 seminarians as young as 13-15 years, such as Juan Vaca, who took part in Jason Berry’s groundbreaking documentary Vows of Silence.

There is no doubt in Paul Lennon’s mind that most of the Legion’s rank and file didn’t have the slightest idea of Maciel’s activities. In Lennon’s view, Maciel certainly was not saint material” but he wonders “what was his genius that he was able to project himself as a saint?” Many were duped, from Pope John Paul II to Fr Richard John Neuhaus, editor of First Things, who said be believed with “moral certainty” that the charges against Maciel were false and malicious, while former US Ambassador to the Vatican, Mary Ann Glendon, spoke of Maciel’s “radiant holiness”.

Many, including his victims and ex-members of the Legion have, according to Lennon, tried to psycho-analyse Maciel. “Was he a narcissist? Was he a paedophile narcissist? That would seem to go along with his personality,” Lennon said. “There was also this deviousness in Maciel – that is why so many people are surprised by the double life. All the cunning and the deceit and lack of consideration for other People’s problems or feelings ties in with what they term an anti-social personality disorder.” Alejandro Espinosa, an early Legionary and another of Maciel’s victims, has written a book in Spanish – El Legionario – about Maciel’s sexual abuse. In it, he asks whether it was something in Maciel’s childhood that created the destructive and harmful man who led the Legion.

Reform

According to Lennon: We know he had a very harsh father who would take the whip to him. Alejandro believes Marcial  was sexually abused by one of [his father’s] farm hands.” Some commentators have suggested that the likely outcome of the Vatican visitation will be the appointment of an external commissioner who will bring the Legion through root and branch reform. The Legion maintains its charism is intact, but should such assurances be taken at face value? In May 2006, the Pope ordered the-then 86-yearold Fr Maciel to a life of prayer and penance, but the Legion never acknowledged his misdeeds. Rather, they maintained that he was a victim of a smear campaign, comparing his suffering to that of Christ, and they continued to foster the cult-like devotion  to their founder. It was only in late March of this year that they made an about-turn, acknowledging Maciel’s misdemeanours. The Legion currently defends itself by saying “a weak vessel can hold a treasure”. For Lennon, who works as a mental health therapist at a department of human services in Virginia, this is akin to suggesting that the Legion is “the flower that springs from a dung heap”. For the former priest, the order’s attempt to distance itself from its founder simply won’t wash. Nor do the current leadership’s denials of any knowledge of Marciel’s misdeeds. For Lennon, the Church must seek to answer the question:  “How deep or how extensive was Maciel’s corruption?” Lennon, who left in 1984 and had a breakdown as he dealt with the legacy of bullying and control tactics, explains further: “In other words, has the leadership been infected by the duplicitousness of Maciel – did they cover up for him? Do Legionaries really have freedom of conscience and is this a real form of religious life?” For Lennon and other ex-members of the Legionaries and Regnum Christi, these two groups have for years displayed worrying cult-like practices which simply must be investigated. “If this is a pseudo religious order, and if it is really corrupt, then it might have to be disbanded,” he said.
