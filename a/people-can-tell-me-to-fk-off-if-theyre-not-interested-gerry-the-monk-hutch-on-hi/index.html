CriminalityCultism

Was a headline in the Sunday Independent on November 17th, cultism or not?

I am reserving judgement as to the motive of Gerry Hutch in standing for election, but Hutch is ‘absolutely’ confident he will be elected in Dublin Central.

Having lived down the road from where Gerry Hutch comes from at Clonmore Villas on the Ballybough Road near Croke Park, I was aware of his presence but more later after his stretch limousine business operated around the area. It was called CAB in defiance of the Criminal Assets Bureau.

I lived across the road from Tony Gregory and worked with his campaign from 1981, but never saw Hutch anywhere. When I first arrived in 1979, I went to see the Jesuit Peter McVerry who was living in the Summerhill flats, and we formed a Mennonite community that remained in the area till 1994.

My next major interaction with Hutch was when Paul Williams relayed Hutch’s past in a cult series called Cracking Cults, where Paul addressed the topic of criminal cults. His focus was on Gerry Hutch and the conference was on Foley Street where he was born. Paul’s knowledge of his criminal strategy and his difference from other criminals sticks in his memory.


Where Sinn Féin, Jonathan Dowdall, Gerry Hutch & Cultism meet?

Posted on 18 April 2023 by dialogueireland | Edit

Sunday World’s Crimeworld have published a wonderful analysis of the trial and the judgement in the case of Gerry Hutch. Not guilty and free to go. There is also a very important article by Fionnán Sheahan in the Irish Independent.

But my memory was triggered again when the case of Jonathan Dowdall came to court and then he was a witness in regard to the Regency Hotel at the Special Criminal Court.

A lot of dots were joined as Dowdall seemed to link Hutch to McDonald but not to any particular criminality

This brought me back to the series of lectures in 2007 organised by Dialogue Ireland called the “Cult Busters” held near Foley St where Gerry Hutch started his acquisition of property and where he was born.

Paul Williams at that point was the Crime Correspondent for the Sunday World.

I had asked Paul to speak and he initially was not sure how relevant linking crime to Cults was?

The 1978 “Jonestown’ massacre began a series of cult explosions which culminated in the September 11 suicide missions. In between these events there were outbreaks of cultist violence in 1993 in Waco, the Solar Temple in 1994, Aum Supreme Truth, killed people on the subway in Japan in 1995. 1997 saw the suicides in San Diego of “Heaven’s Gate’. Again 2000 there was the Marian Cult, “The Ten Commandments of God’ in Uganda which killed all its members. All the world’s religions have had cultist elements.

Even the cults of nationalism and secularism have contributed to this phenomenon. Cracking Cults is a course of 10 seminars organised by Dialogue Ireland and delivered by Ireland’s leading expert on cults, Mike Garde. He will be joined by a number of experts in areas like Crime, Politics, Islam, and Eastern Religion who will look at their area of expertise from a cultist perspective. Its aim is to analyse the cult phenomenon, how cults operate, how seemingly normal people can be induced to plant bombs or change radically after a short period of membership, and how some can be both political and religious in form.

While the focus of the course will be on religious, human potential and philosophical groups, it will stress that cults are not so much entities, but more pervasive attitudes and tendencies that can appear in any group including our own. Hence the course will use the lens of cultism to shed light in dark places in the more general areas of crime and politics but also enable those participating to be equipped to recognise and guard against the power of cults to corrupt and control. This course is a must for anyone with even the slightest interest in the health of our society who wants to study the cultist attitudes and tendencies found here.The only remaining online webpage about the 2007 Dialogue Ireland “Cracking Cults” Conference.

But he gave a fascinating insight into Gerry Hutch which very much corroborated with what Crime World’s Nicola Tallant and Niall Donald said about the case. It was also geographically where the event took place near Talbot St and Monto on Foley St where he was born.

Nicola Tallant already had the cult background working on investigations on Tony Quinn and Scientology. She moved more centrally into crime reporting and became the chief crime correspondent of the Sunday World.

The issues that arise, as now that Easter is behind us, is how the government has this double agenda of being very much behind the peace process but still commemorates the criminal acts of the 1916 Insurrectionists/Rising, Arbour Hill, Bodenstown and Beal na Blagh instead of taking the 1998 GFA agreement as our starting point for peace.

Serious issues arise from the lack of legal undergirding for the Witness Protection programme especially when the main witness Jonathan Dowdall was protecting himself rather than giving actual evidence.

The judgement of the court on Monday 17th April 2023 leads us to the conclusion that not only is Jonathan Dowdall a liar but was also only concerned with saving his own skin. This was to such an extent that he even wished to place his family friend Gary Hutch in the frame as the shooter of David Byrne at the Regency Hotel on 5th February 2016.

Where it is clear from the judgement that Gerry Hutch was involved with the planning of the murder, the judges could not rely on the evidence to link him to the actual carrying out of the murder. They could not rely on the evidence of Dowdall as they considered him a complete liar.

The result is this raises very important questions about Dowdall and his links to Sinn Féin. It is clear that Dowdall is not only linked to Sinn Féin but also the IRA. This raises the question as to whether the Army-Council is still actively controlling Sinn Féin?

The Police forces in Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland have not changed their view that there is an overarching-body controlling Sinn Féin.

This view is now universally rejected by commentators in the South. Is the Republic able to resist the march of Sinn Féin? Are we about to elect a government which has an Army-Council controlling the Cabinet?

Here is the article about Hutch standing for election.


Late Dublin TD Tony Gregory is his political inspiration
Cartel boss Daniel Kinahan was like ‘the devil’
He would work with Sinn Féin’s Mary Lou McDonald and Fianna Fáil’s Micheál Martin if elected
Immigrants are needed if they are here to work


November 17 2024- Ali Bracken

Ireland’s most unlikely general ­election candidate, Gerry ‘The Monk’ Hutch, wants to be very clear about two things.

The first is that he “couldn’t care less” about Sinn Féin leader Mary Lou McDonald. He says he is not running in the Dublin Central constituency to try and take her seat.

He rubbishes suggestions that he is interested in revenge against Sinn Féin after its former councillor, Jonathan Dowdall, was a state witness against Hutch in last year’s Regency murder trial.

“It’s nothing whatsoever to do with Mary Lou. I couldn’t care less about what she does, or what Sinn Féin say they will do,” he told the Sunday Independent. “I’m focused on what I will do. I could probably be considered old, but I’m fresh. I’ve a lot of life experience. To go into the Dáil, you have to be hungry and ready to fight, and I am.”

‘It’s official, I’m running’ – Gerry ‘The Monk’ Hutch confirms registration as Election 2024 candidate
Aontú targeting up to 10 seats in General Election and shuts down any Coalition with current Government
‘I’ve never shied down from a good fight’ – Michael Flatley on cancer, renewing his vows and missing his son at Eton.

The second matter the veteran criminal (61) wants to clear up is why he is running. He said: “I’m doing it because people in my community asked me to. I want to serve the people of Dublin 1, 3 and 7. I want to serve the country. I’ve a list as long as my arm on the things that need to be done.

“But I’m not the type of person who would beg anyone for their vote. If you want to vote for me, then do. I won’t be twisting anyone’s arm. I will be out, knocking on certain doors. People can tell me to f**k off if they’re not interested. I have to expect that cheek from people. Am I confident I’ll get elected? Absolutely.

Hutch sat down with the Sunday Independent last week to discuss his political aspirations, his criminal past and about being the “patriarch” of the Hutch family. His family has been embroiled in a feud with the Kinahan cartel that has claimed 18 lives.

​His brother, Eddie, and three of his nephews were murdered in the violence. Hutch declines to discuss the impact the loss of these family members had on him.

Gerry Hutch says Dublin Central has been failed by its TDs

But he is prepared to talk politics. What are his policies as an Independent candidate for the people of Dublin Central?

“My number one thing is homelessness and housing,” he said. “There are almost 4,500 children homeless. Homelessness turns them into child criminals. On top of that, we have the housing crisis, not enough places for people to buy affordable homes. It’s an emergency situation.

“If there’s a flood, there are emergency measures. This is the same. There are derelict properties all over this city. I’ve been involved in property deals for years, so I have experience. They all need to be bought up by the State. There is too much red tape in this country. We have to get rid of the red tape, and quickly.”

Gardaí say his wealth and collection of properties has been funded by the proceeds of crime. The Monk is long suspected of having planned two of the biggest armed ­robberies in the State.

The first targeted a Securicor van outside the Bank of Ireland at Marino Mart, Dublin, in 1987. The second was the £3m raid on the Brinks Allied depot in Clonshaugh, north Dublin, in 1995.

Hutch insists he had nothing to do with these two armed robberies. “I was arrested for them. They obviously knew it wasn’t me at the end of day because I wasn’t charged.”

Gardaí believe he was the mastermind behind both robberies. They also believe Hutch invested millions from these crimes into the importation of cannabis.

Again, Hutch denies this, saying he has never been involved in drug dealing. He points out that he has no criminal convictions linked to the drugs trade.

There is no denying that several members of the Hutch family are involved in criminality. Hutch’s Regency murder trial heard about the existence of the Hutch organised crime gang (OCG), a group the Garda National Drugs and Organised Crime Bureau (DOCB) also says is high on their radar.

“There was no evidence given that I was a member of this Hutch organised crime gang. It was said that I am the head of the Hutch family, the patriarch.”

But back to his political policies. Where does he stand on the hot election topic of immigration?

‘In the last few years, we’ve had four TDs [in Dublin Central], but we’ve had no representatives,’ says Gerry Hutch. ‘They’re politicians, but they’re doing shag all.’ Photo: Mark Condren
“This country needs immigration. It always has. I remember as a kid, immigration up in Summerhill, the fish and chip shops. There’s a guy there, [he] gives free chips to people who are hungry. He’s immigration. He’s more Irish now than he is Italian.

“I welcome integration and immigrants coming into Ireland, but bring your toolbox with you and bring your skills. Don’t come into Ireland and expect to sponge off the dole.”

Hutch added that “we can make an allowance” for people coming in from Ukraine, but said he felt some asylum-seekers coming into Ireland from elsewhere are “not being vetted” and that they are regularly housed in underprivileged areas rather than in “D4”.

He feels he has been involved in politics for decades, saying he is regularly called in as a mediator when disputes break out within his community in Dublin’s inner city. He even tried to mediate between his nephew Gary and mob boss Daniel Kinahan when they fell out, in a row that ultimately sparked the Hutch-Kinahan feud.

“I’ve always had a bit of politician in me, mediating with people… I’ve been a politician for the last 40 years, really. In the community as an unofficial politician.”

Gardaí have, on occasion, asked him to step in and help sort out disputes between people in the north inner city, he claims. “The police have often asked me to go and sort things. Sometimes the guards are limited to what they can do. That’s where I’ve stepped in before.”

Hutch is up against a number of high-profile sitting TDs in Dublin Central, including Sinn Féin leader McDonald and Fine Gael’s Public Expenditure Minister Paschal Donohoe. The other two TDs in the constituency are Gary Gannon of the Social Democrats and the Green Party’s Neasa Hourigan.

Gerry Hutch leaves court a free man after the Regency murder trial. Photo: Mark Condren
​Hutch doesn’t mince his words in his evaluation of the job these four TDs have done in the community he seeks to represent.

“In the last few years, we’ve had four TDs, but we’ve had no representatives,” he said. “They’re politicians, but they’re doing shag all. I mean, that’s the way I look at it and that’s the way the people in the community see it as well.”

Despite this, Hutch said he would work with any political rival should he be elected. “If I get elected, I’ll be doing everything in the best interest of my constituency and the country. And if I have to work with Mary Lou, or if I have to work with Micheál Martin, then I would.”

Hutch’s political hero is the late Tony Gregory, a former Independent TD and long-standing community representative in Dublin’s north inner city.

“Tony Gregory would have been the top man around this area for a long time. And the people around here are looking for another Tony Gregory. They think they’re getting that in me, but I’d never be able to walk in that man’s shoes in a thousand years.”

Hutch grew up in poverty but opted to move with his own family to Clontarf. He said he would hit back at any criticism for choosing to bring up his family in the significantly more affluent area of Clontarf, saying he did so because he didn’t want his children to grow up in an area “neglected by the State”.

He has maintained his links to the north inner city, where much of his extended family still lives. In 1998 he bought Corinthians Boxing Club, near Buckingham Street, renting it to the club for €1 a year on a 99-year lease after its previous premises were destroyed by a fire. He will be using it “a little” as his election HQ, mainly just to store posters and host the occasional political meeting.

“My heart is 1,000pc in the north inner city. I’m from the heart of the city, until the day I die. I’m only a blow-in in Clontarf.”

The man known as The Monk acknowledges his political campaign will be “quite different” to many others standing for election on November 29.

Gerry Hutch, aged 10, outside Rutland Street school in Dublin


He “won’t bother” with leaflets “because they only end up in the green bin, unread”. But he will put up some election posters, though not as many as his political rivals.

He doesn’t expect to spend even close to €10,000 on his election campaign, saying: “No, nothing like that amount at all.”

Hutch was issued with a garda information message in 2016 — known as a GIM form — warning him of a threat to his life from the Kinahan cartel.

That threat has not been officially removed, but gardaí and Hutch believe it has reduced significantly.

However, he remains on his “guard” about security concerns. As a ­consequence, he will do some door-to-door ­canvassing in Dublin Central, but it won’t be pre-planned or advertised. A lot of his campaign will instead focus on drumming up support online, through video messages.

“I wouldn’t want to put anyone else, or even myself, in danger. So yeah, it’s fair to say it’s a slightly different campaign I’m running.”

There are several other factors that mark his bid for the Dáil as extraordinary.

He was recently released from a short prison stint in Lanzarote, where he lives for much of the year, on suspicion of money-laundering offences.

Hutch was released from Tahiche Prison on November 4 on €100,000 bail. He remains under investigation in Spain as the “alleged leader of an international gang of criminals”.

Is he concerned about the ongoing criminal probe in Lanzarote led by a Spanish judge, and potential criminal charges? “I’m not worried at all. I don’t have a problem with it. I’m not guilty of anything there.”

Hutch became involved in crime at a young age in Dublin’s north inner city. By the time he was a young man, he had amassed 30 criminal convictions for ­offences including burglary, assault, larceny, car theft, joyriding and malicious damage. He was sentenced to two years for malicious damage in 1983, which was his last criminal conviction.

Hutch settled a tax bill for close to IR£2m with the Criminal Assets Bureau in the late 1990s, following garda accusations he was involved in two major armed robberies in 1987 and 1995. “They (Criminal Assets Bureau) like to pick a good target and a big name. And if you pick a good target and a big name and that big name settles, everyone else will be queuing up to settle. And that’s what they were at. They just wanted money, you know, and they got money.”

By 2012, Hutch was living in semi-retirement, mainly in Lanzarote. But a row between his nephew Gary Hutch and Daniel Kinahan in Spain resulted in him getting involved to try and sort the fallout.

Daniel Kinahan


“Daniel got in touch with me, he wanted to organise a meet [with Gary]. I told him, ‘No interest, not involved with you or your gang or any of this. I don’t want to know’. He pleaded with me to get involved, So I did. I got in touch with Gary and I got him back to Daniel and I organised a meet for both of them.”

Gerry Hutch travelled to mainland Spain and put his nephew and Kinahan together in a room, leaving them to sort out their issues. “They shook hands and were moving forward…So it was sorted. I was in the middle, mediating.”

Hutch thought the dispute was solved but months later, in September 2015, Gary was murdered in a double-cross by the cartel. Gerry Hutch knew immediately that Kinahan — who he describes as like “dealing with the devil” — was responsible.

“Daniel tells everyone it was the ­Russians and it was people from Manchester and so on. He tried to make contact with me and I didn’t have anything to do with him. When Gary got killed, a blind man could see who did it.”

The murder of Gary Hutch sparked the Hutch-Kinahan feud. By December of 2015, Kinahan had sent hitmen to Lanzarote to murder Gerry Hutch. They failed.

The Hutch gang retaliated against the Kinahans in February 2016. Gunmen stormed the Regency Hotel in Dublin to try and kill Daniel Kinahan at a boxing weigh-in. Cartel boss Kinahan escaped through a window but one of his lieutenants David Byrne was killed.

In retaliation three days later, Hutch’s brother, Eddie Hutch Sr, was shot dead. Gerry Hutch left Ireland shortly afterwards with a bounty on his head. He would remain on the run until 2021, when he was extradited from Spain to face a murder charge over the fatal shooting of David Byrne.

The late Tony Gregory and Charles Haughey posing together near the Five Lamps in Dublin in 1982
He was held on remand in Wheatfield Prison and one of his murder co-accused, Jonathan Dowdall, was housed on the same wing. The former Sinn Féin councillor was completing a sentence for torturing a man. Hutch said he became suspicious that Dowdall was providing evidence to the State when Dowdall got bail on the murder charge.

“He was at the end of a long stretch…He was on every type of drug he could get his hands on and he was complaining about this and complaining about that. He was finding it very difficult in there [prison],” Hutch said.

Dowdall did provide evidence for the State, claiming Hutch admitted involvement in the Regency Hotel shooting to him. But Dowdall’s testimony against Hutch at the Special Criminal Court was deemed unreliable.

Secret garda audio recordings between Hutch and Dowdall, as they travelled to Northern Ireland days after the Regency shooting, did not provide any evidence that The Monk was directly involved in the Regency murder, the court found.

​Branding Dowdall a “liar”, Hutch added: “There was no evidence. In fact, the audio evidence, it kind of proved it wasn’t me that done the murder.”

On the way to court from prison on the morning of the verdict, Hutch recalls feeling nervous. He doesn’t believe in God but he had “faith in a higher power” that he would be found not guilty. “I actually had a pain in my tummy on the way down to the court that morning. But I had my guardian angels over my head.”

Former Sinn Féin councillor Jonathan Dowdall


The Special Criminal Court found Gerry Hutch not guilty of the Regency murder of David Byrne.

The court said it was satisfied members of the Hutch family were responsible for the hotel murder, but there was no evidence linking Gerry Hutch to it.

There was evidence that he was in control of the three guns used in the murder days after the hotel shooting, and that he intended to “gift” these to dissidents in the North to get them to intervene and help end the feud.

Hutch declines to discuss this, just saying: “I’ve always been a mediator.”

He walked out of court a free man in April last year, having spent 20 months in prison.

“It was emotional. I walked out of court and went home. I actually went down to Seán McDermott Street on the way home and dropped off to a few houses around.”

He said he received a lot of letters of support from people while in prison awaiting trial.

“I remember opening some of the cards. When you’re in the cell on your own… there were tears, you know. It was really emotional and sad.”

This support has continued since his release and helped him make the decision to enter politics.

Comparisons have been made between Hutch and US president-elect Donald Trump. “Don’t go there,” he said. “There’s no comparison.”
