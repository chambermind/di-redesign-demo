Jonestown: Bringing the 917 home.

CultismJonestownUncategorized

It’s now been 47 years since Jim Jones and the mass murder-suicide of US citizens in Guyana, known as the Jonestown Massacre. In the years since, it may feel like every possible angle and analysis of Jonestown has been exhausted, and there’s nothing new to say, but does anybody know what happened to the bodies of the 917 victims in the days immediately after the tragedy?

This year is the 47th anniversary of the Jonestown Massacre, and on the very same date, it’s also the 37th anniversary of International Cult Awareness Day. It’s no coincidence that the latter shares the 18th of November with the event that’s seared into American and international consciousness as the moment the issue of cultism and thought reform was brought to global attention.

Even 47 years after Jonestown, the news film footage and photographs showing scenes on the ground the day after the massacre still beggar belief.

On November 19th, 1978, US television’s Channel 7 interrupted its normal broadcast with a special news report, and presenter Tom Van Amburg encouraged viewer discretion and described the horror of hardened newsmen upon seeing the scenes at Jonestown that had “shades of Auschwitz.”

US television media helicopters circle over a 3,800-acre agricultural settlement in Guyana. Strewn around the central structure are the bodies of 917 people. Multi-generational family members lie motionless in the long grass; some even have pillows beneath their heads, their arms draped over their loved ones, giving the impression to the hovering helicopter overhead of families napping in the afternoon sun.

Response teams first on the scene would find the same bodies supposedly in repose beginning to distend and bloat in the equatorial heat.

For many people, the story of Jonestown ends here in the Guayanese jungle with the discovery of the “murder-suicide.”

You’d also be forgiven for thinking that the 917 US citizens were buried in Guyana, as even the US government had initially planned for the bodies to go into a mass grave in the South American country.

The question of what happened to the bodies afterwards seemed to be of little significance compared to the incredulity of the act itself, as well as the fascination with the man who had presided over it.

But 917 victims (918 if you include Rev. Jim Jones in the final tally) had to go somewhere, and the “clean up” operation on the ground was proving difficult.

Erika Mailman for Rolling Stone writes:

For four or five days, it wasn’t even clear how many people had died. Those working to move the bodies would often find another layer of corpses underneath. Some of the handwritten cardboard tags identifying people, tied to their wrists, became illegible from rain. Although the U.S. government originally intended to bury the bodies in a mass grave in Guyana, “the Guyanese government wanted to get the bodies the heck out of there,” says Rebecca Moore. “It was an American problem dumped in their laps.”

The US government accepted it was an “American problem” and made the arrangements to bring the victims all together, as they’d been in Guyana, back to US soil, and to the unlikely location of Delaware.

However, Delaware was not the first choice:

“Most of the Peoples Temple members came from Northern California. The nearby Oakland Air Force Base — which had processed 50,000 bodies from the Vietnam War — would have been the common-sense choice to unite mourners and their dead. Moore recalls officials saying they didn’t want to be “besieged by relatives” and instead flew the bodies to Dover, Delaware,” writes Mailman.

Despite being miles away from the origins of the Peoples Temple and their families, the Atlantic-facing Dover Air Force Base in Delaware had the facilities to deal with a tragedy of such scale:

Dover Air Force Base’s port mortuary specializes in receiving the remains of U.S. troops who die overseas in the line of duty, honoring them through a dignified transfer before preparing them to be returned to their loved ones for burial.

In the same Delaware Online article, Annie Dawid, author of the fictional 2023 Jonestown novel Paradise Undone (also celebrating its second birthday on 18/11), points out:

Dover’s mortuary was uniquely equipped to handle an operation of this scale, she added. The base would be called upon for this reason in other national tragedies, including 9/11.

Within 24 hours after the massacre, the bodies had started to decompose to the point where identification was nearly impossible, and the response team on the ground in Guayana responsible for ‘cleaning up,’ had to use snow shovels to get some into bags for transportation.

People living in the vicinity of Dover were understandably concerned about the potential health hazards of receiving that number of bodies:

Residents were concerned the air base’s arrival of hundreds of decomposed bodies could pose a health risk. Those fears were shut down by Dr. Ernest S. Tierkel, then Delaware’s chief disease control officer, who explained the situation “may be unsavory, but it’s not unhealthy,” according to The News Journal archives.

Health risks would be contained and carefully managed, but it was impossible mimimize the scale of what was happening on the base, as daughter of the director of the air force base’s morgue recalls: “The whole base stunk” during the Jonestown aftermath, especially in the area where her dad worked, “which is why the mortuary now has top-of-the-line air filtering.” 

The operation from Jonestown to Dover required a total of 25 C-141s and 10 C-130s Air Force transport planes, plus three HH-53 helicopters, according to DAFB. Each casket wore the fragrance of death, a haunting scent that told of a so-called paradise that turned into a valley of sorrow. 

Dover resident Leroy Jubilee was an agricultural inspector for the Department of Homeland Security who remembers the “sickening smell” from working at DAFB.

His job was to make sure pilots, co-pilots and engineers followed customs regulations and didn’t bring over prohibited souvenirs from Guyana. Jubilee also said he inspected the planes.

“The worst incident of my whole career was this Jonestown incident,” Jubilee, 84, recalled. “It was a mess.” 

Just over half of the victims’ remains were retrieved by family members; some were never even identified.

Many members of the People’s Temple came from humble socio-economic backgrounds, and their surviving relatives were concerned about the cost of transporting bodies back west and the possibility of the US government making them foot the bill for transporting their loved ones back from Guyana.

There was even a rumour that People’s Temple members still loyal to Jones were after relatives and survivors.

Five months after Jonestown, on April 26, 1979, the first of the unclaimed bodies made their way back west. Buck Kamphausen, owner of Evergreen Cemetery, in a predominantly African-American neighbourhood in Oakland, California, offered to provide a final resting place for 410 unclaimed bodies:

“Evergreen was sitting in a black neighborhood, an old cemetery without much business in a deteriorated area. We saw that there was more to this story than meets the eye. We could improve the cemetery and provide services to people being discriminated against for being in a cult.”

They’d found a final resting place, and a single headstone with the words “In memory of the victims of the Jonestown tragedy” was the only thing that prevented the ground underneath from being a mass grave of anonymous individuals.

The people who attended the annual memorial service, as well as those who came to pay their respects throughout the rest of the year, knew that the single stone with its perfunctory, almost platitudinous epitaph wasn’t enough.

It wasn’t until 2011 that the Jonestown Memorial Fund raised enough money to have four large plaques engraved with the names of the 917 and laid in place.

One name, however, definitely should not have been included. Controversially, at the very bottom of one of the four flat stones is the name “James Warren Jones.”

The inclusion of Jim Jones’ name has understandably outraged many Jonestown survivors and the relatives of the victims, with some comparing it to including Hitler’s name on a Holocaust memorial, and there are multiple online petitions calling for Jones’s name to be removed.

In 2014, the cremated remains of nine Jonestown victims were found in an abandoned Delaware funeral home, finally reuniting one California resident with his deceased wife, thirty-five years after Jonestown. Four out of the nine were claimed for private burials, while the rest were interred with the others at the Evergreen Cemetery in Oakland.

Inkspot Publishing’s recent podcast featuring Annie Dawid, author of Paradise Undone: A novel of Jonestown, on the 47th anniversary of Jonestown can be watched here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuZqAzyf_4s

Dawid’s book provides a welcome break from the Jim-Jones-heavy analyses of what occurred in the Guyanese jungle and the years that led up to it, focusing instead on the altruistic, socially-conscious members of the People’s Temple, whose place in the ‘Jonestown story’ had long been one of mindless sycophants who walked willingly to their deaths, or at worst—were somehow deserving of their fate.
