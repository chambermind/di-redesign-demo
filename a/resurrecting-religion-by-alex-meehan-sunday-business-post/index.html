Resurrecting religion By Alex Meehan Sunday Business Post

Resurrecting religion
18 July 2010 By Alex Meehan

http://www.sbpost.ie/agenda/resurrecting-religion-50504.html

Resurrecting Religion

It’s 11am on Sunday morning, and in a large building just off the M50 motorway in Dublin, a religious service is about to begin. But this is a service with a difference.

For a start, the car park at the Victory Centre in Firhouse is full to overflowing, and finding parking proves difficult.

Out front, tables and chairs are spread on a patio set before a large water feature, while inside, immaculately dressed worshippers are wandering past a carvery-style restaurant, a shop selling Christian-themed books and DVDs, and a large coffee area complete with a banner proclaiming: ‘‘We proudly brew Starbucks coffee.”

Double doors open into the main auditorium, a space roughly the same size as a large cinema, with room to seat around 1,000 people. It’s a space that has been designed with acoustics in mind – the ceiling is shaped like an armadillo shell, and tiered seating drops away towards a stage.

By now, the auditorium is almost full and as the lights go down, a band launches into an upbeat Christian rock number and the crowd surges to its feet. As they sing along, lyrics flash up on three giant video screens.

The production values on display are astonishing for a Sunday morning religious service; there are successful rock bands that would kill for a set-up like this, complete with smoke machines, dynamic lighting rigs and full audio production.

The opening of the Victory Centre earlier this year marks the arrival in Ireland of a new approach to the promotion of religion, one that involves utilising business and marketing techniques more usually associated with the corporate world.

In the United States, it’s a phenomenon that has become known as pastor-preneurialism, and has become increasingly popular among evangelical Christians.

For the man who coined the phrase, pastor preneurialism is an idea of the moment. Dr John Jackson, a charismatic author and pastor from California who styles himself a ‘‘strategic leader for God-sized purposes’’, describes a pastor-preneur as someone who applies traditional business and marketing techniques to spreading the word of God.

With religion – namely Christian religion – seemingly under siege in the western world, Jackson came up with the term after observing the way small business owners had to become creative to keep their doors open when things got tough. ‘‘I wanted to help pastors be more entrepreneurial about how they spread the good news about love, and I wanted entrepreneurs who were believers to use their skills to help their church,” he says.

‘‘We’re seeing the not-for-profit sector increasingly being required to partner with their communities to help address problems with healthcare, crime and education, and we in the Christian community should be doing that too.

I strongly believe as a churchgoing person that if the message of God’s love is not practised seven days a week, then it’s really not worth that much.”

Jackson has encountered some hostility to the idea of mixing business strategy and religion from hardliners in the US who believe the world of commerce has nothing to teach the religious, but he feels that this stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of his message.

‘‘Some people have been critical of the term, but usually that’s because they think pastor-preneurialism is about making pastors rich, and that couldn’t be further from the truth. It’s about pastors being entrepreneurial in their approach to solving problems and working with their communities,” he says.

‘‘The problem is that this kind of thinking is rarely applied to the church.

The church sometimes presumes its right of existence, but a small business that thinks that way will soon go out of business.

One hour on Sunday is not going to transform an individual, a family, a community or a nation, and I believe that God wants us to transform all those dimensions of our world.

‘‘Whenever the church begins to focus inwards, problems arise.

When the church focuses up towards God and outwards towards the people, then it will thrive.

This idea applies to all churches, of all sizes and all backgrounds.”

Focusing outwards has been one of the main drivers of growth for the Victory Christian Fellowship, the group which runs the Victory Centre.

The fellowship was established by Brendan and Sheila Hade in their home in Dublin 22 years ago; initially, their meetings attracted just eight or ten people, mainly family and friends.

When numbers increased, the Hades rented first a room, and then a building on Westland Row in Dublin city centre.

Fifteen years ago, they bought the site in Firhouse which now accommodates the Victory Centre, the largest non-traditional Christian church in Ireland. Building work on the centre, which cost €12 million to complete, began two years ago and, according to pastor Mark Fennell of the Victory Centre, the project was funded entirely by donations.

‘‘It was paid for by the people who come here – one person might have given €2, another might have given €200,000,” he said.

‘‘We felt we had God’s backing for our old church, and so we were planning for its growth long before the growth came along.

When it happened, as it still is, we were in the right place at the right time.

We put together a business plan, and here we are.” While it’s primarily a place of worship, the centre is also a 6,000 square metre conference centre that is available for hire.

‘‘Obviously we won’t hire it out for purposes that contradict what the Bible stands for, but the Bible says to meet people where they are, and we have to move with the times,” says Fennell. ‘‘There’s no stained glass, statues or other religious icons here, so it can accommodate different groups.”

In addition to its conference facilities, the Victory Centre also has a theatre space, games rooms and creche. Despite its commercial nature, Fennell says the centre’s purpose is not to turn a profit, but rather to support the ideals of the church.

‘‘A business person would look at our activities and find lots of places we could make more money – most of our programmes are free to the public, even though we could charge for them. But our prime motivation isn’t money, and that frees us up to do things that a business couldn’t really justify,” he says.

‘‘We’re a registered charity, not a business.

Any donation that comes in quite obviously goes back into the church, and we’ve been financed by donations – every cent that has been made has gone back into the church.”

Like the majority of the Victory’s Centre’s Irish weekly attendants, Fennell was born and raised a Catholic. His parents got involved with the Victory Group when he was a teenager, and for awhile the family attended both Catholic and fellowship meetings.

‘‘We were practising Catholics, but a friend of the family invited my mum along one week, and she had a good gut reaction to it.

Next my dad went, and then I followed as a teenager.

For me, personally, I didn’t know what I was experiencing, but I knew that there was something good here,” he says.

‘‘The term ‘born again’ is used to describe what happens when God comes into your life: you get a fresh start. It doesn’t make you perfect, but it just means you’re committing to a new way of life.

For me, the proof of the pudding was in the eating. I came to the church, saw what it stood for, what the people committed to the church achieved, helping people who were alcoholics, drug addicts, who were suicidal and I saw that their lives were massively improved through association with the church.

They were blessed in their personal lives and in their working lives.”

At a time when mainstream churches in Ireland are finding it difficult to hang onto their congregations, groups such as the Victory Christian Fellowship are experiencing a boom in attendance figures, undoubtedly helped by the influx of immigrants into Ireland in recent years from Christian countries.

Meanwhile, non-mainstream church groups are using all the tools at their disposal to try to grow their flocks.

At the Whitewell Metropolitan Tabernacle Church in Belfast, Ian McAlees is the IT manager who oversees the church’s extensive online activities.

‘‘Our web activities are not the main driver of church growth by any means, but it is a popular way for people to interact with us,” he says.

Whitewell regularly attracts around 1,800 people to its weekly services, and boasts an audio and video system the equal of any commercial concert hall.

The church makes use of modern technology and marketing techniques to promote its ideologies and has its own outreach programmes, social media campaigns, weekly podcasts, TV shows and websites, where visitors can buy DVDs and MP3s of each week’s sermon.

In addition, the church broadcasts its Sunday services on a Sky television channel.

‘‘Religion and technology might not seem like an obvious match, but all this stuff is pretty normal now – it just seems strange when a church group does it.

But in the US, many churches do even more than we do,” says McAlees.

‘‘I see technology as a neutral thing – it can be used for good or bad, and right now it’s being used for all sorts of messages in the world with the exception of the message we think is the most important of all, God’s message.

We want to get that message out there and make it accessible to people to who might not otherwise hear it.”

‘‘We have a youth site – http://www.wellevent.com – which we’re using to test out some technologies and approaches to social networking with younger people through Facebook and Twitter.

We intend to roll that out within the next two or three months.”

McAlees also explains that the internet offers away for people to see inside the doors of the Whitewell church without having to actually attend a service.

‘‘It can take away some of the fear, allowing people to see and hear what goes on. If you’re a traditional church, people know what to expect, but when you’re not part of a big denomination then they may not.

We’re not doing anything mad or crazy, and this is away to show that,” he says.

Mark Fennell agrees that technology has an important role to play for religious groups seeking to reach young people in particular. ‘‘If Jesus was around today, he’d have a Twitter account and a Facebook page,” Fennell says.

‘‘He spent his life trying to spread a message of good news, so why wouldn’t he make use of the tools that are out there?

We work a lot with teenagers and young people, and the language that they speak and understand involves these tools.

We use our Facebook page to help filter questions and queries from our members, and also to keep people informed about what we’re up to.

The use of these technologies is now totally normal for every other part of society, but we expect church groups to be different.

Why?” Fennell says the Victory Centre is finding it easier to attract new worshippers. ‘‘We have a congregation of over 1,000 people now, with around 700 adults attending every week,” he says.

‘‘People are way more open minded now, they’re more willing to judge for themselves.

When people come in, they tell us afterwards that they felt welcome, that the atmosphere was homely and that they felt like they could wander in or out at any stage.

But they also tell us that they feel better for coming, so they come back.

Our Irish contingency is growing really quickly, but there is a broad spectrum of people worshipping here, from all over Europe, Africa and elsewhere.

Around half of the people coming here are from outside Ireland.”

Fennell believes that many people are disenchanted with what’s happening in Irish society and want away to express their belief in God in away that feels authentic to them.

‘‘Even people who aren’t necessarily looking for a church are coming to us, because they want to explore the idea that there’s more to life than is immediately obvious,” he says.

But while Ireland is undoubtedly becoming an increasingly secular state, with many people who were previously happy to describe themselves as Catholic reassessing their relationship with their faith in the aftermath of the Ryan and Murphy reports, it doesn’t automatically follow that people losing affiliation with the Catholic faith are turning to alternative religions, despite the increasingly sophisticated marketing techniques being used.

According to associate professor Tom Inglis of the Department of Sociology at UCD, an idiosyncratic feature of Irish life is that we are much less likely to change faith if we lose interest in the faith we were born into.

‘‘In Ireland, it seems to be the case that when Catholics become disenchanted and de-affiliated with the Catholic way of being spiritual, rather than switching faiths, they decide not to be spiritual at all,” he says.

‘‘Traditional Catholic Ireland was a deeply religious society, but the emphasis was on Catholicism rather than religion.

If we put all our eggs into the Catholic basket, and that basket starts to creak and fall apart, there doesn’t seem to be ability on behalf of most people to think of being religiously adventurous.

In the US, when Americans move to a new town they’ll shop around the various church groups in their area.

They’re much more willing to switch allegiance within their chosen faith.”

Inglis points out that in 1974, over 90 per cent of the Irish population was Catholic.

By the 2006 census, that had only fallen by 3 per cent, to 87 per cent.

Moreover, much of this decline can be related to the increase in mitgrants, a majority of whom were nonCatholics.

‘‘The 2006 census showed a significant increase in the number of people who stated that they had no religion, up 186,318, an increase of 35 per cent since 2002,but still less than 5 per cent of the population.

It is hard to decipher what exactly the category ‘no religion’ means. It could mean anything from not being a member of a church to being atheist or agnostic.

But the actual number of people who formally declared themselves as atheist or agnostic is still very small. In 2006, there were 929 declared atheists and 1,515 agnostics.”

Only a small number of people categorised themselves as belonging to alternative religions. ‘‘There were 1,691 Pantheists, up 50 per cent from 2002, but the only other category which would suggest membership of new religious movements was ‘other stated religions’, at 8,756,” says Inglis.

‘‘But this represented 0.2 per cent of the population, and was down from 8,920 in 2002.On the other hand, there have been significant increases in the number of Buddhists, up 67 per cent from 2002, and Hindus, up 96 per cent.”

With another census due to be taken next year, many of these figures could have changed significantly.

But Inglis is not convinced that there will be a surge in interest in new religions among disillusioned Catholics.

He suggests that many of them have opted to believe without belonging – to believe in God, but not belong to an institutional church.

‘‘While many people might say that they still believe in God, for my part I’m not sure they do. Instead, religion and faith have become more a matter of cultural identity than actual belief in God. People who have fallen away from the Catholic church tend to still see themselves as Catholics, as being different from Protestants, Jews or Muslims, but that doesn’t mean they actually believe in Catholicism,” Inglis says.

‘‘As an analogy, if I said I was a Manchester United supporter but I couldn’t name the team, hadn’t watched them play on TV let alone been to Old Trafford, and never bothered to looked up the results, could I really say I was a fan? God is not central to such people’s lives.”

Inglis believes there are four distinct types of Catholics in Ireland today – orthodox, cultural, creative and individualistic.

‘‘When it comes to life transitions – christening, deaths, marriages, births, first communions or confirmation – that’s when being a Catholic tends to take on meaning for most people who aren’t orthodox believers.

I think the degree to which this occurs is linked to the influence of Catholicism on education.

Once the church loses its grip on education, there will be a strong pocket of orthodox Catholics in Ireland, but their numbers will continue to diminish, and the number of cultural Catholics will grow,” he says.

‘‘There will also be a small number of creative Catholics, people who pick and choose which bits of the church’s teachings they believe.

The number of individualistic or disenchanted Catholics – people who used to be called lapsed Catholics when it was assumed that they’d return to the church – will grow in number as well.”

In 1997, Inglis attracted some criticism with his book Moral Monopoly: The Rise and Fall of the Catholic Church in Modern Ireland, which suggested that part of the reason why the Church was in decline was that it wasn’t willing to regionalise.

He believes the same criticisms can be levelled at it today.

‘‘I suggested that the Church is like a large multinational company which is interested in reproducing its power, but the problem is that it doesn’t allow local diversification, which all successful multinational companies do.

Every McDonald’s in the world essentially serves the same products, but they also have regional specials – a Teriyaki Burger in Tokyo or a Chicken Maharaja Mac in Mumbai – and that’s part of why it’s so popular,” he says.

‘‘From a corporate point of view, localisation is an important part of globalisation, and local difference is accommodated as part of globalisation.

But the Catholic Church hasn’t learned that. If you go back to movements such as charismatic renewal, ecumenicism or liberation theology – all were slowly strangled.

The deadening hand of Rome is preventing the green shoots of Irish Catholicism appearing.”

Inglis believes there is a need for new Catholic entrepreneurs.

‘‘When we talk about the economy, we talk about the need to feed it and get business growing again. The Church needs the same thing,” he says.

‘‘When people have new ideas, they should be fostered.

Until there is a return to a Gallican church as opposed to an ultramontane church – that is, until greater variety and freedom is tolerated in the church and greater variety of means of being Catholic appears, there isn’t much hope.”

According to Inglis, there is an unwillingness among Catholics to do anything different. ‘‘It doesn’t seem to occur to anyone to seize control of their own church – realistically, what would the chances be of a parish breaking away from the diocese and setting up an independent Catholic republic?”

‘‘There’s an analogous situation in Irish politics – we don’t get new political parties and we don’t get new churches forming, even though these things routinely happen in other countries.”

According to Veronica Jackson of Catholic Youth Care, the fact that Ireland has become a youth-oriented society presents particular challenges to religion in terms of how they make themselves relevant.

‘‘But it’s fantastic at the same time to see the way technology is allowing people to spread ideas quickly, reaching out to large numbers of people in seconds.

We organise a monthly Prayer in the City event; it’s organised via text message, and up to 50 people will show up for that reason,” she says.

She believes that much of the ritual and symbolism within the Church needs to be re-examined and re-explained in the face of a changing society. ‘‘People are very tired, and the scandals in the Church have undoubtedly dampened their spirits, but I think we need a resurgence of something that will bring hope,” she says.

‘‘There is concern in terms of the declining number of young people going to church, but things like gospel choirs and music ministries have really helped.

Younger people really get into singing as a group activity, and the more young adults can be active in the liturgy, the more involvement there will be.”

Peer-to-peer evangelisation is also important, according to Jackson. ‘‘Young people don’t really receive the message well from old fogeys trying to tell them about religion – they’re much more receptive to people their own age,” she says. ‘‘All you can do is plant the seed with younger people and hope that it develops into something. It’s not something you can measure.”

Mark Fennell believes that the Victory Centre will not be the only example of a large-scale non-traditional Church for long.

‘‘For us, the plan is to grow, and not to stagnate.

Other groups are planning on doing what we’ve done, and we’re happily working with them.

‘‘This is the first such church in Ireland, but it certainly won’t be the last.”
