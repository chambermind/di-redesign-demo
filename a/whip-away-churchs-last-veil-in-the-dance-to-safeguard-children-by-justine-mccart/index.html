Whip away church’s last veil in the dance to safeguard children By JUSTINE McCARTHY

Abuse by Church and Irish institutions

Sunday Times Dec 4, 2011

Justine McCarthy Article

Every time you dare think it’s safe to start trusting Catholic church officialdom again, you’re reminded what a gullible fool you are.

Take last Wednesday. One by one, six bishops published glowing reports on current child protection practices in their dioceses. The reports by the National Board for Safeguarding Children in the Catholic Church (NBSC) read like modern-day parables of the prodigal son. They said that, though “significant errors of judgment” had been made in the past, the bishops were on message at last and vigilantly patrolling the church’s crossroads to keep children safe. After two decades of clerical sexual-abuse scandals, today’s children are better protected than ever within the Catholic church. Moreover, they are safer there than within most other institutions; not least the nuclear family, protected as it is by a constitutional padlock. The level of danger in wider civil society is encapsulated in the reports’ statistic that fewer than 5% of allegedly abusive priests in the six dioceses have been convicted in the courts of the land. As Wednesday wore on, however, cynical episcopal PR began surfacing. A media meekened by the defamation of Fr Kevin Reynolds by RTE’s Prime Time Investigates was slow to ask why all six reports were being released at once, when each had been completed at different stages over the past 15 months. The report on Raphoe, where Bishop Philip Boyce decided not to laicise a convicted priest to save his family from trauma, was completed in August 2010. Could it be that the bishops hoped their individual diocesan transgressions would be spread thin in the avalanche? Exactly a week earlier, Pope Benedict accepted the resignation on health grounds of Séamus Hegarty, the bishop of Derry. He had been Raphoe’s bishop from 1982 to 1994, when Fr Eugene Greene, one of Ireland’s most insatiable child abusers, was prowling the Donegal diocese. On Wednesday night, television and radio bulletins led with predominantly positive coverage of the published reports. So did several newspapers the next morning.

Almost eclipsed was news that Cardinal Seán Brady, the Catholic primate, had made a confidential settlement with a victim of Brendan Smyth, the deceased Norbertine priest whose extradition to Northern Ireland contributed to the collapse of Albert Reynolds’s government in 1994. Brendan Boland, the plaintiff, was one of two teenagers whom Brady swore to secrecy in a canonical inquiry in 1975, long before Smyth’s rampage was halted by civic authorities. The revelation last year that Brady had required the boys to promise on oath to keep quiet prompted calls for the cardinal’s resignation. Boland has said that discovering Smyth had abused other
children after he took this vow was like being abused again. Intrinsic to Brady’s refusal to resign was his expressed intention to invite the NBSC to review safeguarding practices in his Armagh archdiocese. Oddly, Armagh was not among 0the six dioceses reviewed last week. Odder still, nobody is asking why. For a sector castigated as anti-church, the media is failing to ask a lot of pertinent questions.
Yet it parrots extraordinarily gymnastic spin passed down from the Vatican. When the Holy See announced the appointment of Monsignor Charles Brown as Ireland’s new papal nuncio recently, it was universally posited that his 17 years in the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith (CDF), which handles child sexual-abuse complaints, made Brown ideal for the job. Why? Is it because during his tenure the CDF ignored requests for documents by the Murphy commission for its investigations in Dublin and Cloyne?
Most egregious of all last Wednesday was the absence of survivors’ voices from the six reports. If today’s Catholic children are safer, it is thanks to older survivors who insisted the truth would out. Often, they were rewarded with adversarial lawyers representing bishops and congregations characterising them as opportunistic fortune-hunters. Wednesday’s reports were another hurt. Sterile records of diocesan procedures, they contained none of the details of abuse and its facilitation that we have grown accustomed to from the Ferns, Ryan and Murphy reports. Perversely, it was data protection legislation, designed to protect citizens’ privacy, that was invoked to gag the survivors’ stories. As the NBSC is a non-statutory body, it had to become a “nominated data processor” to each of the six bishops by means of a legal deed, in order to lawfully access the documentation it needed to conduct its reviews. A condition of this arrangement was that the NBSC was prohibited from sharing the information it got from the bishops with anyone else. Ergo, the reports were nobbled from the outset. None of this was even alluded to in the encomia that flowed from the government on Wednesday. Clearly, there is a cessation of the hostilities between the government and the Vatican, which kicked off when taoiseach Enda Kenny claimed in the Dail that “the rape and torture of children were downplayed or managed, to uphold the primacy of the [church]”. The Holy See recalled the then papal nuncio, Giuseppe Leanza, and later the government announced it was closing Ireland’s embassy in the Vatican. While Kenny’s Dail speech on the Cloyne report signalled a genuine shift in Irish-Vatican relations, other controversies arising from it have been sensationalist hot air. Instead of theological navel-gazing about the jurisdiction of the confession box, we should be debating the virtues of putting the NBSC on a statutory footing. Frances Fitzgerald, the minister for children, has responded to calls for another state inquiry by saying no decision will be made until the remaining NBSC reviews are published, knowing they will not be completed until 2014. Giving statutory status to the NBSC would empower it to disseminate information, to compel witnesses and documents, and to walk into bishops’ palaces and announce:
“We’re here to investigate.” Currently, it must wait politely to be invited. It is a cause for celebration that church safeguards for children have improved. Intense scrutiny over two decades has dragged the institution kicking and screaming to this point. If that scrutiny is relaxed, chances are it could lapse into its old ways. Securing the machinery of scrutiny must be the priority. That has to be the price of the summer-time dance of the seven veils by the government.
