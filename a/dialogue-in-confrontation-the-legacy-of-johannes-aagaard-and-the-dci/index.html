DIALOGUE IN CONFRONTATION: The Legacy of Johannes Aagaard and the DCI

Conferences & Commentaries

DIALOGUE IN CONFRONTATION: The Legacy of Johannes Aagaard and the Dialog Center International in the multireligious situation of today.

MIKE GARDE, MA, DIRECTOR DIALOGUE IRELAND TRUST, DUBLIN

Given on the occasion of the retirement of Rev Thomas Gandow in Berlin, August 27, 2011. This conference was held in conjunction with the Brandenburg-Schlesische Oberlausitz Liturg Church from which Thomas is retiring, the Cult Watching Group within the Lutheran Church, (KLB,) Dialog Center Berlin, and the DCI.

I first came into contact with the DC-DK in Wales when I met a team from Denmark at the World Student Christian Federation Conference, which took place in 1979 in Cardiff. They were under the leadership of Johannes Aagaard of Aarhus University. This was the start of a long relationship, and serves as the root of my growing engagement with and my study of the NRM phenomenon. Such is the world of my shaping, one that helped prepare me for what was to become my life’s work with its spiritual, professional and academic outcomes.

The DCI of course did not exist at this stage, but there was a fresh open and interesting approach to looking at the issue of New Religious Movements Cults and sects. I was used to the Conservative/ Liberal split in Theology and their methodology seemed to transcend this division. Johannes Aagaard was deeply committed to the Ecumenical understanding of Christianity and all traditions of Christianity felt at home with him. Of course if he had any doubts in this regard, which he did not he had his wife Anna Marie to prod him. She was heavily involved in the WCC. He loved singing the Hymn of St Francis and grounded all his experience in the theology and folk tradition of Grundvig the Danish Theologian. When we did form the DCI it was to espouse Classical Christianity. This was understood in the sense that we held to the credo in essentials unity, in non essentials diversity and in all things charity. However, with gradual opening up to the East that classical Christianity was understood in relation to the Ecumenical Creeds of the Church. He stressed at all stages that we work “with the Churches, but not under the Churches.” He like most of the members which made up the DCI accepted the positive benefits of the Church/State relationship. Obviously it was never as developed as the cult watch dogs of the German Church as the State Church did not fund this type of work in Denmark. Also the arrival of our Eastern European friends brought a post Communist search for identity with some advocating the symphony of classical Byzantium. Johannes Aagaard was at home in any part of the Christian world, or any part of the religious world period.

He developed a massively successful parish based organisation in Denmark, which did not manage to make the transfer to an internet and web presence. It was still caught up in the age of Gutenberg and costly published material. Its funeral was in April and all that is now left of his legacy in Denmark is the DCI. Our colleague Christian Szurko was highly successful in negotiating our ownership of the domain for the DCI. We also reconstituted the DCI in May of this year at the Fecris Conference in Warsaw. To date it is too early to say if the network founded by Johannes Aagaard will prosper or not?

Others of us though grateful for the funding that often made our meetings possible were less enamoured by the close connection between Church and State, remnants of Constantianism which in my humble opinion added to our woes.

We agreed on our task of confronting the cultist phenomenon which I call Cultism, but we will return to that issue later. We agreed that whatever we called them, Cult, Sect or NRM we had a common mission, and could disagree on issues of ecclesiology, Church State relations or Christian initiation but work together as a network.

Academic Base:

The Mission Theology Department in the University gave Johannes Aagaard a great foundation to both research and host activists from all over Europe. He was academic but not stuffy and was not living in an ivory tower but was rather a Renaissance man. My first memory of the December seminar in Aarhus was meeting Fritz Haack from Munich who was a great pioneer with Johannes Aagaard, Thomas Gandow and Rudiger Hauth of pioneering field visits to India and other locations. The three Germans made a visit to Ireland and they visited the Temple of Isis! However, Fritz knowing I was an Anabaptist said according to the Augsburg Confession I should be burnt at the stake. I said we would have to take Munster again before he could do that! There were no set lectures but we might have a person in a particular field bring their expertise to bear. We would start with brainstorming session and Johs would develop an agenda and we would share insights and make coherent various aspects of the cultist scene. This was interspersed with lunch at the Mensa for table fellowship and a highlight was an evening at Johs’s house. These events were life giving and a far cry from the empty lectures on cults that often drag on for an eternity. The reality for most of us is that we do not have time to think as we have to respond to the needs of our clients like the fire brigade. Here again Johannes Aagaard academic base was a very important dimension to our early success as he had time to research. There were also Conferences in summer and the invitation to fund those of us in the west and the emerging East to come to these events. He was a pioneer in regard to assisting Eastern European Christians. He used the resources of the Danish state, various foundations and the DC-DK to help us all to attend. This was a major aspect of our evolution and unfortunately ended when he retired. There was not just his academic base but all the resources and the attempt to create a Gurutek, a library especially dedicated to NRMs which had a very short life before the material was dispersed in the great big library.

The major theme of Johannes’s research was the Occult transformation of Western Culture.

He was especially an expert on the esoteric dimension of this phenomenon and explained this process as it unfolded. In this he would link groups like the Mormons, JW’s and Scientology in a seamless manner as part of this phenomenon. Expressions that were common were Consciousness rather than Conscience. He was the first Christian theologian I heard mentioning Kundalini. I remember at one of my early experiences was giving that lecture in Dublin in 1992, entitled, “The Occult transformation of the Western Culture,” and how he made the point that other academics did not even open the telephone directory to see what was going on around them. On radio and in the lecture room his ability to not come over as a stuffy Theology lecturer was amazing and he really made a connection with ordinary people who were unfamiliar with academic terms. He challenged the churches with his cry of what was their mission?

In my thesis I referenced this process as follows, “The secularisation and post-secularisation debates do nothing to lessen the impact of this process of socio-cultural and socio-religious change. Post-modern and New Age forms of spirituality are common today. All of the World Religions are present among us, variant forms of Christianity are emerging, and large numbers of NRMs have been identified. What we are seeing, then, is not just a process of secularisation; a process of de-traditionalisation and pre-rational romantic remythologisation is also in train. This means a return to the past that refuses a future perspective, and is basically conservative. The problem is not myth as such but how it should be conceived and approached, particularly in terms of religious language definition, symbolism and the question of normalcy and the processes of demythologisation and remythologisation with which myth confronts theology. Can we still speak of Christianity when the Christian basic core, the preaching of eternal salvation, is not shared anymore? Are we witnessing a decomposition of Christianity?

Johannes Aagaard had clear boundaries and yet a radical openness as a Christian. He showed openness to those like Eileen Barker whom he disagreed with. In 1998 Johannes Aagaard was back in Dublin and we hoped he would be able to debate with Eileen. She told me on the way in from the airport that the last time she had been in Dublin was in an acting capacity. Johannes was going to address the relativism at the heart of her value free Sociology. He just did not turn up mentally, and at that moment we began to get an insight into the medical problems which were to result in his death in 2007. This incisive and intelligent man was reduced to handing out old copies of a Dialog magazine and he fluffed his lines. Eileen as always chameleon like was able to weave her way around the audience. In the past Johannes Aagaard would have in a most loving way have examined and dissected her position. He expressed a loving Christian difference of opinion, but not the harsh rejection we have seen in recent years of other points of view. This can be seen in relationship to our enemies like the Scientologists and the group we now call cult fellow travellers. The DC-DK was infiltrated by Scientologists and Johannes Aagaard had numerous successful law cases against them. Some even tried to wrest the organisation from him and this left a scar on him. In my thesis I sought to clarify the terminology used to classify the types and categories of groups generally referred to as cults, sects, and new religions. There has been an explosion of terminology in recent years and this has led to a challenging lack of clarity in both scholarly work and the popular media.  The preferred term was Cultist NRMs. In stipulating this choice the work of Johannes Aagaard and Helle Meldgaard has been influential.

In their view,

“The terminology varies. Some call them religions; some call them expressions of Spirituality. We call them new religious movements, thereby simply indicating, that these phenomena are new in the sense of timing, even if they have older roots, religious in their pretensions and practices, movements since most of them have not – yet – settled down as regular religions and institutions. …The movements are not as new as we might think, but they are new in the sense that they are modern, for they are the children of modernism, even if they appear to promote ‘the old paths’. Their religiousness is often questioned by their efficient and secular approaches to power and finances…   The quality of movements of course depends on their ability to move.”

It is important to note, however, that the term cult, despite academic resistance, continues to play a vital part in the popular understanding of NRMs such that its meaning also needs to be clarified. We must retain the term for academic purposes as the arrival of so many terms has lead not to clarity but a proliferation of terminology. The term cult will be stipulated to refer to a tendency or mentality that can be observed in any organisation, rather than a particular form of group structure. So we refer to Cultist NRMs or we call this phenomenon Cultism.

Those of us who are professionals in our field are now reaching the end of our careers as Thomas Gandow does next week. We can’t hold onto the past so we begin to prepare for the future by finding ways to pass on our work to another generation. If we try to hold onto it ourselves it will shrivel up and dies, and are courting failure.

Some of us do not have that option as we are not Church workers and when we close the shop it is a permanent exit. Churches generally do not see Cultism as a major dimension of their work. As soon as someone retires from their job the State Church authorities often close down the post. This has been my experience in talking to cult watchdog personnel in Germany over recent years. How do we pass on our legacy to a new generation? So using the example of Ireland we have one example of how we are trying to address this. This is not to suggest it is right for anyone else, but we offer it as an example of a way forward and an application of the ideas of Johannes Aagaard. We will use our Mission Statement as a means of explaining this vision:

Motivated by the inalienable right to religious freedom, inspired by Christian values, Dialogue Ireland is an independent Trust that seeks to promote people’s freedom to make informed choices about religious, spiritual and philosophical beliefs. Though Dialogue Ireland has its origins as a Christian organisation it is now open to all belief systems and none. All we require is that those working with us share a common understanding of cultism.*

We fully accept the CHARTER OF FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS OF THE EUROPEAN UNION as giving expression to our work:

The role of Dialogue Ireland is:

* To promote the study and understanding of World Religions as the context for understanding Cultist New Religious Movements (NRMs). To provide pastoral support to members and former members of NRMs and their families according to their needs. Also to provide advice and information on NRMs to those affected and to the media.

* To provide information and educational services to schools, vocational groups and other establishments. We cooperate with governmental and police bodies using intelligence collected from our international partners, and our own research, related to cultism. Rather than warn or raise fears, our strategy is to inform and empower people that are making enquiries.

Introducing Dialogue Ireland Trust.

Ireland, like most European countries, has in recent decades been experiencing the presence of New Religious Movements (NRMs), sometimes referred to as “cults” or (in some continental countries) “sects”. The term NRM can be understood to include spiritual, as well as religious, movements properly so called. “New” may refer to movements that have been founded mainly over the past fifty years, though older movements are sometimes included. However, the term as Dialogue Ireland use it also includes centuries-old movements that are new to Ireland, or indeed to the entire western world. Over time, many young people and their families who had become involved with some of these groups turned to the churches for guidance and support.

Members of this committee established contact with the ecumenical Lutheran group, Dialogue Centre International in Denmark, which was already engaged in a similar ministry in Scandinavia. This link and the membership of the General Assembly of the European Federation of Centres for Research and Education on Sects (FECRIS http://www.fecris.org gives a connection to colleagues from Siberia in the East to ourselves in the West. Fecris represents the French tradition of secularity which tends to stress deeds not creeds, whereas the Dialogue Centres tend to say you can’t understand deeds without understanding the creeds.

Further development took place in 2001, when Dialogue Ireland, though continuing to be supported by the churches, became an independent Trust, thus giving it greater freedom in carrying out its mission. Mike Garde is now the Director of Dialogue Ireland and is supported by a strong executive committee. So DI Ireland will work with those from other creeds like Judaism, Hinduism, Islam or Buddhism which reject cultist tendencies. Also we are happy to cooperate with those who reject religion – sceptics, agnostics and atheists who share a common view of cultism. Freedom of the mind is our common platform for cooperation and we do not see this work as an opportunity to promote our own particular faith position.

We acknowledge that “cultist” tendencies, i.e. psychological and spiritual abuses, can creep into any organisation, including movements associated with our own churches or groups. We do retain the use of the term cult, but when we are asked if such and such a group is a cult we tend not to answer that as we try to get people to decide that for themselves. We do receive calls from people who perceive themselves or a family member as being victims of “cultist” activity from a wide variety of groups. These people turn to Dialogue Ireland for guidance and support. Such situations can be unbelievably painful for the people involved. Some have compared it to the death of a loved one without the funeral. Fortunately, the experiences of most people joining new movements are much less dramatic than this.

As its name implies, the starting point of Dialogue Ireland’s mission is to seek relationships of dialogue with New Religious Movements. This will necessitate the study both of the movements themselves and, where relevant, the world religions that may have given rise to them. We recognize that the some of these movements were set up with good intentions and acknowledge that in many instances we have much to learn from them. Also it is clear that the “seekers” who join these groups are genuine in their spiritual search. However, in the case of a number of movements dialogue has proven difficult to achieve. On occasion we have had to engage in dialogue-in-confrontation, or debate publicly with representatives of a group. Our reason for taking such a step arises from the conviction that the public has a right to know how people can be affected by the activities of a particular organisation. When we do reluctantly find it necessary to go public on a group, it is always the aim of Dialogue Ireland to speak the truth in a spirit of genuine love for the members and leaders of the movement in question, as well as for the families of everyone involved. We do feel it is important to give courage to those under the control of the group in question to address the leadership in a robust and confrontational manner. But we are clear that the wounded one’s in these groups need gentle support and when exiting from a group. We advise that until they have recovered sufficiently they not give any interviews to the media. However difficult things may get, some groups are open to change. However, most we deal with seem impervious to transformation and dominated by a high level of control.

So in the same way as hospitals were founded as Christian institutions they later became a community resource and passed into public ownership. In like manner organisations like DI which was founded as part of the culture of Christian apologetics is taking the step out of this context into a secular setting. Remember that long before I even met Johannes Aagaard, I had studied under Francis Schaeffer of L’Abri, Switzerland who gave Christians a wonderful insight into the modern world. He expressed the idea that to people without the bible you have to speak in their language as St Paul did at the Areopagus in Athens. We do not convert them to a Christian narrative but to Christ using the tools available in their discourse. That was why when I met Johannes Aagaard I recognised a similar understanding but even richer than what Francis Schaeffer represented.

So now those of us trying to hand on what we do to a new generation need to consider moving from an apologetic response to one that embraces the defence of the human rights of those we serve. Our very neutrality in regard to proselytisation strengthens our ability to assist those seeking our help. They then see we have no secret desire to recruit them to our particular world view. It is fun watching our Tibetan Buddhist Expert taking on a cultist Marian Pyramid Scheme as fiercely as another Catholic commentator on the comment section of our blog.

Key to this change is for the person retiring to let go and not trying to hold onto the golden goose. We have migrated on line and our website is the only source of information and archive in Ireland on NRM’s. http://www.dialogueireland.org

The blog is where we now engage with the world and there is new material going up on a daily basis. https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/

Elements of the strategy:

  1. As founder of Dialogue Ireland I was very concerned about succession. With this new strategy I am totally dispensable. I hope to work in the field until I am at least 75 but if I exit the stage the work goes on with an enthusiastic group of activists.
  2. We tried to migrate at first to an institutional setting but the Christian Church is in such decline we could not locate anywhere. Also that tended to delay the recognition that the future was not in buildings but on line. The academic route was Johannes Aagaard’s gift we can’t reproduce it now.
  3. Furthermore trying to maintain a Christian base meant a narrowing of effectiveness and a tendency to be talking to oneself.
  4. The cult wars also have migrated on line as we can see with the emergence of Anonymous and it happens one of those most gifted in understanding Scientology is also a member of Dialogue Ireland and our web master. If a dummy like me can post on the internet anyone can. I am ably assisted in this by his expertise.
  5. How we address a particular group is to create a category. Let us use the example of Rigpa a Tibetan Group. We receive requests for help from concerned people who are afraid for obvious reasons to be identified. We post material on line. In this case we have a Buddhist expert who produces the document. Then we have a massive response in the comment section and usually we work with the media and then an article is published or a TV programme is aired. We link to the article and the process of public awareness increases. Usually at this stage we get a legal challenge to take down materials. It is a given in this work that one will be threatened by groups who use the law not to protect human rights but to protect themselves from scrutiny. In 2009 we were attacked by massive assault on our web site and we had to leave two servers as a consequence. We are pretty sure we know the source of the attack but do not have enough evidence to bring it to court. The consequence of this was that we received assistance and we have now made the web site a kind of archive. We have the complete content of the DCI web site archived there. However, we have concentrated our efforts on a WordPress blog site. There nearly costs us nothing and I will be long since dead when we use up capacity on the site. Though we have to deal with claims of libel we have a €1.3m Indemnity insurance policy to assist with these threats. However, there is feature of the WordPress system which commends itself. Though we obviously can’t defame anyone in any jurisdiction. When someone asks us to take down something and they can’t show any evidence that it is not true, they write to us and ask us to take down material from our web site but on the blog. Well we don’t have anything on our web site other than the archive! Also if they don’t take up the offer to respond or to make a post without any censorship they have a difficulty if they take it to WordPress as it is based in the USA which has a lot more freedoms in this regard.
  6.  We have observed how the historical memory represented by Johannes Aagaard has generally been lost with his death in 2007. However we are trying to scan and reproduce as much of it as we can. DC-DK was not able to make the transition to the new context. The Survival Trust in the UK which is a not religious foundation is facing the same issue now. How will the German Church respond to this critical issue with the loss of a cohort of professionals like Thomas Gandow who carry a 32 year irreplaceable memory and experience? I was amazed at the resource found in the great work put into the Hand Book on Religion which we heard about just before this presentation. This a great resource and is the nearest to the Gordon Melton Encyclopaedia of Religion. Would we bring out an on line version and use the network to bring it into a variety of languages?
  7. How do we continue our work in a context of financial restraint? Those of you who work for the state or the church are in well paid public servants with offices, resources, buildings and secretarial assistance. Relative to the rest of society you may be finding a cut in your salaries, but you have a secure future with a pension. Whereas for the bulk of us we are like subsistence farmers scraping an existence off the land. We have no offices or if we do it is nearly impossible to keep them open. Unlike in commercial ventures where the more hits you get the more money comes in, in the helping services and in work addressed to the public good the more hits the more work flows our way and less cash comes in. Most of are self employed and as I said earlier my best hope in the future for income is when I get the state pension, but my intention is to work till I am 75. I would have done so in any case now it is an essential. Following the example of Johannes Aagaard I suggest like Paul who made a collection for the poor in Jerusalem we need to consider a Jubilee Fund where those who are relatively well off assist those struggling at the periphery. I know I can speak for many as one who came to the Conference in 2009 here in Berlin that the support of bringing us here gives us an opportunity to share with those involved here in Germany. We were looking forward to a meeting of the DCI here in Berlin following our successful restoration in Warsaw. Many of us have joined Fecris as we feel we do not have to choose between deeds and creeds but can work with both. We need to use every international occasion which we can to hold our DCI network meetings. The next one is likely to be in May next year. In a world where we are very busy it is essential we take every opportunity to share in the network.

In Conclusion I have outlined one application of how I see the influence of Johannes Aagaard in the development of Dialogue Ireland. It is not a pattern but a lens to look at the vision. We have moved from an explicitly Christian understanding of our mission to one that is non sectarian, non religious and yet is open to all of good will whether people of faith or not. The two pillars of that vision are currently and historically FECRIS and DCI. Today will determine whether these two pillars are together for DI or whether one will stand alone.
