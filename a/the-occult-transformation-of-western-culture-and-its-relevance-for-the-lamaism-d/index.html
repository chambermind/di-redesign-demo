He was especially an expert on the esoteric dimension of this phenomenon and explained this process as it unfolded. In this he would link groups like the Mormons, JW’s and Scientology in a seamless manner as part of this phenomenon. Expressions that were common were Consciousness rather than Conscience. He was the first Christian theologian I heard mentioning Kundalini. I remember at one of my early experiences was giving that lecture in Dublin in 1992, entitled, “The Occult transformation of the Western Culture,” and how he made the point that other academics did not even open the telephone directory to see what was going on around them. On radio and in the lecture room his ability to not come over as a stuffy Theology lecturer was amazing and he really made a connection with ordinary people who were unfamiliar with academic terms. He challenged the churches with his cry of what was their mission?

In my thesis I referenced this process as follows, “The secularisation and post-secularisation debates do nothing to lessen the impact of this process of socio-cultural and socio-religious change. Post-modern and New Age forms of spirituality are common today. All of the World Religions are present among us, variant forms of Christianity are emerging, and large numbers of NRMs have been identified. What we are seeing, then, is not just a process of secularisation; a process of de-traditionalisation and pre-rational romantic remythologisation is also in train. This means a return to the past that refuses a future perspective, and is basically conservative. The problem is not myth as such but how it should be conceived and approached, particularly in terms of religious language definition, symbolism and the question of normalcy and the processes of demythologisation and remythologisation with which myth confronts theology. Can we still speak of Christianity when the Christian basic core, the preaching of eternal salvation, is not shared anymore? Are we witnessing a decomposition of Christianity?

Johannes Aagaard had clear boundaries and yet a radical openness as a Christian. He showed openness to those like Eileen Barker whom he disagreed with. In 1998 Johannes Aagaard was back in Dublin and we hoped he would be able to debate with Eileen. She told me on the way in from the airport that the last time she had been in Dublin was in an acting capacity. Johannes was going to address the relativism at the heart of her value free Sociology. He just did not turn up mentally, and at that moment we began to get an insight into the medical problems which were to result in his death in 2007. This incisive and intelligent man was reduced to handing out old copies of a Dialog magazine and he fluffed his lines. Eileen as always chameleon like was able to weave her way around the audience. In the past Johannes Aagaard would have in a most loving way have examined and dissected her position. He expressed a loving Christian difference of opinion, but not the harsh rejection we have seen in recent years of other points of view. This can be seen in relationship to our enemies like the Scientologists and the group we now call cult fellow travellers. The DC-DK was infiltrated by Scientologists and Johannes Aagaard had numerous successful law cases against them. Some even tried to wrest the organisation from him and this left a scar on him. In my thesis I sought to clarify the terminology used to classify the types and categories of groups generally referred to as cults, sects, and new religions. There has been an explosion of terminology in recent years and this has led to a challenging lack of clarity in both scholarly work and the popular media.  The preferred term was Cultist NRMs. In stipulating this choice the work of Johannes Aagaard and Helle Meldgaard has been influential.

In their view,

“The terminology varies. Some call them religions; some call them expressions of Spirituality. We call them new religious movements, thereby simply indicating, that these phenomena are new in the sense of timing, even if they have older roots, religious in their pretensions and practices, movements since most of them have not – yet – settled down as regular religions and institutions. …The movements are not as new as we might think, but they are new in the sense that they are modern, for they are the children of modernism, even if they appear to promote ‘the old paths’. Their religiousness is often questioned by their efficient and secular approaches to power and finances…   The quality of movements of course depends on their ability to move.”

It is important to note, however, that the term cult, despite academic resistance, continues to play a vital part in the popular understanding of NRMs such that its meaning also needs to be clarified. We must retain the term for academic purposes as the arrival of so many terms has lead not to clarity but a proliferation of terminology. The term cult will be stipulated to refer to a tendency or mentality that can be observed in any organisation, rather than a particular form of group structure. So we refer to Cultist NRMs or we call this phenomenon Cultism.
