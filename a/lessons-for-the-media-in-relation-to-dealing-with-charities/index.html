Lessons for the media in relation to dealing with Charities

Conferences & Commentaries

Sunday Times journalist Stephen Price really gets to the point of the dilemma facing newspapers in a dwindling market for hard copies. He writes eruditely on this subject quite regularly. We in Dialogue Ireland are finding as a charity dedicated to archiving material which can assist the victims of cults an increasing problem of the main media treating this blog as if it was a commercial threat? We were able to get TV programmes and upload them onto YouTube but now we are faced with them being taken down for copyright reasons. Great programmes produced at great expense which were available for a day or on a player are lost for ever. The same is happening with the newspapers. Where as we could get a pdf of a story from a journalist we had assisted in writing a story we now find they themselves can’t even get it!

This article below illustrates this dilemma and the contradictions of a helping group being in trouble for reproducing material of relevance to battered women and children. Equally as a charity that assists the victims of cults it is hardly the answer to not allow us material which can assist our readers.

It was the American politician Hubert Humphrey, once regarded as the greatest president that country never had, who posited that “the right to be heard does not automatically include the right to be taken seriously”. If Humphrey’s wisdom could be applied to one lobby group in recent weeks, it would be the National Newspapers of Ireland (NNI), the umbrella body for Irish newspapers where members “put aside their individual competitive agendas and draw on the strength of acting as a group to inform the public of the power of the press”.

One really has to wonder about that strength because, up until now, few outside the industry have heard of the NNI. It has made the odd grumble about RTE’s website, but mainly it satisfies itself with doling out plaudits and awards. On really serious issues, such as the plurality of media ownership, the NNI remains silent. Now a company connected to the NNI has achieved sudden notoriety over its pursuit of the charity Women’s Aid for publishing hyperlinks, the basic mechanism of connecting one web page to another. As many charities do, Women’s Aid had posted Links In positive press stories on its website, and suddenly it began to receive letters, phone calls and emails from a company called Newspaper Licensing Ireland (NLI) demanding payment. The NLI extracts copyright fees from other companies which redistribute items from Ireland’s newspapers, typically PR finns or media monitoring agencies. On its website, the NNI directs anyone concerned about copyright issues to the NLI with a hugely helpful hyperlink. Naturally, Women’s Aid forwarded the NLI’s demands to a solicitor. Because if content is posted on the internet for free, it seems odd to charge anyone for linking into it. It’s like a shop owner handing out free sweets, then sending customers an invoice. Now if those customers stole the sweets, that would be a different matter, and both the NNI and the NLI would be right to pursue anyone reproducing newspaper content for commercial gain without permission. However, most newspapers leave themselves wide open to ambiguity and exploitation by failing to put their online content behind a paywall, their commercial strategy being to reap advertising revenue from people clicking through on, what else, hyperlinks. That strategy isn’t working. Banner and pop-up advertising earn relatively little, while newspapers forego their cover price or subscription fee for no real gain by posting content free. In America, most national titles are -erecting porous paywalls, which give a limited amount of content away and charge for the rest. News International, which owns this newsaper, uses paywalls. This newspaper is also an NNI member. As an industry, newspapers have collectively made a hames of their approach to the internet, but pursuing charities that help battered women and children is hardly the answer.

The NNI has published a justification in the form of a press release so verbose and convoluted it would drive any self-respecting editor to violence. This failure of communication is all the more puzzling given that the co-ordinating director of the NNI is one Frank Cullen, who is also chief executive officer of NLI.
