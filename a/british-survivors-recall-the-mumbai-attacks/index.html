British survivors recall the Mumbai attacks

Islamism

British survivors recall the Mumbai attacks

From The Sunday Times

November 1, 2009

Interviews by Anmar Frangoul and Caroline Scott

http://www.timesonline.co.uk/tol/news/world/asia/article6896098.ece

Will Pike, 29

Will, a film-maker from London, and his girlfriend, Kelly Doyle, stopped for a night in the Taj Mahal hotel on their way home to the UK. Will fell escaping from their room, and is now paralysed from the waist down

“We were stuck in our room listening to gunfire for five hours. The most horrific point was when the phone in the bathroom rang, about 2½ hours in. I answered it and a voice said, ‘Don’t leave the room.’ I put down the phone and thought, ‘That’s it. They know where we are.’ Moments later we began hearing single gunshots as if gunmen were going into every room executing people. I was crouched behind the door with a cutlery knife ready to stab someone in the neck. It seems abstract now, but at the time it seemed like a plausible option. But the shots stopped and there were more explosions. After three hours, I got a call from my brother in England telling me there was a fire two floors above. Smoke was coming under the door. That was when we started tying sheets and curtains together. We felt if we didn’t try to get out, we’d die of smoke inhalation.

One of the bomb blasts had shattered an outer pane, so when I flung a marble table at the window, it went straight through. We threw the ropes over, I told Kelly I loved her, we hugged and went for it. Soon after, the whole setup loosened and I fell, landing on my back, just above my arse. A guy called Iman found me. He was my saviour. He bundled me into an ambulance and flagged down a fire engine to rescue Kelly. He then took her across town and paid for some blood so I could have an operation.

My surgery lasted 10 hours. I’d broken both arms, shattered my pelvis and crushed my spinal cord. Afterwards I was completely helpless — spoon-fed, turned every few hours. But it wasn’t until I had a meeting with my hospital consultant two months later that I fully understood that the paralysis was for life. Until then I’d been upbeat. That was my Ground Zero.

Every aspect of my past and future went through my mind. I cried every day. My spinal injury affected everything — my identity, what I brought to my relationship with Kelly. Coming to terms with that has been far harder than the ordeal. The Red Cross gave us £15,000 from its emergency relief fund, but the Criminal Injuries Compensation scheme doesn’t cover tourists caught up in terrorist attacks. Neither the hotel group nor the British or Indian governments seem liable for compensation and we’re still fighting the case. I was wheeling myself round Tesco the other day, and a guy on a mobility scooter winked at me. I realised I’ve inadvertently become a member of a club I don’t want to belong to. Kelly doesn’t let me get away with much. It’s like, ‘Don’t give me that wheelchair shit. Just get on with it.’ But the loss is huge. It’s hard to explain, but it’s as if my heart is broken. I’m having to let go of the me I was when I had legs that worked.”

Joey Jeetun, 32

An actor from London, Joey was in Leopold Café when the terrorists struck. Joey, who once played the role of a suicide bomber, was treated as a suspect

“The café was packed that night. At around 9.20pm, I heard what I thought was a light bulb exploding. Then someone pushed me to the floor and told me to stay quiet. Tables and chairs flipped over and people started to scream as shots rang out. I covered my face and lay down on the floor. After about five minutes the shooting stopped, and it was chaos. I was drenched in other people’s blood. A man next to me had a hole in his head. I tried to wake him up, but he was gone. There were dead bodies everywhere.

About 15 minutes later the police arrived and I felt so relieved. But two officers took me to one side and put a gun to my head. I was dragged off to the police station and held as a suspect for 12 hours. I kept telling them that I was a UK citizen and a victim, but they wouldn’t listen. There were some Arabic guys, also being held as suspects, angrily pleading their innocence, but the police seemed more interested in watching events unfold on TV than putting a stop to it all. At 7am they released me without charge or an explanation. I left India the day after. I’ll never forget Mumbai. But I think I’ve dealt with the situation pretty well: the nightmares have stopped and I’ve only had to have one session of counselling. I’m an optimist and I really believe I’ve had my share of sorrow for a lifetime in one go. I’ve got it out the way and from now on things are only going to get better.”

Sajjad Karim, 36

A Tory member of the European Parliament for North West England, Sajjad was staying at the Taj Mahal hotel in Mumbai as part of an official delegation

“My party of six MEPs had travelled to Mumbai to progress the EU/India free-trade negotiations, but an hour after we arrived at the Taj Mahal hotel, all hell broke loose. I was so tired I had decided to get an early night, and then did something I never do: I unpacked my passport and put it on the bedside table. Then I went down to the lobby to tell my colleagues I wouldn’t be joining them for dinner. I was stood at the main entrance when I heard popping noises from a side street. Then a chap ran in carrying a woman who had blood pouring down her leg, followed by about 30 others, screaming, ‘They’ve got guns!’

Everyone in the lobby was running away from the front doors, away from danger. There were about 15 people in front of me. But as we reached the back wall, a man with a machinegun appeared in front of us. He was about 22, clean-shaven, dark-skinned. And he began shooting. I remember looking in his eyes and thinking: ‘Oh my God, he doesn’t care that he is killing these people.’ There was a smile on his face as those in front of me — men women and children — started to fall.

I didn’t turn round because I knew, without looking, that people were being shot behind me too. By this time the lobby was a scene of carnage. The only possible escape was to my right, which meant I had to turn my back on this gunman, while people around me were still falling. As I turned and ran, I remember thinking: ‘This is it, he’s going to shoot me in the back.’ But something inside me took over. If I’d panicked

I would have been dead. As I turned the corner, I ran, and I didn’t stop running until I reached the kitchens. The diners didn’t know anything was wrong. It was surreal. We closed the wooden doors of the Zodiac restaurant and barricaded ourselves inside, and we just stayed there, listening to the gunfire and the screaming getting closer. It was really bad. Grown men on the phone to their wives, crying. The restaurant next door had glass doors and the gunmen blasted through them; I remember them rattling ours at least three times. Sky and BBC news began running stories, with some reports mentioning me. I could see people looking at me and muttering, ‘Is it him?’ I took myself into a corner and disappeared, because I really didn’t know what they’d do. When we were finally led out, seven hours after the siege began, I walked through trails of blood where bodies had been dragged away. The terrorists had been into every room on the third floor, looking for British citizens, shooting and taking people hostage. My passport was next to my bed. Had I gone to sleep instead of walking down to the lobby, I would almost certainly have been shot dead.”

Deepak Kuntawala, 37

A businessman from London, Deepak was staying at the Taj Mahal hotel with his elderly father at the time of the attacks

“I had taken my father to Mumbai to divert his attention from the anniversary of my mother’s death, and it was our last night in the Taj. We had a few hours to kill before our flight, so we went to the hotel’s Sea Lounge. It was a beautiful evening: the sky was dark orange, a piano was playing in the distance. I kept casting my eye to the harbour, and then I saw a dinghy carrying six or seven guys, all bunched tightly together. Minutes later we heard a loud boom. The booms and cracks got closer and closer, and people began to head back to their rooms. Then all of a sudden everyone rushed back into the room we were in, as if they were running away from something awful. I began looking for exits, but our only way out was where the shots were coming from. We ended up in the banqueting hall next door with about 150 other people. We barricaded the room with anything we could find — tables, chairs, sofas — and then lay flat on the floor, trying to keep as quiet as possible.

By 3am, bombs were going off at regular intervals, machineguns were being fired, and smoke was sweeping into the room. We didn’t have much time before the terrorists stormed in or we were engulfed by flames. I asked a few guys to follow me, and we ripped some curtains down and tied them together, smashed the windows and got people out as quick as we could. My father lost his grip and fell about 11 metres, but miraculously only injured his leg. I got out not long after.

I became ill after the attacks. I think the stress triggered Sarcoidosis, where the body attacks itself from the inside out. It took me until May to recover. The terrorists didn’t win. They set out to kill 5,000 and destabilise the world, but they didn’t succeed. I’ve just been back to Mumbai, and I stayed in the Taj. Despite the awful memories, it was amazing to be back.”

Harnish Patel, 30

A chartered surveyor from Hampshire, Harnish was in Leopold Café when the terrorists burst in and began firing

“I’ll always remember the look on the barman’s face. He was pouring a glass of beer, there was a crashing sound — as if someone had dropped a tray of drinks — and he suddenly froze. Then all hell broke loose. They started firing, glass smashed everywhere and people pulled tables and chairs down, trying to shield themselves. I hit the deck and just tried to stay still.

Out of the corner of my eye I could see young guys with machineguns, shooting anything that moved. Something struck my leg. I curled up into a ball and stayed still. After a while the shooting stopped. I could hear a woman screaming and people moaning in pain. It was a blood bath. The floor was covered in thick red puddles, and to my left I could see two faces that had been split open by the gunfire. People began to remove the dead and wounded. I was put into an ambulance, with two bodies, and they had been shot in the face.

It was pandemonium at the hospital. I’d been shot in the chest and left leg, and my right thigh had been ripped in two. I was operated on and I flew back to the UK a few days later. I was laid up in hospital for a long while after that, but by March I was walking again. I was a keen runner before the attacks, and in April I hit the road for the first time since. I hope to run a marathon soon.

As a child my dad would tell me that I rushed into things, that I needed to learn to walk before I ran. It’s taken me all that happened in Mumbai to realise just how right he was.”
