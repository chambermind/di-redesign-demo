COMMENT APRIL 25 • 2010 Irish Mail on Sunday Editorial:Bad law

Icross Charity

COMMENT APRIL 25 • 2010

Irish Mail on Sunday  Editorial:

Meegan Mail on Sunday editorial

Bad law kept the public in the dark about this charity

FOR more than five years now, Irish newspapers have been investigating the activities of Michael Meegan, who runs the ICROSS charity in Kenya. During that period, ICROSS has received millions of euro in donations from the public, plus hundreds of thousands of euro in taxpayers’ cash through grants from state agencies here and abroad. Yet throughout that time, none of the most serious allegations against Mr Meegan had been publicised – until this week. How can this be, given how vital it is that charity donors know their money is going to the right place? It’s because our highly restrictive libel laws mean most newspapers simply cannot afford to take a case to court. If they lose, the damages and costs can be astronomical; even if they win, they may never recover the cash they spent bringing the case. So for years, people carried on giving money to ICROSS with no idea of the serious question marks over Mr Meegan’s conduct. When Mr Meegan began to threaten this  newspaper with a High Court injunction, there were sound commercial reasons for giving in. There was no guarantee that we would win our case – and it would unquestionably cost us tens of thousands of euro to defend our position. We had to secure sworn affidavits from around the world, from Dublin to London and Africa. Meanwhile, ICROSS went to the local police and filed criminal complaints against our witnesses, with the stated intention of seeing them arrested and charged with criminal libel. We had to engage senior counsel and spend days in court trying to secure a hearing, all the while responding to fresh claims being made by Mr Meegan’s legal team – claims that our witnesses were liars, that they were paid to give evidence, that they are part of a conspiracy against him borne of jealousy or even unrequited passion. Eventually, thankfully, we won. The president of the High Court ruled that there was no basis for an injunction against us. Moreover, he ruled that he had been misled in court by Mr Meegan’s barrister and awarded us our costs in full. Mr Meegan now says he has no money – something he never volunteered when he was instructing solicitors and a barrister to silence us. We may never get our costs back, although we do feel Mr Meegan has an obligation to meet his debts. More important than that, however, is that the public know exactly what happened this week: that a man who has taken their money for charity attempted to gag his critics. In future, when Mr Meegan appeals for donations – perhaps to his new charity, New World International – people will be able to see the full picture before they hand over their cash. (We can only hope RTÉ, which lionised Mr Meegan in a 2005 documentary, will now pick up the tale – having chosen not to report Monday’s case). The real morals are twofold. First, charities need to be as tightly regulated as any bank; and second, the rich and powerful should not be allowed to use the law to stop the public from hearing the truth. Last Monday’s decision was a step towards both those goals.
