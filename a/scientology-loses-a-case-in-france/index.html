21 December 1978

Scientology Loses A Case In France

Scientology

Four leaders found guilty of fraud by A.S.

Four representatives of the Church of Scientology have been tried in a Paris court and found guilty in February 1978 of fraud. In a case which was in effect a trial of the Church’s good faith only one of the defendants was present to hear the verdict. The three others, who included two former French Church officials and L. Ron Hubbard himself, did not appear to defend themselves.

The case was brought by the prosecutor of the Republic and two civil parties, the now well-known Association pour la Defense de Famille et de l’Individuelle (ADFI) and an ex-scientologist named Tran Lgoc Lan. The Association finds its membership largely among relatives of youth who have joined various sects; it has exhibited growing strength in its attacks on the methods employed by some sects, particularly the Moonists. M. Tran, a teacher who had become involved with the Church out of loneliness, claimed to have been misled and mistreated.

The charge was fraud, and read as follows (in unofficial translation from the court record):
“That between 1967 and 8th 1976 (the four accused), employing fraudulent manoeuvres to persuade (them) of the existence of false enterprises or of an imaginary power or credit, or to arouse the hope of some success or other visionary event, to wit:

by representing itself (under the cover of registration as an association) as a church, the true object of which is uniquely philosophical and religious, while this organisation functions as an organized psycho­therapeutic system and cloaks a well-managed and growing commercial enterprise, which distributes abundant advertising in the form of tracts, prospectuses, oral propaganda, etc;

by carrying out false personality tests, administered by unqualified personnel, using under conditions that do not conform to scientific standards an apparatus which has been long in existence to take measurements long known, but representing this apparatus as copies of a prototype recently invented by the “founder of the religion”, and dressing it up with a religious regulation;

by exerting through these means an intellectual and moral pressure on persons attracted by the hope of an improved personal equilibrium, greater professional success and “happiness” in general, and by persuading these persons that the aims sought for could only be attained by an initiation of a religion, character concretized in courses of study, services or books or documents dispensed or delivered at high prices, the intrinsic value of these courses, services or books being without relation to the prices demanded;

did obtain from numerous persons the delivery of large sums of money, defrauding by these means such persons, of all or part of their fortunes.”

Hearings took place the 7-9th and 14-16th November 1977 and judgment was given the 14th February 1978. The ADFI demanded damages of one franc and the publication of the verdict in various journals, as did M. Tram. Witnesses were heard on both sides, documentation was adduced as evidence and a court-appointed firm of auditors provided information on the financial situation and dealings of the Church. (Accounts were found to be meticulously kept). Psychologists were also present as expert witnesses.

M. Georges Andreu in court pleaded not guilty, mainly on the basis, it seems, that he did not personally profit from the business. A second defendant, M.Laarhuis, had appeared before the examining magistrate and had given evidence to the appointed auditors but had disappeared before the court hearings opened. The other two defendants had not appeared at all. The court was nevertheless irritated by the fact that in ignorance or defiance of French practice they sent letters and telegrams “of all sorts” during the hearings and submitted arguments in their names through M. Andreu.

Evidence revealed a number of interesting facts:

  1. The French church, which had registered as an independent religious “association” (and had undergone a number of changes of name through the years) was not in fact independent. It was a subsidiary of the Church of Scientology of California; it was obliged to remit 10% of its income to the “mother church” in England and its activities were subject to control by the “Action Chief Europe” who was located on the yacht Athena, the floating headquarters of L. Ron Hubbard and the world organization. The very effective control exerted by this headquarters on all adherents and the use of efficient procedures served “one of the essential goals” of the orga­nization, to bring in “as much money as possible”, very efficiently.
  2. The French Church, although it used the term “surplus” instead of “profits” and attempted to avoid taxation by various devices, could not in truth claim to be “an association without financial goals”. It was a commercial enterprise. A 1965 order from Hubbard had stated that “remittance of funds is essential”. Course fees (which provided 90% of income) had to be paid in advance and some students had made loans to pay them. The use of the term “donation” rather than “fee” in most advertising materials served two purposes: payments did not then appear as revenues in the books, and on the other hand they could not be reclaimed (nevertheless in a few cases refunds had been made under pressure). The question of whether or not Scientology as such was a religion was not before this court, although it seemed doubtful to the court.
  3. Both deception and psychological pressure was practiced to recruit and keep adherents, which recruitment and holding activity was necessary to bring in funds. Promises were made by the officials of Scientology of professional success, which witnesses for the defense stated had proved true in certain cases. Officials had also promised “material success” and the healing of psychic or psychomatic maladies; such healing was never proved, “rather the contrary”. Promises had even been made that somatic illnesses would be healed including burns.

Psychological pressure was exerted on adherents by various means. A very strict system of discipline was in practice, and adherents were subject to punishment according to a system of which they did not know the details. Punishment included fines and work assignments and for “high crimes” the ultimate punishment was to be expelled and denominated a “suppressive”; the recalcitrant person was then treated as an enemy, all communication with him (her) was forbidden. Discipline was in charge of the “ethics officer”, who among others had access to the supposedly confidential files of adherents’ “auditing” sessions, which were in fact in the nature of confessionals or psychotherapeutic sessions. The “Hubbard electrometer” was a means of encouraging frankness and self-analysis in the subject. The court stated that this electrometer “properly used could be a scientific apparatus, but by no means a religious one”.

The court held that these deceptive practices, both in claiming to be a non-profit organization and in its dea­lings with recruits and adherents, had been consciously carried on in the interest of increasing income and profits. It noted that the management of the Church had “never per­formed an act of charity toward anyone, the word charity being taken in its sense of humanitarian”. It stated that the fact that a person did not profit personally from frau­dulent action did not absolve him of guilt.

Accordingly, the defendants were found guilty as charged.

Hubbard was sentenced in absentia to four years in prison and a fine of Fr. 35,000. H.W. Laarhuis was sentenced in absentia to three years in prison and Fr. 15,000 fine. Jacqueline Valantin was sentenced in absentia to two years imprisonment and Fr. 10,000 fine. Mandates of the arrest were authorized against all three. Georges Andreu was given a suspended sentence of one year and a Fr. 3,000 fine. Each of the defendants was assigned to pay one quater of the court costs of Fr. 32,525.93.

The ADFI claim for damages was disallowed on the grounds that it had not suffered direct damages. M. Tran was awarded the symbolic damages claimed (Fr. 1), but the additional claims were by law disallowed.

M. Andreu, according to newspaper reports, declared that case would be appealed.

Note: The case reported above is summarized from the official record of the trial and judgment numbered 9 – 11266/70 and dated 14 February 1978. Words and phases in quotation marks are translations from the French text by the writer of this report.
