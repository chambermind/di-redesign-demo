A REAL CONSPIRACY STORY: SEIZING CONTROL IN BELIZE

Tony Quinn

To check the references in this article please refer to Justice Bannister’s Judgement:

https://dialogueireland.wordpress.com/2012/07/30/judge-bannisters-judgement-read-it-for-yourself/
Tony Quinn claims to teach people to make money – but there’s no mystery about how people get rich through doing business. They do it by taking risks that pay off. They put their money on their judgements, and if their judgement is right, they win. The people who attended Educo seminars were taught that they ought to have faith in their judgement, keep positive and believe in themselves. Some did this and were unlucky – for instance, the people who invested in a would-be pop singer who never made it into the big time.

Some did this and were lucky – for instance, the people who bought shares from Sheila McCaffrey in an oil company that struck oil on its first drilling.

So, by the rules of becoming wealthy through business, these people ought to have been very happy for the last five years – enjoying the huge dividends coming from the massively profitable company they owned shares in, and looking forward to many more of the same. However, this is not the case. How did this happen? Read on and discover a tale of

 Secret meetings, backdated forged letters, a collective of ‘ladies-in-waiting to the Quinn’ taking mysterious secret orders, shuttling across oceans and continents, putting documents together with various datings and “loyally” coming up with versions of these events in Court that the Judge kindly called “mistaken”. As usual, no sooner was one stage of the conspiracy accomplished (the freezing out of the Class B shareholders) than the next to be targeted were some of the conspirators themselves, and three directors disappeared from the Board.

At the beginning, it all looked so good for the Class B shareholders. The company they had invested in was governed by an Operating Agreement that formed the contract under which they bought their shares. It was business-like, open and fair. It protected the company against unwanted takeovers, it protected the interests of shareholders, and it made sure that any important decisions had to be run past the company members first. Judge Bannister set out its important points in his judgement (the numbers in square brackets refer to the clauses in the judgement)

1. There were two classes of shares – Class A and Class B [4]

Class A shares give two voting units for each percentage point of the total shares issued by the company – so if there were 100 shares issued, a Class A share would give 2 voting units. Class B shares gives one voting unit for each percentage point. [9]

2. There were five people who were allocated Class A shares – these were called the Originators of the company and although the judgement doesn’t say exactly what they all did, no-one disputes that without all of them the oil venture could never have happened. They were

Susan Morrice – a petroleum geologist

Jean Cornec – a geologist

Sheila McCaffrey

Paul Marriott – an oil drilling engineer

Michael Usher – a Belizian seismic surveyor [4]

3. There could not be more than 10 Directors on the Board of the company. The 5 Originators were all automatically Directors and could not be removed from the Board under any circumstances. If one of them died, however, their Successor in title (inheritor) could take their place, so when Michael Usher died in 2004, eventually his widow Patricia took his place. Class B shareholders who were “suitably qualified” (the Judge does not say what qualification would be regarded as suitable) could be appointed to the Board. The only other way a new director could be appointed would be if they were elected by a vote of the majority of the membership. To do this, all members would have to be notified of the proposal to elect a director, and given the opportunity to vote. [5] [6] None of these things happened when Tony Quinn was supposedly appointed to the Board, so the judgement clearly found that he had never legally been a Director. [91]

4. There had to be an Annual General Meeting – the time and place could be decided by the Board, but there had to be at least one meeting each year where a notice of this meeting and the agenda were sent out to each member and they were given an opportunity to attend. Things that usually happen at an AGM, although the judgement does not detail these, are that the audited annual company accounts are presented to the members, who can vote to accept them or to query them, and the Directors answer the members’ questions about the company and how it has been run during the year. In a normal company AGM, the Directors often have to answer challenging questions from critical shareholders who are unhappy with their management. This principle of the right of shareholders to open information about how their money is being used is so important that the Agreement stipulated that actions which were supposed to happen in meetings could only be taken without a meeting if every member entitled to vote signed a written consent to this. [8]

So if the Operating Agreement was proposed to be changed, for instance, either 25% of the members had to propose this, or the Board of Directors had to propose it to the members, but every single member had to see this resolution and sign it, whether they voted for or against it, or it would not be valid even if a majority of voting units (and remember the Class A shares count for 2 of these to every 1 of the Class B) voted for it. This is important because if a minority of shareholders believe they are being oppressed by the majority, they can appeal to the Court even if the majority are against them. So the Agreement made sure that they would know what was happening, so they had the knowledge necessary to make an appeal to the Court. Shareholders, just like citizens of a democracy, cannot exercise their proper and legal rights if they are kept in the dark, so the open circulation of information is crucial. [47] [48]

  1. The Directors could decide if and when to issue dividends. They did not have to issue dividends at any particular time, or at all, but each year, each shareholder’s share of the profits was allocated to them pro rata for their shareholding – that is, if you owned one share, and there were 100 shares, 1% of the company’s audited profit that year would belong to you. That meant that no-one could come along later and be allocated dividends based on that share of the profit. This is a very important right, and meant that the people who had taken the risk and put in money before the oil was found, got their proper reward for their business enterprise and their good luck. Dividends when issued were to be pro rata – that is in exact proportion to the number of shares owned by the member. [10]

6. When people have put money into a company, they are its owners – the shareholders, not the Directors, own the business, it is their property, that is the law. So there are two very important rights in a company that have to be protected – one is that the shareholders have a right to dispose of their shareholding, to sell it because they own it, but the company may often insist that they first have to offer their shares to the company, so that control is not lost to a rival company, for instance, a “hostile takeover”. The other is that further shares, if they are issued, can only be issued for a good reason (such as to raise more money) and with the knowledge of the existing shareholders who are given an opportunity to vote so that they know of the issue of more shares and can go to Court if they feel their rights are being oppressed. These rights were protected very well under the original Operating Agreement.

   

  1. Members can sell their shares to any other members with no problem. They can also sell to outside entities, either persons or companies or pension funds, for instance, but they first have to offer the shares to the company in case it wants to buy them back. The Board can only agree to buy them if the members all have an opportunity to vote on this and a majority agrees to approve the buyback of the sale, or they can vote instead for some or all of the members to buy the shares themselves. If the company doesn’t want to buy them back, and no other members want to buy them, then the shareholder can sell to an outside party, but the price to the outside party can’t be less than the price they were offered at to the Board or other members. In practice, what this means is that if a shareholder is tempted by a good offer from outside, then they are able to take this deal, but the company and other members are protected by the proviso that they have the opportunity to buy the shares first. So the shareholder gets the good price, but the company is protected from hostile takeover. It is a good balance of rights and a frequent arrangement in companies. [11]
  1. New shares can be issued with the written consent of members with a two-thirds majority – this means that all members must be given the opportunity to vote and this, of course, means that they are aware of what is happening and can appeal to the Court if they feel they are being unfairly treated. The shares have to be issued in exchange for something, whether this is money, goods or services. It was because there was no vote of members and nothing given in exchange that Judge Bannister found there was a “serious question” over whether the issue of 64,000 shares to Tony Quinn were valid or not. [12] [24]

So, that’s a normal business arrangement – everything is clear, the owners of the company are protected, how things are done is all out in the open.

Now fast forward five years from 2002, and in 2007 a new Operating Agreement is put in place. Judge Bannister sums up the principal differences from the original [48]

  1. Tony Quinn was backdated as an “originator”
  2. Originators could veto the issue of Class A shares
  3. “Substantial restrictions” were introduced on the extent to which a vote of a majority of shareholders could stop the Directors from doing what they wanted
  4. New Directors could only be selected by Class A members
  5. The Directors did not have to allocate dividends pro rata for share ownership – that is, they could play favourites
  6. Shareholders could only sell their shares to individual persons, that is, not to companies or pension funds, for instance, who might offer a much better price
  7. The Directors could refuse to allow shareholders to sell their shares to anyone at all, if they wanted to
  8. “Savage restrictions” were introduced on the ability to sell or transfer Class A shares, which reduced them to Class B shares except for voting rights
  9. Class B shares could only be transferred freely (that is, with the guaranteed consent of the Directors, who could otherwise refuse consent for no reason because of no. 7 above) to other existing shareholders who had completed one of Tony Quinn’s Educo training courses – that is, it was all to be kept under Quinn control, Class B shareholders could not sell even to the few Class B shareholders who were not Educo-trained
  10. Future changes to the Operating Agreement could be made by a majority of the Class A voting units

The above is not a business agreement. Point no. 9 makes that clear – it bears about as much relation to a business enterprise, a limited company owned by its shareholders, as the People’s Republic of China at the time of the Cultural Revolution did to a democratic state run by and for its citizens. The shareholders are serfs, to be disposed of at the will of the all-powerful Directors. The restrictions on their ability to sell their own property – their shares – and their rights to the profits of their company in dividends, effectively leave a cabal of Directors able to keep shareholders, who are technically wealthy people, owning shares in a phenomenally successful oil company and having rights to dividends, trapped in poverty without access to their own money.

And how did that happen? Why, by a classic conspiracy story. Secret meetings, backdated forged letters, a collective of ‘ladies-in-waiting to the Quinn’ taking mysterious secret orders, shuttling across oceans and continents, putting documents together with various datings and “loyally” [36] coming up with versions of these events in Court that the Judge kindly called “mistaken” [43]. The full exciting story, full of twists, turns, and stabs in the back, is told from Clauses 29 to 63 of Judge Bannister’s judgement. As usual, no sooner was one stage of the conspiracy accomplished (the freezing out of the Class B shareholders) than the next to be targeted were some of the conspirators themselves, and three directors disappeared from the Board.

And why did a conspiracy have to be undertaken? Because reality had to be changed, and that can’t be done in the open. Because the reality was

  • Tony Quinn was and is not a businessman. He does not take risks. The only asset he puts in is hot air of low quality – his latest brilliant invention, apparently, is that you “put the cart before the horse”, and instructions for doing this will be given to the lucky members of the Good Life Club. The contracts he gets people to sign are “heads I win, tails you lose” – they exchange money and services for jumbled-up ideas that they have to try to make work in order not to face the fact that they have made a serious error of judgement. Despite the great results Quinn promises, there are no “money-back” guarantees, so people try their hardest to believe they have got something out of it. If they don’t, it’s their fault for not making it work and the solution is take another seminar – so no risk to Quinn!

A sustainable business can only be run by people who have at least some grasp of reality – that is, they realize that other people have rights, and if they ignore that then there will be consequences. Otherwise, it’s not a business enterprise but a raiding party.
