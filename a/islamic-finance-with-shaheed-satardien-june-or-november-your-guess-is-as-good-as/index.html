Co-existence, Financial Stability & Peaceful Co-operation —

                                        Mutually assured necessities.

 

How many marriages have failed due to economic difficulties and money problems? Indeed that number is not small at all. Relations between people quickly become sour when financial problems arise. Friends fall out, families disintegrate and associations disassociate. That is the personal impact of economic instability; and from the micro to the macro: how many wars have resulted from economic difficulties between nations?

Many reasons have been forwarded for the source of World War 2, but undoubtedly economics played a significant part. The economic collapse of the Weimar Republic in Germany, and financial reparations for World War 1 levied on the German people, lead to the rise of the Nazi party with all that it implied.

After that conflict there was a certain recognition (particularly in Germany & France as historical belligerents) that economic co-operation was essential to generate peaceful co-operation. That recognition lead to the setting up of the European Coal & Steel Community of six countries in 1952, which in turn lead to the creation of the European Economic Community (EEC), which came into being in 1958. This eventually morphed into the European Union (EU).

Whatever one may think of the European project, and the economic integration at its core, it has been very successful in maintaining peaceful co-operation between those countries participating in the project.

So evidence of the link between financial stability and peaceful co-operation is clear. Countries that are mutually economically dependent and/or supportive of each other are highly likely to exist in a state of peaceful co-operation which is in their mutual interests.

Currently there is instability in the world’s economic system and in the European economic system in particular. There are significant structural weaknesses in the Eurozone, including excessive government debt, banking crises, uncompetitive strictures, economic incompetence, vested interests stifling change etc, etc. Indeed it is not too much of an exaggeration to state that the Euro zone itself (as it is presently constituted) is under threat.

This instability allied with financial mismanagement has already resulted in serious social unrest in Greece and if the Euro project fails then further unrest in other countries is highly likely.

Whilst peaceful co-operation can only be achieved in the presence of economic stability, the converse is also true – it is difficult to think of any economic well being in the absence of peaceful co-operation. Business can only thrive in an atmosphere of social stability, where there is some level of certainty as to future events, and where social upheaval is unlikely.

The integrated nature of the world economy means that a fiscal disaster in one area will have very serious consequences in other areas, no matter how far away they are. So the world economy must be stabilised, and not just for purely financial reasons.

It is likely that there is no one solution, no “silver bullet”, to the problems facing our economic systems. The complexity of the issues involved, the political realities, self-interest, and so on, mean that any solutions must be multi-faceted too. The instinct to play it safe at this juncture is compelling, but the issues of the banking crisis and sovereign debt is of such enormity that some radical solutions in financial systems must be contemplated (at least).

Some futurologists contend that future conflicts will be based on commodities (oil, water, food, information, technology, energy, etc) but such negative predictions can be avoided by ensuring that our financial systems remain healthy enough to feed economic growth sufficient for a prosperous future for all.

Islamic finance as a contributor to global fiscal stability & peaceful co-operation

                                                                                                                   

2008 was a watershed year for Global Finance, the virtual meltdown of the world banking system had many causes including rewarding of reckless lending, herd-instinct behaviour masquerading as informed analysis, short-term viewpoints and the abandonment of responsible banking practices.

In the wake of that disaster, the consequences of which are still being played out to this day, the Islamic banking sector was a model of relative stability. The Islamic compliant sector did suffer losses, some collateral damage in the global finance implosion was inevitable, but it suffered damage at a far lesser rate than its Western equivalent.

What can the Western world’s financial system learn from the Islamic financial systems? How can global finance wean itself off the pursuit of an addictive “fast buck” to a more sustainable model; and how does this lead to peaceful co-operation?

 

This conference aims to present an overview of Islamic banking, insurance and investments and how its philosophy can contribute to sustainable prosperity. It will address the relationship between financial stability and peace. It will deal with the influence of interest, the notion of shared risk/shared profit, lease-purchase, the rules under which financial services are deemed to be Islam-compliant and much, much more.

Papers may include:

The Emerald Isle and Islamic Finance & Investments

The island of Ireland is a great little country with many tasks before it, not least of which is the generation of economic growth necessary for prosperity.

The size of the domestic economy, allied with the fiscal retrenchment required by the State’s lenders, means that the export of goods & services will remain the key element in economic planning for some time to come.

The difficulty facing the Government is finding the wherewithal to generate economic activity. Thus all opportunities for direct foreign investment with minimal financial contribution from the State is a positive development; and any creation of business and entrepreneurial activity should be grasped with both hands.

The Muslim Council of Ireland and the Irish Muslim Chamber of Commerce and Enterprise are proposing to organise an International Conference in the Republic of Ireland on the contribution that Islamic Finance can make to economic stability and the relationship between economic stability & peaceful co-existence.

According to Standard & Poor’s Ratings Services, Islamic Finance assets reached approximately $400 billion during 2009, but with a potential market of $4 trillion. Other analysts, notably in “The Banker”, estimate assets reached over $800 billion in 2008. Whatever about the exact figure it is clear that very, very significant sums are involved.

The Irish government developed legislation (Section 35 of the Finance Bill 2010) to facilitate Islamic Finance in Ireland. In his address to the Dáil on 9th February 2010 the Minister of Finance stated:

“As a Government we must ensure that, in the face of a growing competitive threat from new emerging markets and the aggressive marketing of established centres, the IFSC remains competitive in all areas. That is why Section 35 of the Bill contains provisions which will help us to attract our share of the growing market of Islamic Finance. In 2007, Islamic Finance was estimated to be worth about US $700bn and is estimated to be growing at roughly 10% per annum. The measures in the Bill will provide level playing fields for all commercial financial product providers so that the tax benefits that accrue to conventional financial products are also available to Shari’a compliant products.”

 

 

Making Ireland a hub for Islamic financial activity in the West

[It is important to point out – given Ireland’s recent experience – that borrowing money based on Islamic Finance principles has as one of the key points the sharing of risk  (and reward) between the lender and the borrower. Under such principles it is simply impossible for a lender to insist that a borrower assume all risk, whilst the lender gets all the reward, without any risk.]

There are significant advantages for Islamic Finance in Ireland, they include:

1.                  The corporate taxation rate is only 12.5%, one of the lowest in the EU. The UK rate is between 21% and 28%, Germany is over 30% and France is 33.33%. This has lead to large numbers of multi-national companies basing their European Headquarters in Ireland, including Google, Facebook, Linkedin, Microsoft, Intel etc. A number of UK financial services entities have also moved their offices to Ireland.

2.                  There is a large existing International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) in Dublin with all the necessary infrastructure and support that such implies. There are over 400 international operations approved to trade in the IFSC, while a further 700 managed entities are approved to carry on business under the IFSC programme. It is host to half of the world’s top 50 banks, and half of the top 20 insurance companies. http://www.ifsc.ie/default.aspx

3.                  English is the primary language in Ireland, and the level of EU & world migration over the past 20 years means that all world languages are represented in the community.

4.                  Ireland is a member of the EU, and the Eurozone community which is controlled by the European Central Bank (ECB) with all the supervisory and regulatory rigor that such implies.

5.                  The rectification of Ireland’s banking industry has resulted in the financial regulatory architecture of the State being very robust, with Irish banks being the most capitalised financial institutions in the EU.

6.                  The Muslim population of Ireland is approximately 50,000 (officially about 40,000) and growing in an overall population of approximately 4 million plus. There is thus a local demand for Islamic financial services.

7.                  Relations between Muslims, the wider Irish society and the State are generally good. The State financially supports Muslim schools in the same way as non-Muslim schools, Muslim leaders have ready access to Governments of all persuasions and there is no legislation enacted, or pending, opposing any Muslim practices, including face veils. The Constitution of Ireland protects religious freedom and the State is active in doing so without discrimination. This is in stark contrast to some other EU nations.

8.                  Relations between Islam and the wider community in a number of other European countries have deteriorated in recent years. We have seen the rise of demagogues in The Netherlands and others in France, Germany, Sweden, Norway, Italy and elsewhere. This makes a base for Islamic banking in other EU countries a less attractive option.

9.                  The Irish economy is very open and export led, with exports accounting for approximately 25% of GDP.

10.              Whilst the City of London has the advantage of critical mass, the UK is not in the Eurozone and recent events may conspire to distance the UK even further from the European economy.

11.              Ireland does not have a colonial history (in marked contrast to several other EU States), it is not a member of any military alliance, and has not participated in any military expeditions in any other State except for UN-sanctioned peace keeping operations.

Thus Ireland has an opportunity to position itself as a centre for Islamic Finance in the EU. It has the infrastructure, it has the regulatory regime, it has social stability, it has favourable corporate taxation and it does not have historical baggage vis-à-vis Islam.

The delegates and speakers will be introduced to Ireland, its facilities, its financial regulatory regime and its advantages for Islamic finance and investment.

The potential for economic development for the IFSC in particular is obvious.
