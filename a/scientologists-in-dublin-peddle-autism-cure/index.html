29 November 2017

Scientologists in Dublin peddle ‘autism cure’

Scientology

 

A woman who says that the Church of Scientology in Dublin offered her a €1,600 detoxification programme that would cure her autistic son has complained to gardaí.

The church, which recently opened a centre in Firhouse, is likely to face questions about its alleged promotion of the controversial treatment. It follows claims by Fiona O’Leary, an autism rights campaigner, that a Scientology member based in the centre offered to sell her a “purification rundown”* programme to treat her son.

Ms O’Leary founded Autistic Rights Together, which has been campaigning against anti-vaccine conspiracy theories and therapies that are falsely sold as a treatment for autism.

Purification Rundown is a trademark owned by the governing body of the Church of Scientology. The programme includes sweating in a sauna for several hours a day, an intense exercise regime and taking high doses of supplements for several weeks. It is used by the church’s drug rehabilitation programme.

Niacin, otherwise known as Vitamin B3, is commonly used in doses of up to 5,000 mg as part of the programme. The daily recommended allowance for adults is about 15 mg.

Ms O’Leary said that she had been contacted by a representative of the Firhouse centre after she completed a personality test on a Scientology website. During the conversation she said that she was offered a copy of a book on purification rundown by L Ron Hubbard, the founder of Scientology, and then the treatment itself after she had asked about what it did. The Times has heard a recording of the conversation.

Ms O’Leary said that she was horrified to learn that the treatment was being sold with the promise that it worked “100 per cent” for conditions such as autism. A Scientologist also told her that they could provide a room to rent for €80 per week for anyone on the programme.

“Scientologists are anti-science and anti-psychiatry and encourage people to walk away from professional care,” Ms O’Leary said.

“They believe that auditing and purification rundowns can keep you free from sickness. They also claim to cure many conditions which people may have.”

She also pointed to claims about deaths among people who had had purification rundowns.

In 2003 during a legal action against the Church of Scientology, Michael Ryan, who was head of University College Dublin’s pharmacology department, told the High Court that the treatment was not safe or scientifically verified.

Ms O’Leary said that people exposed to the treatment were being placed at risk and that vulnerable adults and children would be enticed to try it on the back of misleading health claims. “This is a public health issue and the government needs to be doing something about it,” she said.

She has notified gardai as well as the Medical Council and the Health Products Regulatory Authority.

A garda spokesman confirmed yesterday that a report had been filed with gardaí in Tallaght in relation to a health issue but declined to state if it related to the Church of Scientology.

The HPRA refused to comment. The Church of Scientology in Dublin did not respond to a request for comment.

Complaints have been posted on social media that events open to the public at the new centre are being used by Scientologists to recruit members.

The 1,200-seater venue is located in the former Victory Centre which the church bought for €6 million.

Its official opening was attended by David Miscavige, the movement’s leader. Security was tight and several dozen protesters staged a demonstration outside.
