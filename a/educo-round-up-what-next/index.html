EDUCO ROUND UP – WHAT NEXT?

Tony Quinn

Here at Dialogue Ireland, we respond to queries and requests for help from people who have suffered, are suffering or know someone who has suffered or is suffering from oppression and/or exploitation at the hands of cult leaders. Our response is to provide them with information about cults in general and about their cult in particular, if we have such information in our archives, to help them to understand what has happened in the cult and to regain an awareness of their rights as human beings and citizens of a free country. The most important way to help people exiting or trying to exit from such organisations is read what others have experienced and use the blog to leave comments as part of their recovery. The reason we sometimes extend our activities to having a presence outside some cult events such as Educo-related occasions is that we know from people contacting us that DI is represented as a sinister force to cult followers, who are warned not to read our “negative” blog and to distrust our information. Hence we present on these occasions with a humorous and approachable appearance, banners etc to counteract this propaganda, so that in their time of need, wavering and disillusioned followers may decide to turn to us after all. This may be the only chance some cult followers get, to be exposed to views outside the cult bubble. Many have told us that during their time of devoted cult following, they “never thought they’d ever be talking to Dialogue Ireland”, so we consider it part of our mission to make ourselves as visible as possible to those who may benefit from our services now or in the future. We never harass anyone or attempt to prevent them from attending the event, and indeed often meet insults thrown our way, with humorous banter.

We stress again, we do not provide legal advice nor are we experts in any kind of law. However, owing to our long experience of dealing with cults and our international connections with other cult awareness and assistance organisations, we are aware of cases where former cult followers attempt to regain some of the personal and financial losses they have suffered, through legal action, and we know that it is of value to people contemplating this type of action to have a forum where these issues can be aired, provided it is understood that this is only opinion-sharing and that it does not constitute anything other than ordinary discussion among non-legal experts. We as we did at the INE as part of our services have people who are legally trained to assist victims to inform themselves about how they might obtain redress for courses which were misrepresented and cost beyond any real market value.

We have inadvertently received constitutional protection for work of DI from the Irish Courts. The reason why we were in court case was Susan Morrice was trying to get us to give evidence in a Denver case. Not only did the judge throw that out but he actually gave all of those who seek our assistance great support.

“Since Mr. Garde’s activities fall squarely within the education of public opinion, there is a high constitutional value in ensuring that his right to voice these views in relation to the actions of religious cults is protected. It does not require much imagination to accept that critical information in relation to the actions of those bodies would dry up if Mr. Garde could be compelled to reveal this information, whether in the course of litigation or otherwise. It is obvious from the very text of Article 40.6.1 that the right to educate (and influence) public opinion is at the very heart of the rightful liberty of expression. That rightful liberty would be compromised – perhaps even completely jeopardised – if disclosure of sources and discussions with sources could readily be compelled through litigation.”

The case of the Educo/INE shareholders is particularly complex, because they started off mostly as two separate activities – being an Educo follower was one thing, and being an INE shareholder was another. But with the entry of the Educo leader, Tony Quinn, into INE, and a new Operating Agreement installed (as it turned out illegally) in the company which among many other unusually severe restrictions on shareholders forbade the sale of shares to anyone who had not attended an Educo seminar, INE essentially ceased to be a normal commercial enterprise and became part of the Educo cult.

Not only is the company now being run in this unusual manner, but cases about it have to be heard in the British Virgin Islands, which is outside the experience of most solicitors or legal advice centres that most people in Ireland can get access to. So shareholders in Ireland that we have heard from, after the confusion of the AGM are still very uncertain of the current position, and of what they can do next. The events of the AGM were so confusing, and we have heard so many different opinions, that we believe we can provide a useful service to this group of people suffering from the actions of the cult leader, Tony Quinn, and some of his followers, in particular Susan Morrice, by collecting and publishing here some of the opinions we have heard. Many of these are simply logical and common-sense. There are other options being pursued and discussed among shareholders, which we cannot express an opinion about as we do not know the oil business. However, the one we get asked the most about is following up Judge Bannister’s statement in his judgment that he wanted to hear from the Class B shareholders, to see if they wanted a dissolution of the company, and as it is always useful to have options, we have collected and share here the following material about this.

  1. We understand that the shareholders who are opposed to the cultist takeover and running of the company are cooperating together. Most of them could not afford to take legal action alone and at least a number of them would be best to work together,and probably also, if a decision is made to pursue an action in court, to seek legal representation on a no-win, no-fee basis. In order to get meaningful legal advice, it is essential to present a clear and factual case with documentary evidence where possible, and to focus on the aspects of the case that a court can act on.
  2. It would obviously be advisable to check whether any appeals have been lodged against the Bannister judgment, and if so, on what grounds, that is, which of the findings of Judge Bannister are being challenged legally and whether these are relevant for a case by the Class B shareholders to seek a dissolution of the company and also a ruling on whether the shares issued to Tony Quinn for no consideration were validly issued or should be cancelled, the other issue that Judge Bannister stated that the Class B shareholders, an no one else, were entitled to pursue. This could possibly be discovered by a query to the Registrar of the BVI Court

An appeal could only be lodged by one of the parties to the case which was heard by Judge Bannister, but it is possible that either Sheila McCaffrey or Susan Morrice/Tony Quinn etc might lodge/have lodged an appeal and this might affect whether a new case based on the judgment could be made until this appeal has been heard. Again, the Registrar might be able to answer this.

  1. In any case, it is important to establish the legal status of what happened at the AGM, under the terms of the original Operating Agreement and under the law of the British Virgin Islands on the running of companies.
  2. The Bannister judgment found that the company was not being run in accordance with its Operating Agreement of 2002, that the Operating Agreement of 2007 was not legal, and that under BVI law there was a serious case to be made for dissolving the company.
  3. From the reaction of Susan Morrice to the judgment, it appeared that her intention was to claim that her running of the company and the actions she had taken were validated by Judge Bannister, and that her intention in holding an AGM and in issuing dividends immediately before the AGM was to succeed in installing either the full Operating Agreement of 2007 or some parts of it, to legitimise her position. So it would be very helpful for any concerned shareholders to gather together the following
  4. Copies of any documents such as Press Releases, communications to shareholders etc which they received from INE after the Bannister judgment was issued.
  5. Copies of any documents which were sent to them by INE in advance of the AGM – such as an agenda, supporting documentation explaining any items on which they would be asked to vote at the AGM, company accounts etc.
  6. Copies of any documentation accompanying the issue of dividends, showing for instance how they were calculated and what period of company trading they were based on.

An account of what happened at the AGM – this would be best written separately by each person, in a form which could be taken to a solicitor and sworn as an affidavit (a statement which is signed by the person making the statement in front of a solicitor or “commissioner for oaths” who administers an oath such as is given in court, and witnesses the statement to attest that such an oath has been administered – this gives the statement the same status as evidence given in court). These statements do not have to be elaborate at all – plain and simple unvarnished accounts of exactly what you remember, written down as close to the time of the events as possible, is preferred by the court. Of particular interest is the voting procedure and conduct of the meeting – witness accounts we have heard indicate that a solicitor in the employ of Susan Morrice was introduced as an objective and impartial chairman, and that voting was by a show of hands, on which the “chairman” declared the motion carried as “the ayes have it”, although some have stated to us that they believed only a minority of hands were raised in favour. It would appear that what could be expected for a normal AGM is that a detailed list of shareholders would be checked by a firm of independent scrutineers, that voting would be allowed by post with the forms sent by and returned to the scrutineers, and that given that the voting units (i.e. the number of shares) held by each shareholder varies, counting a show of hands would appear to be totally inadequate and inaccurate as a method of measuring whether a motion was carried or not.

  1. Copies of any documentation received from INE since the AGM, for instance, making claims or stating intentions as to how the company was now to be run.
  2. These documents can be labelled and listed as 1, 2, 3 etc together with a summary of the details of the case. Also included in the documents would be a copy of the original Operating Agreement of 2002 (if anyone has it), a copy of the attempted Operating Agreement of 2007 (if anyone has it), and a copy of the Bannister judgment, together with details of any appeal currently lodged against this judgment.
  3. Bear in mind that even if the agenda set out by Susan Morrice had been successfully passed at the AGM, the conditions may be considered to be “oppressive” to shareholders who may therefore be entitled to take their case to court in any event.
  4. The bundle of documents and summary, and setting out the action you’d like to take e.g. seeking dissolution of the company, gives you the basic ingredients to obtain an Opinion from Counsel. The legal profession as you probably know is divided in many countries, certainly in England, Ireland and the BVI (both Ireland and the BVI are more or less based on the English system) into solicitors who handle most client contacts and barristers who can speak in court. Barristers, or Counsel as they are also called, develop specialisations in different areas of law, and before launching into the expense and risk of a court action, solicitors on behalf of their clients will often seek an expert opinion from counsel with a specialisation in the area involved in their case. You can get a written opinion, and/or a conference with the barrister. You don’t actually have to employ a solicitor, just as you can represent yourself in court, you can, as far as we know, also directly ask for a barrister’s opinion. Barristers mostly tend to work in group practices known as chambers, and which barrister gets which job is usually organised by the clerk of the chambers, who is familiar with their specialisations. Depending on the complexity of the case, obtaining an opinion may cost £2,000 or more. It also depends on the rank and experience of the barrister. Getting an opinion from a barrister does not, of course, guarantee your success, but it can give an idea of the parts of any case that are worth taking to court. The more organised and focused you can get your material to present to the barrister, the more easily the opinion can be given and probably at a lesser cost. The clerk of the chambers is usually able to give you an estimate of costs so you can decide whether you want to go ahead with this or not. The question is, which barrister would be the best to ask? We at DI have no expertise in this, however these are some suggestions we have heard
  5. Type “Barristers in BVI” or similar into a Google search – we did this and came up with a number of firms in the British Virgin Islands which all, naturally, spoke very highly of themselves. This might be one route to go. It is worth remembering that most people taking cases in the BVI Commercial Court are international corporations, banks etc so the barristers in this area will be more used to clients like this. However, this is probably the case for most barristers specialising in this area, wherever they are located.
  6. Judge Bannister himself, in his capacity as a Queen’s Counsel (the highest rank) in the English system, or if he is not able to do it because he is the judge in the case, perhaps someone recommended in the area of BVI commercial law by the clerk of the chambers. This would be the first step, risking only the cost of the opinion and the effort of putting together the bundle, which does not commit anyone to pursue a legal case or take any further action, but should hopefully provide some clarity and impartiality in the situation.

Looking further ahead, to the decision as to whether or not to pursue a court action depending on the counsel’s opinion given, from general advice given to us by lawyers and people who have been through court cases, it is worth bearing in mind the following

  1. Being involved in a legal case is the equivalent of a part-time job. Since there would be a group of people involved, it would be important that everybody could be realistic about what role they would be able to take and fulfil their obligations.
  2. It is best to regard it as a job, and to take an entirely practical attitude to it – if it is not worth it for what it could eventually bring you financially, then you might be better off spending the time and energy working at something else.
  3. Get some idea of time scale – some cases take years to come to court, there may be ways of speeding this up, however these may also have costs. Bear in mind that the other side may attempt to use delaying tactics in order to wear you out.
  4. Even no-win, no-fee arrangements need to be paid eventually – the ideal is that you win and the court awards costs against the other party so that you don’t have costs deducted from your award, but this might not be the case, or might only be partially the case, also if costs are awarded against you, who pays these, does your legal firm provide an indemnity from their insurance? If not, do you have any insurance that could cover this or could you obtain some? Also, if you change your mind part way through and decide not to continue, will the no-win, no-fee arrangement allow you to walk away, or will you be stuck either with carrying on to the end even if you’re ill or desperately need to do something else, or paying the bill so far? It is best to be entirely clear about what you’re getting into and to make sure that this agreement is in writing.
  5. Your lawyers are experts in the law, you are the expert on the facts of your case. Keep on top of what is happening at all times, keep your common sense to the fore and don’t hesitate to give your opinion if you think things should be done differently while having respect for your lawyer’s expertise, and be pro-active in producing and collecting documentation so that costs are not run up by the lawyers having to do it for you.
  6. Don’t let the case get too elaborate, in particular don’t get sidetracked by “clever” points – sometimes lawyers like to try these out to see if they will work, but they often annoy judges who feel their time is being wasted and it usually works best to stick with the basics. It is your case and in the end the lawyers will walk away and carry on with their lives and you will have to live with the judgment.
  1. Don’t get obsessed by details that have made a particular impression on you, or personality-based or emotionally-charged aspects of the case – in this instance, it doesn’t matter how betrayed you felt when you read about what had gone on behind the scenes when you had believed in Tony Quinn, or what on earth was in that picture when the plug was pulled out of the screen – the essential points are your rights as a shareholder and what difference a good price for your shares that might be obtained by a liquidator could make to your life and your family’s life.
  2. Liquidation is often an uncertain business – many liquidations are only able to pay the liquidators’ fees which are often very high. One of the questions to ask, possibly of the counsel (who will tell you if that is not within the terms of giving his or her legal advice) or possibly of a firm of liquidators (sometimes these might be combined in a solicitors office, certainly this seemed the case in some of the BVI firms we saw online, would be what would be the likely financial outcome for shareholders in the case of a dissolution of the company. This would involve providing some information on the market i.e. what other companies would be interested in buying the oil operation and what they might pay for it. The INE situation has some unusual features compared to most proposed liquidations because
  3.          i.            The company is (supposedly, since accounts don’t seem to be publicly available at least we at DI have not seen any) profitable and still has substantial assets and so could be sold as a going concern.
  4.        ii.            Very substantial sums have been spent from the company and are still being spent (we have been told that seven lawyers were ferried in to the AGM by Susan Morrice and legal cases continue to be issued, the latest being a challenge to the High Court ruling of Judge Gerald Hogan) to the Irish Supreme Court. Judge Bannister commented in his judgment that “there is evidence that INE money is being used by Ms Morrice and Mr. Quinn to fund litigation in a manner that is not authorised by the 2002 agreement” [Judgment, Clause 91].

It would be worth asking counsel’s opinion if the liquidator would have the right to withhold monies which were spent from the company, authorised by Susan Morrice, which the liquidator judged to be unjustified, from the amount of money owed to Susan Morrice in respect of her shares in the company, in the event of a dissolution and sale of the assets of the company. If this was not sufficient to cover the substantial sums which she has apparently authorised to be spent, and is apparently still spending on legal and other expenditures such as travel and accommodation, then she is a very wealthy woman and it would be worth asking whether the liquidator would have the right to pursue her personally for monies that were judged to have been spent inappropriately to be restored to the company from her personal funds and added to the profits of the company for the benefit of shareholders. She could also be liable for the liquidators’ costs in pursuing such an action. Indeed, as it is largely through her actions in pushing through the illegal 2007 agreement and subsequently running the company as she did, that the costs of a liquidation had to be incurred by the company at all, it would be worth asking if all or a substantial part of the liquidator’s costs should be borne by her personally.

  1.       iii.     If Tony Quinn’s shares are invalid, then the LRP issued to him on the basis of these shares would presumably be repayable by him, or perhaps again by Morrice since presumably she authorised this payment to an invalid shareholder. If he was issued a dividend, the same would apply.

A few final comments

Let us remember who is at the heart of this dispute and how it is that the shareholders have not been happily collecting dividends for the last six years from a properly run company and looking forward to continuing this until the oil runs out

  • Tony Quinn who claims to have made a vast fortune and lives in a tax haven in case anyone should ask him to make a contribution to the running of the country where he grew up, and
  • Susan Morrice who, as an originating Class A shareholder has a shareholding which must be worth at least something similar to what she agreed to pay Jean Cornec for a similar shareholding – $15 million – and yet stated in court that the “estate [that is, the joint assets of herself and her husband when amalgamated to be divided up in their divorce] was so much larger that it [her INE shareholding] became irrelevant” (quoted in the unofficial transcripts made by shareholders attending the court in BVI). That is, this woman is so wealthy that $15 million is small change.

At $180 per share (which was the value quoted by Susan Morrice for her divorce settlement), someone having a small Class B shareholding, say 200 shares, would have $36,000. That is, about €25,000. That would make a lot of difference to many shareholders – you can imagine what it would do, for instance, to help provide a home or education for some of your family, for you and your family’s health or security in an emergency, for care for an elderly parent…

According to themselves, it’s small change to Tony Quinn and Susan Morrice, they’d barely hear it rattling in their pockets, but they’d still rather spend Other People’s Money.
