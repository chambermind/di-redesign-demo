Political Implications In Neo-Buddhism

Religion

By Thomas Hultberg

Did The Buddha give any explicitly Political teaching or is it possible to find in the Dhamma any basis for political programme or view point? Some Buddhists today say “no” while others try to base political activities on the Buddha Dhamma. Who are right and who are wrong?

First we must look at the basic texts i.e. the Pali Canon. We can go to Mahaparinibbana Sutta in Digha Nikaya. The Buddha says there that if a country follows the seven conditions for the well-being of a state it will neither decline nor be subject to conquest. The content of these seven conditions is that the people are not supposed to destroy the traditions they have received. The people are supposed to listen to the elders, not to neglect the traditional religious cults and temples, to support and safeguard Buddhist monks. The government is to consult frequently with a council and to make and execute its decisions unanimously.

The Buddha himself organized his order of monks (Sangha) along these lines. He was not interested in reforms or revolutionary changes in society. On the contrary – he preferred a stable society governed by a just government, either monarchic or republican. As in all other matters the Buddha in this respect was pragmatic: that society is good which can guarantee the existence of the Sangha and, of course, the Dhamma.

It is permissible for people living in society to worship gods. That is a part of the religious tradition which helps people to live a better life. But for the ultimate religious goal of Buddhism, the achievement of enlightenment and Nirvana, the worship of gods is utterly irrelevant indeed prohibited. Monks are not allowed to indulge in such practices, as being men set apart from society who have left a worldly life behind them.

In the Sangha all monks are in principle equal – the one who enters the Sangha steps out of the social differences of caste and of other barriers between men. This rule held also for most of the other old indian groups of ascetics.

How are we then to find a basis for political action? Agganna Sutta in Digha Nikaya says that every social class has to fulfil duties which are in the interest of society as a whole. From this idea were developed socio-moral ideas giving rise to a social ethics.

Let us listen to the Secretary General of Mahamakut Buddhist University, Phra Dhammadhajamuni, in his preface to a dissertation written by a Thai scholar
Dr Siddhi Butr-Indr (The social Philosophy of Buddhism. Bangkok: Mahamakut Buddhist University 1973). His words give a good picture of the optimistic view today of the possibility of adapting Buddhism to the needs of modern man and of its capacity to give answers and guidance for life in society.

“The study of religious and other aspects of Buddhism, one of the most dominant cultural treasures of Eastern societies, has been contributed by many scholars Western as well as Eastern. Some of them, especially the Western, are inclined to conclude irrespectively with their personal backgrounds of onesided view that Buddhism is concerned only with reclusive life, that it lacks doctrines and spirit dealing with secular existence, and that the best thing it can do is to stimulate man to retire absolutely from worldly involvement, regardless of whatever happe’ns relatively to other people and abstaining from doing things good and benevolent to the rest of mankind.”

“The researcher interprets, faithfully and scientifically, the teachings of the Buddha with regard mainly to the secular involvements of human society, and studies the fundamental principles on which the Buddhist society is based and developed. So far
as the secularism of human humanity is concerned, in terminology of comparative studies with other philosophies, he is strongly against any interpretation of the Buddhist doctrine in the camps either of Capitalism or of its antithesis, either of Materialism or of its antithesis which, in his view, are althogether extremes.”

“The essential spirit of Buddhism represents “The Middle Way of Life”, dialectically rejecting the evil aspects of the above mentioned extremisms; or rather transcending them. Buddhism stands for the welfare and well-being, physical as well as mental, of all mankind. The need for physical or mental welfare is acknowledged, but in the final analysis of human existence it is not made the ultimate end of humanity; the highest, deepest, and absolute of human value lies in the moral, mental and spiritual.”

Buddhists in the West rarely have a Sangha in the neighbourhood on which they can rely and on which they can center their religious life. They must rely on their own studies, pujas and meditation, in groups or alone without the support and teaching of a monk. That is why Buddhism in the West is a torso because the Sangha is a nescessary part of Buddhism. Western Buudhists concentrate on the layman’s ethics as well as they can. A layman is to take his refuge in Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha, he is to follow the five basic moral precepts (panca) sila), he is to conquer selfishness and be generous. An ethical code is to be found in the ten precepts of right actions (dasa kusalakammani):
1. One refrains from killing and lives mercifully towards all living beings. 2. One refrains from stealing (and is honest and pure in heart). 3. One refrains from sexual misconduct and does not violate the social mores with regard to sex. 4. One refrains from telling a lie. 5. One refrains from slander and calumny. 6. One refrains from harsh speech and uses language that is civil and pleasant to hear. 7. One refrains from idle gossip and speaks at the right time what is true and factual. 8. One refrains from covetousness and does not feel jealous of another’s fortune and prosperity. 9. One refrains from and does not harbour any wickedness of thought. 10. One refrains from holding false views and believes in the right philosophy of life.

From another source (Digha Nikaya, III) we have a more detailed description of the far-reaching standard of good conduct, which is reported to have been observed and worked out by the Buddha himself in previous existences and which also deals effectively with the ethical side of civil conduct. The context in which it occurs implies that every Buddhist is expected to follow the example of the Teacher. It refers to undertakings in all good things for an individual as well as a social life:

“…unfaltering enterprise in wholesome ways of thought, word and deed, engaging oneself in altruistic activities and virtuous undertakings, observing filial and pious duties by honouring the superiors or leaders of the family, and, we may assume, the community and society; (keeping of traditions and other such things of loyalty and supporting religious men, accumulating and working for the good of others)… living for the welfare of the multitudes, being a dispeller of fright, terror and panic, a surveyor of just protection and wardenship, giving supplies,…dwelling modestly and compassionately, being merciful and friendly to all fellow human beings and all other living beings,…practising the four fold supporting principle as mentioned above,… becoming a bearer of welfare and happiness to fellow people,…a zealous learner of arts and science, good conduct and action, approaching religious men and asking them for advice for ways of good conduct,… living without wrath, but with serenity, subduing the habit of finding fault with others,…uniting and reuniting people by making them live together harmoniously and, having created harmony, rejoicing in
it oneself,…being sincerely desirous of contemplating the goodwill of the public, able to understand, analyse and distinguish different natures of different people,… growing affective habits for the good and interest, comfort and well-being, security and stability of the public, considering how they may increase in confidence, morality, education, generosity, rightousness, intelligence, economic prosperity and so on,…acquiring the habit of harming no creatures…of an upright, candid and lofty mind, contemplating people with an affectionate eye,…putting away abusive habits…and wrong ways of livelyhood, and revolting against all kinds of cheating and unfair treatment by avoiding all sorts of violence.”

And how about Neo-Buddhism? We must define the concept. So far Neo-Buddhism has meant at least three things: 1. the Theosophical approach to Buddhism as it was pro-fessed by Western Buddhists at the end of the last century and at the beginning of this century; 2. Dr Ambedkar’s movement in India that led to a mass conversion of outcasts to Buddhism in 1956; Buddhism as it is seen in the West today as a result of Buddhist missionary activities or as being found in books, magazines and the mass media. Here we have at least three sub groups: a. traditionally organized branches of Asian Buddhism (i.e. monasteries with one or more monks surrounded by laymen); b. societies and study groups coming together with the purpose of studying Buddha Dhamma; c. the infiltration of Buddhist thoughts through many channels going on in the West today.

No Theravada (Hinayana or Southern) Buddhism has had any direct political implications, as seen above with few exception (e.g. in Burma and Ceylon this century). As long as there is stability, law and justice in the society the Dhamma and the Sangha can prevail and that is all. In Mahayana (Northern) Buddhism the situation has been different. As was seen during the Vietnam war in the 1960s and 1970s the Sangha was very engaged in political activities. Zen Buddhism in feudal Japan was the ideological basis for Samuraj regents. And Tibet was for many centuries a “theocratic” state based on Vajrayana (Tantric) Buddhism. But what about Neo-Buddhism in the third sense of the word? So far no direct political implications have been seen because of the small number of adherents. But a growing indirect influence is to be seen. People breaking out of society in order to join a Buddhist monastery as a novice or monk will have some social implications. Male Buddhists in the West, either living in a monastery or just embracing Buddha Dhamma as a view of life are very often radical pacifists denying any sort of engagement in the total defence of a country. Finally Buddhist ideas like karma and rebirth might in the long run have some implications on the social wellfare work: a man reaps what he has sown, so why interfere?
We are just at the very beginning of Buddhist activities in the West, but we must not underestimate the impact of Buddhist ethics and world view especially on young peoples’ thinking. The number of adherents is still small, but the ideas are wide-spread and through many channels they are insinuating themselves into our everyday way of thinking.
