23 December 1978

Cults Are Coming, Attracting Millions Of Nation’s Youth

Conferences & Commentaries

St, Paul Pioneer Press, September 6, 1978

The San Francisco Bay Area has become the religious cult center of America, edging out Los Angeles and New York, says Dr. Lowell D. Streiker, Mental health administrator and religion scholar.

There are literally hundreds of new religious sects on the Bay Area scene today that were not here a decade ago,” said the 39-year-old executive director of the Mental Health Association of San Mateo County.

To keep abreast of developments among these mostly youth-oriented religious grups, the prestigious Graduate Theolo¬gical Union in Berkely last year established the nation’s first center for the study of new religions.

According to a Gallup Poll, about 6 million Americans are involved in Transcendental Meditation; 5 million practice yoga; 3 million belong to Christian charismatic movements; 3 million follow mysticism, and 2 million practice Oriental religions such as Buddhism and the Hare Krishna movement.

“In the minds of many parents, the cults have replaced drugs as the No. 1 threat to our high school and college-age offspring,” Streiker said.

For these troubled parents Streiker offered some encourage¬ment, noting that “three-quarters of these kids are not going to stick with these movements,” a premise supported by other religious scholars.

Streiker says people join religious cults “to escape self-dissatisfaction, loneliness and boredom.” They are most susceptible to the “lure of a religious movement” when they are unhappy with themselves and when the demands upon them are too great and their resources too meager, he writes.

Even though most of the young cult members Streiker has in¬terviewed knew little of deprivation and were well-fed, well-housed and well-clothed, he found they suffered from “a terrible emptiness – a lack not of the necessities of life but of the values that make life human, humane and fulfilling.”

Although Streiker, who holds a bachelor’s degree in reli¬gion from Temple University and a master’s degree and a doctorate in religion from Princeton University, sympath-izes with parents who view this new religious conscious¬ness “with fear, suspicion, and loathing,” he advises them to “play it cool.”

“Children who are lost to their families after their con¬version to a religious cult were probably lost to them before their conversion,” Streiker said.

Streiker’s severest criticism of the new cults is their virtual worship of a founder-prophet,” which he says is a key element of all cults. He said he finds it difficult to find anything “particularly attractive” about the current crop of cult leaders.

“In most cases, the adherents of cults are models of the sacrificial asceticism, while their gurus and the institutions they have founded flaunt spectacular wealth,” he said.
Streiker believes money is at the root of all the “evil” that besets most of the new religious cults. He is especially in¬censed by the “deceptive manner in which they raise their money.”

Drawing on his communications background, he credited much of the ‘new cults’ success to the news media.

By calling attention to bizarre and unusual aspects of these groups, television and the press provide a “visibility that money could never buy … providing them free advertising, as it were,” he said.
