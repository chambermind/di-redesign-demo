CODE GRIBBEN: A Church Torn Apart by DIFFERENCES

ChristianCultismEvangelicalismPresbyterian

Here, Jenny Friel gives us a great overview of the PCI story. She has been able to understand what is going on, but we need to get an understanding of how to find the key to unlock the Code. The Code is a legal court-style system that is creating great hardship, but those in the Presbyterian Church think it is fine, but thereare many lying wounded on the battlefield

In his groundbreaking article in 2023, Chris Moore told the story of the catastrophic abuse suffered by Barry Reid. In the Republic, the Presbyterians are a non-story, so when we started to hear about what was happening to Steven Smyrl, we basically ignored it. We took some notice of the Rev Catherine Myers, who resigned over issues related to how the PCI dealt with her and her congregation in their Church court system and outside it in their underhanded methods.

Steven Smyrl is no longer a Presbyterian because the church he was an elder in (in Sandymount) was closed down to get rid of him. He himself was a subject of bullying and intimidation. In his case, they knew if they excommunicated him in post Catholic Ireland, he would be treated very positively, whereas the Presbyterian elders who arranged his exit would not get a favourable review.

Basically, they masterminded an attempt to get him to jump before he was pushed.  No proper procedures and no provision of an agenda, so the records of what happened could not be recorded. As I wrote earlier, they came up with a masterful strategy to get rid of him. In the Republic, there used to be a number of joint Presbyterian and Methodist churches. Normally, these churches would have had rather divergent theologies, but when in a location where one’s presence is quite weak, these differences are not as important. 

Anyway, Steven was an elder in the church. So when they could not bounce him out of the church, a decision was made to kick the stool from under him, and all these joint churches were closed, and suddenly Steven was a captain on the bridge, but the ship was sunk. We will return to his story in the future.

People like Lord Alderdice were shocked at the decision of the PCI to break with the Church of Scotland over the issue of homosexuality. He clearly disagreed with the decision to break with the Mother church, and as his concerns were not around the issues of conformity to church doctrine. But for orthodox Presbyterians you have to subscribe to the Westminster Confession of Faith.

He then joined the non-subscribing Presbyterians. In other words, like many Presbyterians with gay children or who found no space in the PCI where you must subscribe to the Westminster Confession, it is a logical move. So it was natural that the now dominant Evangelical wing of the PCI viewed all these issues as an attack on the current Moderator, Trevor Gribben, as coming from that liberal non subscribing wing of the church.

Trevor Gribben

Also, there were nearly daily pro LGBTQ attacks coming from Colin Flinn, as he singlehandedly tried to show that the current church  is not able to deal with people like him and it looked like a clear conspiracy.  But ever since Dialogue Ireland was requested to assist it became clear that the massive clear issue was best illustrated by the catastrophic treatment of Barry Reid. I have talked to a lot of people since March 2024, and nearly all are conservative Evangelicals who have become caught in a church that has a disciplinary system that is not fit for purpose. This leads to a Stasi-like situation where everyone is looking over their shoulder.

Gradually, it is becoming clear what the central issue is how they have attached power to the centre is leaving wreckage all over the place.

It is the Code, the long, boring rules and regulations used by the church to discipline its members is the key to understanding what is going on. 

The problem is that the church uses the Code in a manner similar to how Catholics use the Code of Canon Law. It appears that most of these churches do not have a tradition of church discipline.  It is more about dogmatic beliefs, subscribing to the right doctrine rather than addressing disciplinary issues. But also, human sexuality is made a test of one’s involvement with the church.

What we need is a disciplinary system that gets people to change their behaviour. Jesus suggests a loving way to address this by first making a private visit to the person who has done something wrong! 

Whereas the Code is a rigid legal document, the person is brought to a kind of court for sentencing. They are allowed to bring a friend who must be a Presbyterian, but with no legal training. First, it should not be a legal process, but rather a form of mediation. But if it is a court, imagine coming into it without representation. It reminds me of the disfellowshipping of the JW’s and the practice of cutting off people in Scientology called disconnection.

Is our Church about to lose its charitable status? If that does happen, then the financial cost to PCI would be catastrophic. Across the whole denomination, it would run to a loss of many millions per annum.  

Media pressure on PCI’s leaders had been mounting for weeks when on Tuesday, 12 September, in the Republic, RTE broke the news that an extensive dossier of complaints about our Church has been submitted by Lord John Alderdice to the Northern Ireland Charity Commissioner, alleging “a pattern of bullying, intimidation, and misuse of power by senior officials”. The news report is still available on RTE’s website, https://www.rte.ie/news/ireland/2025/0812/1527991-presbyterian-church-concerns/ 

These allegations are shocking, almost beyond belief. A week later, on Tuesday, 19 August, radio broadcaster William Crawley interviewed Lord Alderdice, former PCI minister Roy Simpson, and journalist Angela Davison, on BBC NI’s radio show ‘Talkback’. Lord Alderdice revealed that the dossier runs to more than 150 pages and details twelve sample cases. The co-author of the dossier, Roy Simpson, described the twelve as “only really the tip of the iceberg”, but that they are “very decent people, they’re upright people, they’re people who have given their lives in service to the church, they’re not cranks and they don’t tell lies”. You can read the BBC report which includes a link to the radio broadcast,  https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/ckg4l78rg94o 

In the same interview Lord Alderdice spoke about PCI’s use of non-disclosure agreements, (known as NDAs), saying it indicated a lack of openness. He went on to say, “If ministers for example do open up and try to speak about things, they’re accused of contumacy, they’re accused of bringing the church into disrepute”. 

The following day Talkback dedicated a large part of its programme to the use by churches of NDAs, referring to them as gagging orders. Mr Crawley also issued an invitation to PCI leaders to appear on the show and explain their use. But instead, PCI attempted to dodge accountability and control the narrative by issuing a statement in which it tried to legitimise its use of NDAs. You can listen to the relevant excerpt of the show using this hyperlink: https://www.bbc.com/audio/play/p0lxzpc0 

The statement claimed “PCI is a large organisation, employing over 600 members of staff…As in all such organisations, very occasionally, issues around employment arise…NDAs can be used by mutual agreement between parties as part of a resolution process and to support mutually agreed outcomes. This practice is not uncommon in organisations of our size and is always approached with care and discernment…The use of NDAs is limited, and guided by legal standards, ethical principles, and our commitment to fairness. Over the past 10 years, as part of mutually agreed settlements, PCI has been involved in a very small number of NDAs.”  

The statement was quoted by the Belfast Newsletter and later by the Belfast Telegraph. You can read the full news item in the Newsletter on its website, https://shorturl.at/OrNpS. 

The Talkback discussion about NDAs made it clear that they are rarely consensual or mutual. Typically, the party with more to lose uses their position of power to pressure the other party into signing an NDA, often by making it a condition for receiving a settlement and thereby avoiding litigation. These agreements almost always serve to suppress information that would be, at minimum, embarrassing, and moreover might imply blame, wrongdoing, or guilt.  The UK government is about to publish a ban on these NDAs.

On Saturday, 23 August, the Irish Daily Mail published a particularly in-depth article about the various allegations being made about PCI under the headline ‘A Church Torn Apart by Differences’, https://shorturl.at/yRS2t  

The next day, Sunday, 24 August, a news item was published by Sunday Life and the Belfast Telegraph with this hard-hitting headline, ‘Years of bullying drove Presbyterian ministers to the brink of suicide’. This was another claim from Lord Alderdice on foot of his enquiry into PCI’s mismanagement, included in his submission to the Charity Commissioner. Unfortunately the report in the Belfast Telegraph is only available to subscribers, https://www.belfasttelegraph.co.uk/sunday-life/news/years-of-bullying-drove-presbyterian-ministers-to-the-brink-of-suicide/a1185840575.html

The only sensitive information he could have disclosed were the facts surrounding his curious and sudden departure from the college, which were the subject of the proceedings he brought against PCI before the Fair Employment Tribunal. 

Most recently, on Saturday, 30 August, an episode of the BelTel Podcast (Belfast Telegraph) was posted online, in which BT journalist Angela Davison interviewed Lord Alderdice in depth about the dossier he submitted to the Charity Commissioner three years ago, and the background to it.   • a growing lack of trust between ordinary ministers and elders and the people they answer to in Assembly Buildings;  
  • denial of employment rights for ministers;

Beyond ministers claiming to have been driven almost to suicide, perhaps the most telling comment by Lord Alderdice concerned the imposition of NDAs – how in the vast majority of cases it signals guilt on the part of the more powerful party. It doesn’t take too much thought to realise that the party with something to hide is the one willing to pay for it to be hidden. This leads one to ask what PCI may have been so anxious to conceal when it bought the silence of Prof Laurence Kirkpatrick and Tom Finnegan? A Church torn apart by DIFFERENCES

Irish Daily Mail. 23 Aug 2025.

By Jenny Friel

Former Presbyterian elder John Alderdice says that distressed ministers were stalked, bullied and harrassed, driving them out of the Church in Ireland, yet charity regulators are still refusing to investigate where taxpayers’ money is really being spent.

A GOOD few years ago, back when Lord John Alderdice was still an elder in the Presbyterian Church in Ireland and working as a psychiatrist, he was asked to help counsel stressed out ministers. ‘It was when I was much younger, I was approached by the director of the Christian training centre who was worried about the well-being of our ministerial colleagues,’ he explains. ‘They were under a lot of pressure, so I ran a number of sessions to help them deal with all the difficulties they were experiencing.

‘Then in recent years I started getting ministers and elders contacting me, some of them I knew, others were complete strangers. But they all told me they were having a terrible time, that they’d been accused of things that were not true and effectively driven out of the Church.

‘For example, they might have been accused of having an affair with a member of the Church, it was all absurd nonsense.’

These distressed ministers told Alderdice how the investigations into these claims ‘were horrible and unpleasant’ and ‘didn’t follow due process’.

‘Basically, if some of these guys at the top were out to get you, they would do it in all sorts of ways,’ says Alderdice.

‘I thought it was terrible, and I asked these people, did you not appeal through the various mechanisms that are available within the Church? And they’d say, “oh yes, I went through all of that but got absolutely nowhere”. The control was held at the top and we got nowhere at all.’

‘People were too fearful to be included in the report’

Anxious to help, Alderdice decided to begin compiling a report into all these claims. He enlisted the help of Roy Simpson, a retired Presbyterian Church in Ireland (PCI) minister who once ran his own management and training consultancy.

‘Roy conducted most of the interviews,’ says Alderdice. ‘Then with the information, data and background I had, we put it all together.’

The resulting report runs to 150 pages and includes 12 confidential case studies of ministers and elders who’ve made disturbing allegations of being spied on, stalked, bullied and harassed by senior members within the PCI.

‘We interviewed other people whose cases we didn’t put into the report,’ says Alderdice. ‘They agreed to talk to us but were too fearful to be included.’

Like all religious orders, the PCI is a registered charity, on both sides of the border.

Alderdice submitted the report to the Charity Commission for Northern Ireland and the Charities Regulator in the Republic.

‘The reason they have charity status is because they provide a public service, but what that means is, they get huge amounts of tax relief,’ explains Alderdice.

‘So if you’re giving your money to the Church on a regular basis, the Church doesn’t have to pay corporation tax. It’s one of the reasons I picked it up. People say that because I left the Church, it’s nothing to do with me any more. Wait a minute, I’m a parliamentarian and there are huge amounts of public money involved.

‘I have a responsibility, if I think public money is being given to an organisation that is not fulfilling the requirements that one would expect a charity to fulfil, then of course I have a responsibility to say something about it.’

He says the regulator in the south has told him it will not be opening an inquiry.

‘They said there were only one or two cases [in the Republic] and that they didn’t think any law had been broken,’ he explains. ‘Of course I would like them to have done more.

‘But in the North there were a substantial number of cases and three years on, the Charity Commission hasn’t even decided whether it’s going to conduct an inquiry. They ought to be able to decide whether it’s yes or no.’

The Irish Daily Mail contacted the Charity Commission for Northern Ireland for comment, but received no response.

Alderdice’s frustration at the lack of any action or decision is palpable.

The PCI was a huge part of his life for a very long time. His father, Rev David Alderdice, was a PCI minister and he himself was an elder for more than 30 years, meaning he was elected by his congregation and ordained to serve alongside the minister in the oversight and governance of the Church.

A Liberal Democrat member of the House of Lords since 1996, he is currently a senior research fellow at the University of Oxford in the UK. But he’s perhaps best known as a one-time leader of the Alliance Party of Northern Ireland, when he played a significant role in negotiating the 1998 Good Friday Agreement.

Born in Belfast, he studied medicine at Queen’s University and qualified as a psychiatrist. He set up and ran the Centre for Psychotherapy in Belfast until his retirement from clinical practice in 2010.

Throughout his long career, the 70-year-old has been awarded a plethora of honours for his contributions in medicine and psychiatry, as well as for his political work, including the John F Kennedy

Profiles in Courage Award and the W Averell Harriman Democracy Award.

So when he announced in June 2018 that he was quitting the PCI over its decision to break with the Church of Scotland over differing policies regarding same-sex relationships, it got a lot of attention.

Earlier that month, at the PCI’s annual General Assembly, it was voted to enforce new guidelines that ban gay people in relationships from being full members of the Church or from receiving

Communion. Their children also cannot be baptised.

They stated: ‘In light of our understanding of scripture and the Church’s understanding of a credible profession of faith, it is clear that same-sex couples are not eligible for communicant

‘One person was glad his father had passed on as it would destroy him to see what has happened’

membership nor are they qualified to receive baptism for their children. We believe that their outward conduct and lifestyle is at variance with a life of obedience to Christ.’

Alderdice was not the only one to quit the PCI over the new policies, indeed it’s been repeatedly reported that many of those with more tolerant views regarding same-sex relationships have felt compelled to give up on a Church they were often raised in and devoted to.

‘When I did it, I was contacted by a number of others, including others whose fathers had been ministers and were brought up in the Presbyterian faith like me,’ says Alderdice.

‘One person told me he was glad his father had passed on, because it would destroy him to see what has happened to the Church that he loved and to which he gave his life.’

This hardening of PCI’s policies, which some have described as a movement to the right, has been in train for some time. Alderdice, and others, believe that because so many have left, those remaining have taken the opportunity to change the ethos and approach of how PCI is run.

‘Some left to join other denominations, some have lost any sense of faith at all,’ says Alderdice. ‘Many of them will say the Church left them more than they left the Church. I would say that too, it’s not the same denomination, it doesn’t stand for the same things.

‘That doesn’t mean individual ministers or members of the Church don’t stand for those things, they do. But there are others who have moved away, and they’ve created a dynamic, which in my view, has taken the Church away from where it was.’

The PCI has over 540 congregations that fall into 19 different Presbyteries across Ireland. Each year they hold a General Assembly, with around 1,300 delegates, that decides on rules and policies for the Church.

The moderator, who is elected annually and is the chief public representative, presides over the Assembly but has no power over policy. While women have been ordained as PCI ministers since

‘A cohort has made decisions outside of the Assembly’

the 1970s, there has never been a female moderator.

With just under 230,000 current members, in the last ten to 15 years the Church has seen a decline of more than one-fifth in its numbers.

Some areas have been hit harder than others. For instance, in Belfast it has shed around 40 per cent of its members.

Recent annual reports by the PCI have noted that ‘not only are we a declining denomination, but we are also an ageing one’. Figures from 2021 showed a 6.5 per cent drop in just one year.

While the PCI serves the whole of Ireland, the vast majority of the congregations are in Northern Ireland, with most in the south found on the border in counties Donegal, Monaghan and Cavan, also in and around Dublin.

There are around 400 ministers currently in active service, but those numbers are falling, and the PCI estimates by 2027 it will have around 75 vacancies.

Its recent controversies are certainly doing it no favours.

In 2023, the incoming moderator, Rev Sam Mawhinney, gave an interview in which he revealed he was not in favour of women being ordained as ministers.

The following year, Rev Dr Katherine Meyer resigned after she was twice hauled in front of the PCI’s Judicial Commission for taking part in LGBTQ+ events.

She later described the PCI’s investigation and disciplinary processes as ‘flawed and destructive’.

However, according to Alderdice, it’s not just members with more tolerant outlooks who are being targeted.

‘Some of the people put into difficulties, they weren’t theologically liberal at all,’ he says. ‘But their approach just didn’t fit in, or maybe they didn’t accept the authority of those who regarded themselves as being in charge.’

He describes this group as a ‘cohort’, which has ‘increasingly taken control of all sorts of things and made decisions outside of the Assembly’.

Even within the Assembly, he says, there are now tight controls over how debates and discussions are held. One of the ministers interviewed for the report, he says, told how he wanted to ask a question at a General Assembly but was warned that if he did, he’d be thrown out.

‘The structures were historically supposed to be democratic and open to allow those who dissented to have their say,’ he explains. ‘It’s a way of living together with differences.’

A very public case he points to is the 2019 sacking of Rev Professor Laurence Kirkpatrick from his 22-year post at the PCI’s Union Theological College in Belfast after his appearance on a BBC radio show.

‘He said he’d be very upset if any of his students who were gay were not able to proceed with their studies,’ explains Alderdice. ‘It was immediately taken up by the authorities in the Church and he was told to go.’

Rev Kirkpatrick took a case against the PCI for his sacking, and it was finally settled in June, after reaching a ‘confidential resolution’.

After leaving the PCI in 2018, Alderdice joined the Non-Subscribing Presbyterian Church of Ireland in Belfast, which he describes as ‘a small liberal denomination’.

‘But then I moved to England, and I now live in Oxfordshire with my wife and work at Cambridge University and the House of Lords,’ he says. ‘I’m a member of the Church of England now and we go to the Anglican Church in the little village we live in.’

It’s a lovely existence he admits, but he still loves and misses the PCI that he once knew.

‘I’m deeply saddened,’ he says. ‘I don’t want it to be penalised or closed down, I want it to go back to its own standards and public standards and live by those.’
