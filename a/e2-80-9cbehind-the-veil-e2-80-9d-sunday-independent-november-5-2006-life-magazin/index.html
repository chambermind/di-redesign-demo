“Behind the Veil” Sunday Independent November 5, 2006 LIFE MAGAZINE BY Niamh Horan

Islamism

“Behind the Veil”  Sunday Independent Sunday November 5, 2006

LIFE MAGAZINE BY Niamh Horan ©

For the past five years, the Muslim community has been dissected and scrutinised like never before. Not a day goes by where we open a newspaper or turn on our televisions without being faced with politicians debating issues such as the veil or Muslim religious leaders defending yet another part of their culture. As tensions have risen, with everything from the killing of film maker Theo Van Gough to the burning of the Pope’s effigy following his recent remarks, to the banning of cribs in some public places at Christmas time, it seems the idea that Europe can exist as a melting pot of cultures is dead and buried.

With between 25 and 30,000 Muslims now living in Ireland, (this years census is not released yet but could see anything up to 40,000 Muslims on our isle) the issue is on our doorsteps as to how we are going to avoid the mistakes of our neighbouring countries and integrate a range of dramatically different cultures while holding fast to our own identity. One thing we know for sure is that blindly accepting another culture’s traditions and practises is a dangerous road to take and serious questions must be asked about various aspects of others ways of life if we know they don’t adhere to our basic societal principles. But with so much conflicting information being catapulted at us from all sections of the media, and with so many people promoting their own agendas, driven by everything from political ambition to blatant Islamophobia, more and more people are viewing the Muslim debate as an issue, which is clouded in confusion.

Is the Muslim community merely another culture that our society must learn to peacefully coexist beside or does it pose a threat to our core value system? Are Muslims victims of rash stereotyping or are many harbouring some form of misogynistic views and anti-western ideologies?  Do they simply wish to quietly live by their own cultural traditions or do they hope to gradually introduce more and more of their ways of life into our society? Are we facing the same ghettoised problems as what we are seeing in the UK or is the community sufficiently dispersed so that issue won’t even arise? And arguably the most important question of all; are there extremist Muslims living in our midst? To truly understand the Muslim community and the challenges we face as we try to integrate it, we must first look at various aspects of their society. There are two Muslim primary schools operating in Dublin at the moment. The North Dublin Muslim School Project in Cabra and the Clonskeagh Muslim School. To take the latter school as an example, the numbers of Muslim children attending it have jumped from just 40 pupils in 1990 to 275 today. And even at that, the school is still turning away children because they simply don’t have enough room to cater for the demand. So what exactly is being taught in these schools? Colm McGlade is Principal at the Clonskeagh school and says that all of his pupils are Muslim, with at least 20 nationalities being represented.

“A lot of the children are Irish born and reared. Around 30 per cent of their mothers are Irish but all of the fathers are foreign nationals. The ethos of the school is distinctly Islamic but they study all of the normal subjects, like English, Maths and even Irish. Part time religious studies teachers then come in to teach the kids the Islamic religion, Arabic and the Koran. The teachers here also make a conscious effort to permeate the curriculum with Islam so, for example, when teaching singing, we teach them some Islamic songs, in history class, they learn some of the history of Islam and then in geography they are taught the geography of Islamic countries.”

He says that the school ethos also has to accommodate a number of cultural differences including the fact that parents prefer if boys and girls do not sit beside each other from second class on. With regards to the veil he remarks how he has only come across the Burka once or twice in his 19 years at the school when the parents would have wanted their child to wear it and he explains that pupils are encouraged to wear some sort of head scarf in fifth or sixth class, but that he sometimes sees the “odd one in infants coming in with the hi-jab”.  In addition to this, to accommodate the religious beliefs at the school, “from third class up pupils are brought to the mosque to pray once a day and, during Ramadan, some of the kids take part in fasting.” I ask him if he ever worries about the health implications of fasting for up to twelve hours a day for his pupils, some of whom are as young as three and four years of age.  “Well yes, at times it can be very rigorous when some of them try to do the full fast but it’s up to themselves and their parents. I remember when I came here initially and saw young children fasting and even though there were no complaints I was still a bit conscious of it but I talked to a doctor and he said it wouldn’t do them the slightest bit of harm. It’s not my place to get involved though, at the end of the day it’s up to the parents, but if I thought for a moment that it was having an adverse effect on a child I’d raise the issue. Saying that however, I suppose it’s just part of their religion and their way of life and the school has to respect that.” I also ask him if he knows what way of life is being taught during religious classes at the school.  “The religious teachers that come into the school are Muslim and I don’t particularly supervise the classes because it’s organised by the religious teachers themselves and the Imam. However there was something in the media a while ago which indicated we were teaching violence in these classes and that is completely untrue. We only teach the basic principles of the Islamic faith, the Koran and Arabic.” “I’m not of the same religion so it’s not up to me to say what should and shouldn’t be taught and I think that’s fair enough. If you had a Muslim principal in a Catholic school you wouldn’t want him to get involved in what was taught in religion classes there either. But I know these religious teachers for years and they are very upright people and I know 100 per cent that nothing but the true Islam faith is taught in class. If I thought otherwise I wouldn’t be here.” But one has to wonder how he can be so certain if he doesn’t supervise the classes or speak Arabic, the language through which the Koran is being taught. One also has to question if his idea of the true Islam faith is the same as some of the Muslims who are involved in the running of the school.

I ask Khalil Quzi, the head of the school’s religion department what is taught to the children during the classes. “Religion classes are taught in English and the Koran is taught through Arabic. But we only teach the script, we don’t teach translations or anything like that, we only teach the pupils how to read the Koran and there are only a limited number of chapters that we read. I ask him how he deals with teaching children that acts such as suicide bombing is wrong when they may hear some media reports detailing how some Muslims are using the Koran to justify violence. He says, “My concept is very clear; I tell them that suicide bombing is not allowed in my religion. The challenge is trying to tackle these ideas and trying to explain that people who say they carry out these acts in the name of the Koran are wrong.”

“I remember the day after the September 11th attacks happened we had assembly in the school and I made a point of making it very clear to the pupils that whoever did this was totally wrong.”

Even though Quzi’s views are moderate, one source, who wishes to remain anonymous, tells me that his intelligence has led him to have doubts over certain connections with the Clonskeagh mosque, beside which the school resides.

“What we are being told is that there are no radical Muslims attached to Clonskeagh. If that’s the case then what do you call the European Council of Fatwa and Research (ECFR), which is permanently headquartered there? The Imam of Clonskeagh, Sheikh Hussein Hallawa, is permanent Chair of the organisation, an establishment which promotes the use of suicide bombers to kill innocent civilians, advocates violence against homosexuals and supports the practice of female genital mutilation.”

But the head of religion at the school completely distances himself from the council. “The Imam is criticised for his beliefs in Ireland and I for one completely disagree with him. We have no connections with that organisation here and I have witnessed meetings where he has been criticised by Muslims and scholars in Ireland for the beliefs that he has. It is totally unaccepted here”

But still, Mike Garde, Director of Dialogue Ireland Trust is surprised at government official’s reaction to the extremist views of the European Council of Fatwa and Research.  “Recent media reports said the jury is out as to whether this is moderate or not. We have a very strange concept of moderate in our society at the moment. Suicide bombings, killing homosexuals and female mutilation, wouldn’t you call that extreme? And yet the President of Ireland recently visites the Islamic Cultural Centre in Clonskeagh.”

Taoiseach Bertie Ahern has also visited the centre and Garde says that although Irish representatives should visit Muslim groups, it is important to ensure they respect the Irish constitution and its legal framework. So is this the case?

Garde explains, “One leading Irish Muslim has said his ultimate goal is to subvert the constitution of the Irish Republic by introducing Sharia law. That indicates the goal they have is not integration.”

Meanwhile, Sheikh Dr. Shaheed Satardien is an Imam who delivers sermons each week to a group of Muslim followers in Blanchardstown. As a young cleric and political activist against the Apartheid system he had to flee South Africa in the 1970’s after his involvement in a student uprising. Again, in 2002, he had to flee Saudi Arabia, after he spoke out against idolizing Osama Bin Laden following the September 11th attacks. He says that since he has moved to Ireland he has been issued more threats by members of the Irish Muslim community, after he spoke out against extremism in this country. “I have been told that I mustn’t make trouble for myself because what happened in South Africa, where I was issued death threats, could happen again here.”  He believes that Islamic leaders in Ireland are in denial about the radicalisation of young Irish Muslims and claims that some even put on a front for the Irish public knowing full well that what goes on behind closed doors is a very different reality. “When I spoke out about extremism in Ireland some of the big guns in the Muslim community came to visit me after one of my sermons in Blanchardstown and told me I had to deny there were any extremists living in Ireland because it would cause Islamaphobia. They also asked why I was speaking against suicide bombers, saying it was an integral part of Jihad. But you have to understand I cannot protect the Muslim community at the expense of the rest of society. If I do that then it will only exacerbate the problem.”  Shaheed believes some Muslim leaders have to put up a façade for the media because the fear a backlash from the Irish community while claiming that certain views about western society are only spoken about very covertly. “I have been to sermons where Imams condemn Christmas in Ireland. They speak to the Muslim congregation talking the wrongs of Jesus and how we shouldn’t celebrate Christmas.”  “I fear for this society if things carry on the way they are going. I’m not raising a scare-mongering flag, I’m just pleading with people to address the problem of certain extremist views in the Muslim community. I don’t want us to end up like Britain. What makes us think we are so different? Muslims all over the world are affected deeply when Palestinians are being punished by Israelis, when the Iraq war is going on, even Muslims in Ireland feel very strongly about it. What makes you think the same type of things that happened in other parts of Europe can’t happen here?”

Undeniably, Britain is currently facing a multicultural dilemma now in a whole range of areas. Take honour killings and Heshu Yones, a 16 year old girl who lived in London with her Kurdish family, as an example. She struggled for so long to be allowed to integrate into western society the way she wanted to. She tried to dress like westerners and even had a Christian boyfriend. A decision that would ultimately prove fatal. The teenager was stabbed to death by her father, Abdullah, who strongly disapproved of her independent attitude.  That incident happened four years ago and since then their have been countless more so called honour killings in Britain, at a rate of about 12 a year, to be more precise. A UN report has also estimated that, worldwide, the figure of women being killed because they are deemed to have brought dishonour on their families and communities could be as high as 5,000 women a year, more than 13 a day. But that problem is so far removed from our shores isn’t it? Surely there are no terrified young Muslim women living in our midst, with an indescribable fear of what would happen if they even dared to do something so simple as remove their veil in public, right? Wrong.  Fatima* (name changed) is a 22 year old girl who was born in Ireland to Iraqi parents. When I ask her what would happen if her father knew she had done something as simple as attend a friend’s party on her University campus, she shakes her head and says it doesn’t even bear thinking about.  “I’d run away rather than face my parents if they found out. That’s what I would do. It wouldn’t be worth it to go home. My dad is very, very strict. You have no idea”. She looks over her shoulder again and again as she talks, her eyes darting around the room, trying to see if there’s anybody listening in nearby, before she turns back to me, her hands practically shaking at the prospect of being caught, as she attempts to relay her fears. “Please, you have to understand, I’m just so scared, if anyone heard, if I was ever found out, that would be it.” I met Fatima* earlier that evening at an Ireland UK Islamic Student Society event where hundreds of students had gathered in a hall in UCD to break their fast together at the beginning of Ramadan.  When I entered the hall, the first thing that struck me was the definite divide right down the middle of the room. On one half, the girls sat covered head to toe in scarves and traditional religious dress, while on the other side of the room, the round tables were filled with young Muslim men, only some of whom were wearing the traditional headpiece.

The young Muslim student manning the door couldn’t have been more welcoming. I explained where I was from and why I was there and told him I was happy to sit at the edge and simply observe. But he wouldn’t hear of it and, after much insistence, ushered me to join a group of young girls at one of the tables in the middle of the room and placed a plate brimming with spicy eastern food at my place. I couldn’t help but wonder if a Muslim had wandered into an Irish event completely uninvited would he have gotten the same hospitality.  And it was there, amongst the welcomes and the chatter and the celebratory atmosphere at a religious gathering, that I met a girl so frightened and anxious of breaking her family’s customs that if the truth came out, she is adamant she would flee rather than face the unspeakable punishment.  Her hair was completely hidden by a headscarf and she was covered from top to bottom in a traditional dress so only her pretty face with her wide brown eyes were on show. In a soft spoken Irish accent she began to tell me how she’s spent all her life living in Ireland, and at first comes across as a happy go-lucky girl, making an effort to spark up a conversation with me and smiling widely when I ask her to explain the divide between the sexes at the social event.  “At an Islamic society event we run it according to our beliefs, when we socialise, we always set a limit. I was brought up to believe that there’s a certain line that you never ever cross and because of it I know myself I’m more relaxed around Muslim women. You can’t really express yourself, friendliness-wize, in front of Muslim men; there are always certain limits you have to respect.” In front of her fellow Muslims she comes across as an obedient follower of Islam and an ideal daughter in the face of all that is expected from her strict Muslim family. She talks of the Muslim stringent no alcohol policy labelling drink a ‘poison’. “For us, we just see it in a bad light. It’s probably hard for you to understand because here it is so normal to go out and have a couple of drinks at the weekend, but you have to ask yourself ‘why is it ok?’ when it only leads to all these other problems.” She also explains that any physical contact with a male is strictly forbidden. “No kissing, no boyfriend, no physical contact until we are given the go ahead to get married and until then he definitely couldn’t see me without my headscarf on.”

“So you’re telling me you’re in your twenties and you’ve never even experienced kissing a boy?” I ask. She begins to get a little unsettled looking and scans the table to see if any of her friends are listening in before leaning closer and saying she’s afraid to continue talking. “I’m sorry, I would love to talk to you” she whispers, “It’s just not here, I can’t.” I ask her if she’d come outside with me for a while so we can continue our conversation and she obliges. I make my way over towards the doors that I came in and as we walk through, she stalls and pulls me back for a moment giggling. When I ask what’s wrong she explains that I’m leaving through the male exit, another symbol of a world it seems she has become fenced into, always expected to keep at her own side of the divide, a place for everything, and everything in its place, so to speak.

Once away from prying ears she settles into telling me how hard it is to live by Muslim expectations in an Irish society. “Sometimes I get so upset. It’s the little things. I try to hang out with Irish friends as much as possible but there’s always a point where my customs come into play. If there’s a party on campus, I can’t go because there will be alcohol and boys there. Even if I don’t touch a drink, it doesn’t matter, I’m not meant to be around it.”

“I remember I couldn’t even go to my end of college graduation ball because there would have been alcohol at it. I was so upset. And even if I was allowed go what would be the point? I wouldn’t have been able to wear a dress like the other girls; I would have had to wear a headscarf covering my hair, long sleeves and a dress down to my ankles. I wouldn’t even have been able to join in the dancing because there would have been boys on the dance floor.”

I ask her if she has ever crossed the forbidden line, remarking that she seemed a little unsettled when I mentioned kissing earlier in our conversation. “Yes well I have kissed boys, both Muslim and Irish. I have taken off my headscarf once or twice with them too. But only when we’re alone and never ever when anyone else can see. When I do it I feel so vulnerable, it’s hard to describe, I almost feel naked. I don’t look the same without my headscarf on.” She stalls for a moment as if she’s just snapped back into reality. “If my father ever found out he would kill me.”

What would happen? “Where my parents come from, there are serious punishments for that type of thing.” I ask if she fears physical punishment. “Well yes, I would definitely have to get beatings, but I’m telling you, beatings wouldn’t even be enough. Especially if they found out I had kissed an Irish boy. It would be unimaginable, the punishment, I don’t even want to think about it.”

She spins around abruptly again when she hears some Muslims who have wandered out of the hall chatting near by. She continues talking as she looks over her shoulder every few seconds, making sure they haven’t gotten any closer into earshot.

“None of my Muslim friends know. I am living in fear because I’ve started to go out more to parties and stuff and when I go to a club I obviously stand out from the crowd. I’m always afraid that I’m going to be caught.”

I ask her what’s the hardest part of living by her traditions in a western society. “I’m always afraid I’ll fall in love with the wrong guy. Whoever I get married to, it will be someone who my dad wants. If a man wants me as a wife he has to ask me for my dad’s number to see if my dad is happy with him. He has to talk to him first and if he’s not what my parents are looking for then I wouldn’t even get a chance to meet with or even talk to him. If my dad does like him then I’m never allowed to be alone with him, I have to meet him in a public place and my parents will know exactly where we are and for how long.”

“I’m really scared of falling for a guy my dad doesn’t know about. It happened before. I met a guy I really liked but I knew my dad wouldn’t like him so I couldn’t carry it on.”

So is this what we are producing? A new generation of young Muslims who are experiencing the most frustrating kind of identity crisis? Who are torn between the old ways of their Muslim parents, where they speak Arabic in the home, try to live up to their strict upbringing and adhere to certain principals like arranged marriage which many of their young Irish friends would find appalling? While, in the outside world, these same young people are trying their best to mix with a bunch of Irish friends who they’ve perhaps plucked up the courage to talk to on the first day of college, who invite them out to parties on campus and discuss gossip and boy troubles with them as they would with anyone else? And all the time the young Muslim is torn between two polar opposite cultures. Their friends telling them it’s ok to have a couple of pints, their parents calling it a poison, their friends introducing to them to that good looking guy or girl they’ve noticed in their lectures while at home, their father is insisting that only a Muslim background will do, while screening all potential partners.

Are the new generation of young Muslims born and reared in Ireland facing a lose-lose situation? It seems, try as they might, they can never fully integrate with their Irish counterparts to the extent that they wish, that we would like them to. There will always be some element of there culture at odds with ours, mentally pulling them back at the last minute, like an elastic band, snapping them back into the reality of their cultural traditions.

Although Fatima’s experiences give me a very unsettling view of Islamic culture and the grim prospects of trying to integrate such differing customs and beliefs into our society, I meet two young students later on in the evening who have a very different view of growing up as a Muslim in a western society, trying to follow Islamic traditions.  Zayd Cajee and Nedal Alkatib are both 20 year old students from Canada and Melbourne, respectively. I tell them about the encounter I had earlier that evening and ask if the girl’s predicament is common place is Islamic society.  Zayd, a 2nd year medicine in UCD, says on the contrary, that he grew up in a very liberated Muslim family where segregation was never even an issue. “I think a lot of Muslims are very misunderstood. Some people tend to paint us all with the same brush. It’s not like that for all of us”  Nedal, a 3rd year medical student in the RCSI agrees. “That type of situation is merely related to the type of upbringing you have and not religion. I think people sometimes make the mistake of confusing culture with religion and for example, what you saw inside tonight, where the room was separated into tables for boys and girls, that’s more to do with cultural baggage than their faith.  So what about relationships, how much is out of bounds for them? “I do try and socialize with girls as much as I can” he smiles and the two lads laugh “but at the same time I want to be serious about my religion, I don’t want to mess around.  But saying that, if I did fall in love with someone, and we both decided it was the right thing to do then why should that stop us from doing anything? If we were committed to one another then nothing should be out of bounds.”  The view he has certainly seems to be a world away from the anxiety of the girl I spoke to earlier for who, being at the same party as a boy brought about serious consequences. So do they feel stereotyped as Muslims?

“The very least I think is that we’re misunderstood,” says Zayd “but yeah, we’re definitely stereotyped. If someone was to look at us and take us at face value they could say that we were freaks for not going to nightclubs and drinking but there is a lot of logic behind our ways, you just have to bother to ask questions and look for the reasoning behind it. I don’t want to make out that we’re victims or anything; I mean everyone is stereotyped to a degree aren’t they? I just think Muslims tend to be all brushed into one big pile by society and certain sections of the media. But then again the blacks had to deal with it, so did the Irish and the Jews, so I guess it’s just an act that’s prevalent in society.”  Zayd tells me that, because he looks more typically like a Muslim than his friend Nedal, sometimes the stereotyping can turn into blatant harassment.  “Nothing has ever happened to me in Ireland but when I was living in Canada I had one or two incidents. The week after the September 11th attacks I was playing a very heated soccer match and, as you can imagine, the atmosphere was still incredibly tense after the attacks, and a guy from the other team shouted at one of his team mates to ‘mark Osama Bin Laden over there’. I was absolutely devastated. I’m telling you I was nearly in tears. That really affected me. That someone could relate me to that act purely because of the colour of my skin was very hard to comprehend.” “Another time it was just a simple road rage incident when somebody shouted at me to go back to the caves to Osama Bin Laden.”  Nedal laughs as he recounts a story about the time he was looking for an apartment to rent in Dublin and a potential landlord was giving him an informal interview before they finalised the deal. “When it came up that I was a Muslim he just stood their looking shocked and said ‘but you look so normal!’ it was very funny but it tells you a lot about the view some people have of Muslims”.  Although tensions are no where near as high as they are in the UK at the moment, with two cultures living so closely together on one island and having such dramatically differing belief and value systems, it’s easy to see how a conflict of interests can occur. Many people question the supposed reluctance of the Islamic community to bend for our customs as much as we do for theirs, saying that Muslims want respect here but they don’t want the responsibilities that come with it. For example, when western women enter certain parts of the Islamic world they are expected to wear the appropriate dress and yet many Muslim women living in western society demand that they are allowed to keep their traditional dress code at all times when seen in public.  But Yahya al Hussein, an Imam at the South Circular Road Mosque, believes you can’t compare the two. “There is no comparison. There is no problem if a Muslim woman wears the traditional dress here, it doesn’t go against any law, but if a woman from the West is not wearing something appropriate then it is against the law in certain countries.  He continues by saying there are other laws, which work in favour of Western society. “If a Christian is living in a Muslim country they are entitled to inheritance under the family law of that country but that is not the same for Muslims living here. If a Muslim dies, then his legacy is distributed in accordance to the law of this country.”  In an age where many of us walk on politically correct eggshells and are afraid too question another group’s customs or laws for fear of being labelled a ‘racist’, it is no wonder that many people winced when they tuned into the news last month and saw that Jack Straw, a politician with a sizeable population of Muslims in his own constituency, had questioned the practice of covering ones face behind a veil, saying that cutting off eye contact led to a barrier being placed between the wearer and the rest of society.  Quiet predictably, there was uproar from certain sections of the community and, despite his array of pro-Islamic work and the fact that he is held in high esteem by many Muslim leaders, both in his constituency of Blackburn and further afield, the Leader of the Commons was accused of showing total disrespect for other people’s religious practices. But the question needed to be asked. If not for communication purposes, then purely for the reason of basic human rights. Ironically, critics of Straw’s comments argue that people should have the freedom of choice to wear what they want. And yet in some sections of the Islamic community, it seems some women’s peers are taking that very choice away from them, forcing them under no uncertain terms to wear the traditional dress or face the consequences. Take my earlier encounter for example. And how many more of these young women live among us?  Perhaps it is an opportunity for Muslim leaders such as Yahya to lead the process of getting their own members to debate in an open manner, their thinking on why they don’t wish to give freedom of choice to their families and move some members of the Islamic community towards a more open minded way of thinking.

There’s no doubt about it, there is a key generation of young Muslims living in Ireland today. They are the stepping-stone between the strong Islamic tradition their fathers have grown up in and the principles and ideals of Western society that they are now immersed in. True, they have a great responsibility on their shoulders to hold fast onto their cultural identity while living in such juxtaposing surroundings but, with leaders like Yahya Al Hussein who tells me he denounces extremism and promotes the need to respect each others differing lifestyles to lead the way, they also have the chance to prove the predictors wrong.

Indeed, during an interview at his South Circular Road office, Ya Ya Al tells me he condemns the September 11th attacks saying “I think many people would agree, regardless of their faith, that there is something wrong with what is happening in Iraq and Palestine but if you are angry with the British or American government, this is not the way to go about it, any religion will tell you, killing innocent civilians is wrong.”  I ask him if he believes that someone who dies fighting for his religion will go straight to heaven.  “There is a text where Prophet Mohammed says that a martyr who dies for a good cause will be admitted into paradise but who is a martyr? That’s the question. Those who fight against the Russian occupation in Afghanistan and fight communism, in the view of some Muslims, are considered martyrs, the person who fights to defend his religion and his country.”

So does he thinks there have been any post September 11th martyrs? “I can’t recall any right now”.  Some of Ya Ya’s critics would question his condemnation of the September 11th attacks and say that he uses clever tactics to answer media queries. I hear sheer frustration in the voice of Shaheed as he tells me “the reason Ya Ya can condemn the attacks is because he believes Muslims didn’t do it. Muslims all over the world believe 9/11 is a conspiracy. They believe that the MI5 and the CIA are responsible for it. That’s a common belief in Muslim communities.”  Sure enough, a number of Muslims I interviewed in the process of researching the article presented me with the same September 11th conspiracy theory.  Indeed even when I talked to the head of the religion of the Muslim National school in Clonskeagh and asked him after he condemned the attacks if he thought Muslims were behind it, he said “I really am totally confused, when I see people carry out such acts in the name of Islam I doubt his religion and his faith so I think he might have been brainwashed.” By whom I ask? “I don’t know, I don’t have any evidence. I just think there are a lot of strange things happening at the moment and I am very confused as to who was used to commit these acts.”  Shaheed continued saying, “Ask Ya Ya if he condemns suicide bombers in Palestine and Iraq and then that will shut him up. Ask him to condemn Abbas Bugrab. He left Lucan and was jailed in Northern Ireland for downloading bomb making material like the type of material the 24 people who were arrested in London had. That idea was hatched here in Ireland by him. Ask Ya Ya what he thinks of him and I guarantee you he’ll say he doesn’t know him. Ask him to condemn his actions and I tell you he’ll refuse because he’ll say he doesn’t know him.”

Ya Ya Al Hussein came across as a very moderate Muslim leader who called for integration, greater understanding of the Muslim community and condemned under no uncertain terms various high profile terrorist attacks. This new assertion surprises me, to say the least, so I go back and put these questions to Sudanese Imam. “Why does this man say these things? He knows nothing about me. I don’t believe at all in conspiracy theories so yes I do think Muslims were behind the September 11th attacks.”  Next I ask him about Abbas Bugrab. “I don’t know the man, I never met him so I can’t comment on his actions.”  I explain that Bugrab was arrested for downloading bomb-making instructions to which he replies, “Do we even know what he was going to do with the information? And you must remember that this information was already on the Internet so whoever put it there is as much to blame as Bugrab? But just because someone put it there does that necessarily mean that he wants to blow up a plane?”  Finally I ask him if he condemns the actions of suicide bombers in Palestine and Iraq. “Well this is another issue altogether. It’s different; Palestine and Iraq are war zones, it’s not like the United States or Madrid. We have the right to fight against occupation so no I don’t condemn them.

Are they Martyrs? “It’s not up to me to decide whether they are martyrs or not. I don’t know who’s going to go straight to heaven and who is not.”

As soon as I press him on his views on suicide bombing, the calm and collected tone he has presented his answers with until now suddenly disappears and instead he raises his voice saying, “What are you trying to do? If you want to turn Muslims into enemies then the Muslims are ready and everyone will suffer from the consequences” before hanging up the phone.

I tell Mike Garde, Director of Dialogue Ireland, that during my interviews most of what I’m being presented with is a series of conflicting arguments as to whether or not there are extremist Muslims operating in Ireland to which he replies, “My concern is that (until now) he is giving you information you want to hear so that you will write about it and then other people will read it and believe it too. But who are you going to believe? The Imam or Shaheed, who spent three years attending the mosques and studying with principal Muslim characters and who is telling us that the Muslim community is preaching one thing and doing another. What has he to gain for telling us that? All he is getting from it is threats from Muslim leaders. The key thing for Irish people to understand us that a lot of the personal conversations between Muslims that contain extremist views are spoken through the Arabic language so if a journalist goes and interviews a Muslim leader they mightn’t get the full extent of their views but a sanitised version of his beliefs.”

Kamel Ghanem is an Algerian Muslim who is living in Ireland for the past 30 years. He strongly disagrees with the idea that there are extremist Muslims operating in Ireland.  “If people do believe there are extremist views being shared at different gatherings then why aren’t terrible things happening here in Ireland as well? Have you seen any? No. And why? Because most decent Muslims, if they came across such things, would immediately report them to the police.”

He continues, “I am part of the older generation of Muslims who have literally spent most of their lives living and working here. I believe we are Irish as much as any other person living here. We have to stand beside this country and I certainly feel great pride as a Muslim living here.” Perhaps Kamel in his simple estimation of what it’s like to be a Muslim and to be as ‘Irish’ at the same time, hits the nail on the head as to what we need if we are to live side by side with Muslims in Ireland.  A Mike Garde argues, “It seems, out of everyone, the US model is getting it right, in that they have a melting pot of cultures. There aren’t any Irish people living in America only Irish Americans, the same way as there aren’t any Iraqis living there only Iraqi-Americans.  The same with Arabs, Libyans and so on. I think we need to let Muslims become as Irish as the Normans before.”  To do that however, we have to ensure that Muslims aren’t living parallel lives in an Irish society. And so, first and foremost, many would argue that we must work against the ghetto situation that we see in Britain, France and Holland which it seems has brought about a generation of Muslims who are becoming harder and harder to assimilate into the country’s society.  Perhaps the fact that there are no Muslim secondary schools in Ireland, presents us with an ideal opportunity to work towards the melting pot ideology. This means that, after their children have gotten a strong Islamic grounding in an all-Muslim primary school; Muslim parents must accept the fact that they have to integrate their children into second level schools where the vast majority of the student population is Irish. So by the time the children reach third level they will have been working towards an Iraqi-Irish mentality, for example, from a very young age and will be much better equipped to deal with the responsibility of juggling an Islamic culture in a western society.  In the meantime however, the next chapter of Muslim Ireland has yet to unfold and the fact remains that we are relying on ourselves as much as any member of the Muslim community to make sure it develops in a way that respects both side’s cultures and value systems while still strongly reflecting the Irish framework in which it exists.
