More tax, same bite -what do we know about oil?

Tony Quinn

More tax, same bite

(Millions and Millions missing in Belize as Quinn is put centre stage.  The Government have called in the international auditors.)

Posted: 05/02/2010 – 10:29 AM

Author: Adele Ramos – adelescribe@gmail.com

http://www.amandala.com.bz/index.php?id=9510

There has been much talk in the recent weeks about what everyone concerned is getting from the petroleum pie. The figures keep changing, but public sentiments of skepticism and calls for proper accounting from both Belizeans and investors remain unabated.

In 2006, under pressure from the public, the Said Musa administration hiked the income tax rate from 25% to 40%.

Ironically, it turns out, however, that the Government and people of Belize are still getting the same cut of gross revenues as they were receiving before the tax hike.

In 2006, when Belize Natural Energy (BNE) rejected the tax, saying that it would “kill” BNE, its figures indicated that GOB would move from getting 23% of gross to 33%. Latest figures say that GOB is still getting 23%, despite the tax hike—$64 million short of projections.

To do this story, Amandala had to review publications spanning four years, from 2006 to 2010.

BNE, the only petroleum producer, had claimed in its Energy Exchange Report of February, 2008, that GOB was getting 65% of profits; today, February 3, 2010, it reports in the latest edition of Crude Facts that GOB is getting a 58% take.

For the period 2005 to 2009, says BNE’s Crude Facts titled, “How the oil is shared,” it has netted US$326.1 million (or BZ$652.2 million) in sales for Belize’s high quality light crude, low in sulphur, to the local and world markets.

There has been much debate over Government’s take of oil revenues, which BNE reports as 58% or $151.6 million, based on net revenue of $218 million and roughly $45 million in royalties.

BNE claims it has earned, for itself and CHx—joint venture partners, a total of $111 million in profits (which comes after all expenses are deducted). The two companies BNE and CHx split the profits 60-40.

Even though BNE and GOB claim that the government’s take is more than half the net, serious questions have been raised over the nearly $400 million that BNE has charged in expenses between 2005 and 2009.

Moreover, a study of past reports and today’s figures reveals a glaring irony: Today, even with the increase in income tax rate to 40%, the split of gross revenues is, according to BNE’s numbers, the same 23% GOB was getting when the tax rate was 25%.

This is according to BNE’s own numbers presented at a press conference in June 2006, as well as updated figures we received today.

In June 2006, when BNE challenged GOB over the increase in the income tax rate from 25% to 40%, the company said that the hike in the tax rate would mean that GOB would get 64% of profits.

BNE’s numbers today say, however, that GOB is getting 58% of what the industry describes as the “take”—what’s left from oil revenues after expenses are deducted.

Data presented by BNE on that occasion in 2006 indicated that the split of gross petroleum revenues was 23 cents on the dollar for GOB, and 77 cents for BNE. That’s when the tax rate was only 25%.

(Then director Sheila McCaffrey had said the split would move from 50-50 for GOB and BNE to a 63-27 split, in favor of GOB.)

At the time, BNE had reported sales of 344,000 barrels, netting US$20.6 million, with US$4.67 million to GOB under the then arrangement. (US$4.67 million out of US$20.6 million is 23%.)

With the new tax, BNE data had indicated, GOB would get a third (33%) of gross monies collected from total oil sales and 64% of profits, close to the figure quoted two years later, in February 2008.

In its latest edition of Crude Facts, BNE attempts to justify the figures with a Commonwealth Secretariat report titled, A Review of Petroleum Fiscal Regime in Belize (2006), saying that the state take should be 45% to 55% or higher for marginal to low profitability developments, but higher (55% to 60% or more) for highly profitable developments. The company also contends that the state take is in line with those for “frontier countries” which don’t have an established industry.

On Tuesday morning, Andre Cho, the director of the Geology and Petroleum Department, told Amandala that GOB’s receipts were $120 million out of $592 million earned from the sale of 4.8 million barrels of Belizean crude.

The department had said that BNE had reported expenses of $318 million up to 2008. However, BNE’s latest figures tally to $384.2 million up to the end of 2009 (the last year being un-audited). Net revenue, BNE says, amounts to US$108.9 million (BZ$217.8 million).

We know that there are those in government, as well as BNE shareholders, who have been raising hard questions over the expense deductions that BNE makes, as well as payments to related parties, such as Maranco, owned by Paul Marriott of the UK, who, at the time of their partnership, was considered a major shareholder in BNE and company director.

Also, BNE does business with CHx and the CHx subsidiary, Blue Sky, which does some crude oil processing for the market; and ASG, which has done seismic work for the company. (See more on CHx elsewhere in the Amandala.)

Some investors have also expressed concerns over a US$42.5 million (BZ$95 million) loan BNE took in 2009, which they said they did not know about. (The collateral charge document was done by Barrow and Williams, the Prime Minister’s law firm.) Shareholders did not, and still have not gotten, a dividend, despite declared profits, and instead got a loan against future profits for last Christmas.

Even with the existing disquiet over the BNE-GOB deal, the Barrow administration has gone on record to indicate that it would honor the existing agreement with the oil company. Prime Minister Dean Barrow has furthermore made accusations of mischief-making and ignorance with the BNE issue.

“What has upset me is the level of ignorance, which has then served as a platform for people to make mischief,” he told Channel 7 news Wednesday.

When asked about Belize’s take of petroleum revenues, Barrow, the Minister of Finance, indicated that he was unsure what the exact figure on the dollar really is.

Asked whether his government would revisit the BNE deal, Barrow said, “…I am going to say very clearly at the risk of any political fallout that might ensue, that I have no—absolutely no—intention in the current circumstances of unilaterally breaking the contractual arrangements that govern the BNE situation. … At this juncture, I don’t believe that we can do any better in the context of the practicalities of the situation, including the legal contractual arrangements.”

As the Leader of the Opposition in 2006, Barrow, also an attorney, was quite vocal about Belizeans getting their fair share of the petroleum pie.

At the introduction of the Income Tax amendment bill to increase the rate from 25% to 40%, he had also commented, “Clearly the populace, the electorate, wants to be certain that this country will get the full benefit of the oil industry; we’ll get (to the last penny), [our] just desserts.”

Barrow later had said, on November 17, 2006, when the House had to revisit the bill because of Senate objections to tax rate and retroactivity of the bill:

“In the same way that this government [then speaking of his rival, PUP] has in fact utilized the powers of the sovereign legislature to introduce their 40%, a succeeding government will similarly be in a position to utilize the sovereign powers of the legislature to ensure that excessive profits, on the part of BNE to the prejudice of the Belizean people, will not be allowed to stand, and we serve notice that the situation will have to be revisited, because of what we are convinced now is the truth. What has been proposed, what has been passed, is far from fair to the Belizean people.” (Channel 7 transcript.)

In line with that position, after Barrow took office in 2008, he implemented a windfall tax, a campaign promise, but placed the threshold at a high US$90 a barrel. Prices promptly fell below that mark and remained below it, leaving GOB with zilch in collections from the new tax.

While some have been suggesting a revision of that windfall tax threshold, the Barrow administration has not flexed its sovereign legislative muscle to amend it.
