King of Kings Church Tuam

Controversy over King of Kings continues

THE TUAM HERALD  21st July 2004

By Claire Gallagher

WHILE a follow-up report in a Sunday newspaper suggesting that “a sinister cult had taken a grip” of Tuam is regarded locally as exaggerated, former members of the controversial King of Kings Fellowship (KoK) remain fearful of its leaders.

As more information is revealed about the workings of the movement previously known as the Tuam Christian Fellowship, families of members and former members continue to contact The Tuam Herald with stories of how the KoK controlled every aspect of members’ lives.

One man explained that when his wife joined the group his marriage broke up, and his family were taken away.

He now claims that his former wife, who received half of the family’s assets in the separation, is now penniless.

A former treasurer of the Tuam sect has also come forward with claims of “spiritual abuse, abuse of money and families.”

John L’Espoir, now living in Holland, went as far as saying that in the early days of the Church when he was in charge of finances a substantial amount of money was coming into the church — “substantial with a capital ‘s’”.

He said that originally people were encouraged to give 10 per cent of their earnings, a donation that was voluntary. After a while this donation became compulsory.

The money collected, he claimed, was used to support the Church leaders.

He said a lot of money went to them and that as treasurer he often asked for receipts, none of which were available.

The Company Registration Office confirmed to The Tuam Herald this week that King of Kings was registered as a limited company on September 29, 2000.

Meanwhile the Tuam Christian Fellowship is still registered as a charity with the Revenue Commission.

Local gardai had received no complaints or reports into the workings of the KoK as of Tuesday, July 20.

Some small business people in the town still live in fear of speaking out against the group, claiming that their businesses have been affected by the KoK and in many cases lost altogether.

Some former members are now claiming to be in serious debt as a result of their involvement.

One ex-member explained to The Herald that the KoK system worked like a pyramid, with the pastors at the top.

Blessings ten-fold

One leader, Mary Sheridan, is reported as having told members that if members blessed the pastors financially the blessing would start at the top and flow onto the other members of the church who would be blessed ten fold.

When others criticised the church she would say “what they accuse us of is what they are guilty of themselves”.

Former members speaking to The Herald following last week’s report have also accused the KoK of intimidation, spiritual abuse and greed.

One former member recalled vividly the public humiliation experienced, the loss of self belief and the financial burden it placed on families.

She said she was originally “loved into the Church”, through words of encouragement and praise.

Once a member, she was expected to attend prayer meetings or women’s meetings at least five times a week.

On Sunday the main service could take up to four hours, beginning at 10.30 am.

During the service the leader, or as she preferred to be known the pastor, Mary Sheridan would speak for hours often building to loud ranting or “talking in tongues” if she felt there was “witch craft among the congregation”.

Another one-time member explained that she sat nervously waiting for the pastor to arrive in Church every Sunday, as the pastor’s mood often determined how many deliverances or exorcisms she would perform.

As a self professed prophet she alone had the authority to do these exorcisms and as one ex-member explained you could not question her or you would be accused as being in rebellion and therefore in need of deliverance yourself.

She said “there was no peace” as the Church ate into all free time.

Fear also played a large part in members’ loyalty to Mrs Sheridan as she claimed that members would die in a car crash if they left, as had happened to one former member.

While describing the process of her teachings as a form of “Boot Camp” Mrs Sheridan said it would weed out the weak members while leaving the Church’s leaders.

She intended for the Church to grow to thousands of members by bringing 12 women up around her who would eventually recruit 12 more members and so on.

The Tuam Herald made several attempts to contact this group leadership, but our calls were not returned.
