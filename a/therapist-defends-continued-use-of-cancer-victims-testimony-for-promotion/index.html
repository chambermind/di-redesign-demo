Therapist defends continued use of cancer victim’s testimony for promotion

Therapy Groups

Healer uses dead patient in book PR; Therapist defends continued use of cancer victim’s testimony for promotion, writes Mark Tighe

By Mark Tighe, The Sunday Times    22 May 2011

© 2011 Times Newspapers Ltd. All rights reserved

A “BIO-ENERGY healer”, who has been endorsed by Michael Flatley and DJ Carey, is continuing to sell a book and promote his services using testimony from a woman “cured of cancer”, even though she died more than two months ago. Just Imagine: A Life Without Illness, by Michael O’Doherty, was published in November 2009 with a quote on the cover from Hilary O’Carroll, one of the healer’s clients. “I was diagnosed with ovarian cancer in December 2008 and told I had 18 months to live,” she said. “After treatment from Michael O’Doherty, my July 2009 scans showed no cancer.”  Inside the book O’Carroll says: “Now I’m cured thanks to Michael O’Doherty.” O’Carroll died in Milford hospice in Limerick on March 6. Yesterday, O’Doherty defended his continued use of the quotes for promotional purposes.

He said they do not mean he is portraying himself as having cured O’Carroll.

A former prison warden and martial arts instructor, O’Doherty has received a series of celebrity endorsements. He attributes his career as a healer to Gay Byrne and the exposure he received during a Late Late Show appearance in 1990.

Among those to have credited him with curing illnesses or injuries are DJ Carey, the former Kilkenny hurler, and his partner Sarah Newman, a former Dragons’ Den investor; Matt Molloy, a musician with the Chieftains; Davy Fitzgerald, the Waterford hurling manager; and Gary Kelly, a former Irish soccer international.

Michael Flatley, the dancer famous for Riverdance, has credited O’Doherty with returning him to health after a debilitating virus.

The healer has defended a number of controversial opinions attributed to him in the book. He insists women should not regularly check their breasts for cancer as “if they kept checking for lumps that is exactly what they would eventually find”. He also claims drug companies are not interested in creating a form of chemotherapy to cure cancer because “sickness is a big money-making business”. John Crown, a senator and oncologist, said such beliefs are nonsense. “I’ve treated far too many drug company executives and their families who’ve had cancer to buy into the theory that drug companies are suppressing an effective treatment.

It’s a hideous accusation to make,” he said. “After 25 years of oncology practice I am highly sceptical about unproven alternative therapies. I have never seen one work.”

O’Carroll was the keynote speaker at O’Doherty’s book launch in November 2009, announcing she had been cancer-free since her treatment with him, having earlier been given 15 months to live. She said in sessions with O’Doherty when he was “balancing my energy”, she visualised “a warm healing light spreading throughout my body and melting the cancer tumours”.

O’Doherty denied trying to portray himself as having cured the Limerick woman.

“What it says are the facts,” he said. “The scans were clear up to January 2010; these were Hilary’s own words. She was going to the medical people and they were saying to her ‘your scans are clear’.

“The work with us helped her scans be clear 15 months after she was diagnosed. If a drug could extend someone’s life by double what they said [was left], they would probably win the Nobel Peace Prize.”

O’Doherty insists O’Carroll’s life was extended by working with her energy, modifying her diet and other treatment. He said doctors had detected secondary cancer in O’Carroll from March 2010 and he continued to work with her up to a month before her death. He said he didn’t charge her for treatment.

Ronnie Walker, O’Carroll’s husband, said he had not read the book and was unaware of what way his wife’s quotes were used. “I know Hilary had a very postive frame of mind and was better able to cope with the psychological effects of her illness from her dealings with Michael,” he said.

Despite testimonials on the book jacket and on his website to the contrary, O’Doherty insists he has never claimed to be able to cure people. “We do not cure illnesses,” said O’Doherty. “Our purpose is to administer therapy and to help people heal themselves.”

John O’Connor, managing director of Blackwater Press, O’Doherty’s publishers, said O’Carroll’s quotes were not misleading.

“I met Hilary personally and she assured me everything she said was accurate,” he said. “Everything in the book was accurate at the time.”
