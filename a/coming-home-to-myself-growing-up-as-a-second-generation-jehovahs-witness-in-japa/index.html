Coming Home to Myself: Growing Up as a Second-Generation Jehovah’s Witness in Japan

ChristianJehovah's Witnesses

Toru Onuki’s new book shares his experiences growing up as a Jehovah’s Witness in Japan, leaving the group as an adult, and the struggle of adapting to a new world where his own decisions would now shape his reality.

In his own words, Toru has kindly shared his personal story with Dialogue Ireland, as well as offering insight into the movement’s history in Japan, changes in its ideology and practices in the years after he left, and humbly hoping that his book helps ‘people with similar experiences to know that their past was not entirely wasted, that the good qualities they developed still have value, and that they deserve to live their own lives.’

The following account has been translated from Japanese.

My name is Toru Onuki. I was born in 1966 and live in Kitakyushu, on the island of Kyushu in southwestern Japan. I am sixty years old. I grew up as a second-generation Jehovah’s Witness, and this year I published a memoir, Coming Home to Myself.

I am sharing my experience because I hope someone with a similar upbringing might feel less alone. I also hope that parents and their adult children can understand one another, even when their beliefs are no longer the same.

Growing up within a faith

When I was in kindergarten, my mother became a Jehovah’s Witness after one of her former classmates introduced her to the faith. The religion became the world in which I grew up.

Both of my parents were pioneers (Jehovah’s Witnesses who devoted substantial time to preaching), and my father was also an elder. After graduating from high school, I too became a pioneer.

In the second grade, our family moved to Saga Prefecture for missionary activity. In the sixth grade, we returned to Kitakyushu. I changed schools about four times during elementary school. Each move meant leaving friends and trying to find my place again.

At school, I did not salute the flag or sing the national anthem. I wanted to play with other children after school, but friendships outside the religion were restricted. In high school, I explained to my teachers that I could not take part in martial arts classes for religious reasons. I did exercises or cleaned the training hall instead. I wanted to join the same classes as my friends, but I kept that wish to myself.

“It was not that I disliked my friends. I wanted to play with them, and I said no anyway.” (Chapter 1, PDF p. 10)

At home, I was sometimes struck with a belt as religious discipline, known as “the rod.” I also heard warnings that I could be destroyed at Armageddon. Before I was old enough to choose my beliefs, I had learned to be afraid of getting things wrong.

As a child, I nevertheless regarded the Witnesses as a serious, respectable religion. I did not see it as a group engaged in bizarre or criminal activities. That memory exists alongside the pain of the restrictions I experienced.

A little history, and why the movement grew

Christianity’s history in Japan long predates my family’s involvement with the Witnesses. The Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier arrived in 1549. Persecution followed in later decades, and the Tokugawa government imposed a nationwide ban in 1614. Some communities preserved their faith in secret through generations; the ban was lifted in 1873. [1, 13]

The Witness movement was also present before the Second World War. Junzo Akashi returned to Japan as its representative in 1926, and a branch office opened in Kobe in 1927. The movement was then known locally as Todaisha. Its opposition to militarism and emperor worship brought it into conflict with the state, and members suffered arrests and imprisonment in 1939. Missionaries restarted the work after the war, in 1949. [14, 2] This history of persecution deserves to be acknowledged alongside the experiences of children who later grew up within the movement.

Research helps explain its later expansion. Mizuho Yamaguchi’s study of the mid-1970s to the mid-1990s highlights the time devoted to house-to-house preaching and “pioneering,” the prominent role of housewives with non-Witness husbands, and the close relationship between the Japanese branch and world headquarters. [3] Nelson also discusses how religious involvement could meet social and spiritual needs among women who had lost local community connections. [2] These are historical explanations, rather than a claim that every person joined for the same reason.

Remembering 1975 as a child

In the years leading up to 1975, publications and discussions among Jehovah’s Witnesses contributed to expectations that the year might have particular significance, and some expected Armageddon to come around that time.

I was already attending meetings and taking part in religious activities with my mother during the period of expectation surrounding 1975. I remember her often speaking to her brothers and sisters about that year.

When Armageddon did not come, she seemed embarrassed to me. I was still a child and did not fully understand the significance of what was happening, but I remember that there was criticism at the time.

The organisation’s later account acknowledges that its publications helped build expectations around 1975. [4] That historical record is separate from what I understood as a child. I would not describe that disappointment alone as the reason I eventually left.

Why I wanted to leave

The strongest reason was the pain of continually suppressing ordinary human wishes, especially the feelings and desires that arise when one is young. So much of the life expected of us seemed to move in the opposite direction from ordinary life and the world around us.

As I came to know the outside world, I felt a very strong longing for its freedom. I no longer felt drawn to the religious way of life.

A parent who chooses a faith out of deep conviction may accept its restrictions willingly. A child brought up within it has not necessarily made that same choice. For me, the gap between my own feelings and the life I was expected to lead was very painful.

This is why the expressions closest to my experience are shūkyō nisei—someone brought up in a parent’s religion—and “restrictions imposed by faith.” I do not often use the word “cult” when speaking about my own upbringing. What I most want people to understand is what it felt like to grow up within limits I had not chosen.

My departure did not follow a systematic investigation of theology or biblical interpretation. By then, I no longer felt any desire to undertake that kind of study. I did not want to keep examining explanations for expectations that had not been fulfilled.

My responsibility, and life afterwards

I married at around twenty-five, within the faith. We had two wedding ceremonies: one with people who shared our religion, and another arranged by my grandfather. Later, our daughter was born. There was real happiness in our life together.

But I was unfaithful. I hurt my wife and daughter, and I take responsibility for what I did. My upbringing does not excuse that choice.

When we divorced, my daughter was two. After completing the paperwork, I looked for her in the car park. A car carrying her passed in front of me, and my wife held her up so that I could see her through the window. I called my daughter’s name. The car drove away before I could hold her.

I need to distinguish my responsibility for that hurt from my reasons for leaving the religion. It would not fully describe my experience to say that I simply left because of a moral failing while my attachment to the faith remained intact. My longing for freedom and my difficulty with the life expected of me were central.

After the divorce, I returned to meetings for about a year. Although the elders considered me repentant and I was never formally disfellowshipped, other members of the congregation did not speak to me; only the elders did. Since I had never been formally disfellowshipped, there was no formal reinstatement process.

Around the age of thirty-one, I stopped attending meetings. Leaving did not make me immediately free inside. I carried guilt, loneliness, and the habit of putting my own feelings aside. There were also friendships, work, travel, and happy moments. My life afterwards was not only suffering, but learning to make my own choices took time.

Changes since my time, and the next generation

In my time, we attended gatherings on three days each week. Today the organisation describes its worship meetings as twice weekly. It has also made videoconferencing available for people unable to attend in person. [5, 6] I have noticed literature stands in public places, although this has not replaced house-to-house preaching altogether. [7]

There have also been policy changes that need to be described accurately. As a child, I understood that I must not accept a blood transfusion, even if my life depended on it. I was too young to weigh medical or ethical questions for myself. It felt as though the answer had already been decided for me. That childhood understanding should not be confused with every detail of the organisation’s present policy.

On 18 September 2026, it announced that accepting the four main blood components—red cells, white cells, plasma and platelets—would be a matter of individual conscience. Its prohibition on transfusions of whole blood remained. [8]

I also associated disfellowshipping with losing people. As I understood it then, someone who had been expelled could no longer be treated as an ordinary friend; even a greeting seemed forbidden. I feared what leaving might mean for my relationships, including my family. It is important to distinguish my childhood fears from a blanket claim about every family. In its 2024 guidance, the organisation allowed members to use their conscience about inviting certain people removed from the congregation to a meeting, or offering a brief greeting there. Restrictions on socialising remained, with exceptions to the greeting provision for people regarded as actively promoting apostasy or wrongdoing. [9] Its current explanation also distinguishes the situation of a removed person living with a Witness spouse and young children, stating that normal family dealings continue within that household. [10]

Some practical burdens appear lighter than they were in my day. My description of physical punishment refers to my own childhood; I cannot speak for every family today. Nor can I know the organisation’s motives for each change.

For me, the deeper question is whether children can develop their own wishes, make their own choices, and remain supported by their families. Changes in schedules or wording do not, by themselves, resolve that question.

Almost all the second-generation Witness friends I grew up with have since left the religion. This is my own circle and generation, not a national survey of young people today. I cannot give a reliable figure for Japan as a whole. My view is that, as young people discover the wider world, restrictions that run against personal freedom and the diversity of modern life will make it difficult to keep their attachment. That is my view, rather than a statistical prediction.

Other religions and Japanese everyday life

After leaving, I did not consider joining another Christian church. I now visit Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples in the ordinary course of life—for example, at New Year or when visiting a well-known shrine while travelling. This has not involved joining a new religious organisation.

I regard Aum Shinrikyo, with its violence and crimes, as a cult. Reports about the Unification Church’s donation-related problems also led me to compare religions with the environment I knew. Among the Witnesses, although donations existed, I do not remember being required to sell everything needed for daily life in order to donate. I am describing my own experience, rather than every Witness household.

For me, these comparisons do not erase the restrictions I lived with. I could regard a religion as respectable and still find its demands deeply painful.

What I hope will change in Japan

Following the 2022 killing of former Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, the term shūkyō nisei and the difficulties faced by people raised in a parent’s religion entered much wider public discussion. [12]

I feel that Japan has begun to look at these experiences differently. What might once have been dismissed as a private matter within a devout family is increasingly discussed in terms of children’s rights. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s guidance issued on 27 December 2022, addressing child abuse associated with religious beliefs, is one concrete example. [11] For me, speaking about what happened is also a way of saying that a child’s pain deserves to be heard.

What pains me most is the position of children brought into a religious life before they can choose, whose everyday lives and future options are then restricted.

I hope Japan will become a society where children can choose their education, values, and direction in life, whatever their parents believe. If they decide to leave a religion, they should be able to begin an ordinary life without being left isolated.

What I hope for parents and children

I no longer want to spend my life resenting my parents. I believe they sincerely wanted us to be happy and tried to give us what they thought was right. Recognising their intentions does not erase my childhood pain or make physical punishment acceptable. I want to acknowledge both their intentions and what I experienced.

Children have lives of their own. If a child leaves the faith, I hope their parents will support them as they find their own way, including a life outside religion. Even when they cannot share the same beliefs, I hope parents can say, “I want you to be well. I’m here for you.” If their child is struggling, that care matters just as much as when things are going well.

I have lost family members to suicide. I do not believe those deaths can be explained by a single cause. But those losses have made this wish especially important to me: that we place a person’s life and well-being before whether they remain in a religion. Above all, I hope parents will want their children to stay alive, feel supported, and find some joy in living.

I especially wish that parents would not blame themselves endlessly simply because their child has left. That choice is not, in itself, a failure of parenting. Different beliefs do not have to erase the love and meaningful experiences a family has shared. Being able to say, “I’m glad you’re happy,” might be a beginning.

For those who grew up in the faith, I hope there can be room for joy as well as painful memories. I do not ask anyone to forgive, reconcile, or reach the same conclusions that I have. Each person has their own history and pace.

What I am looking for now

Religion once occupied the whole of my life. It was the entire world in which I lived. Perhaps that is why I do not now feel a need to search for another particular teaching or a single correct answer to life.

I want to live within the natural rhythms of an ordinary day. Rather than searching for a great answer somewhere else, I want to value my own feelings and what I experience in daily life, and live each day with care.

Today I work in the driving school industry and live with my cats, Moe and Suzu. I value time with friends, travel, a hot spring, and quiet moments at home. At sixty, I am learning to give my own feelings a place in my life.

I do not see having been a Jehovah’s Witness as a shameful stain on my past. Alongside the pain, I believe that upbringing helped me develop compassion, love for others, humility, a willingness to listen and learn, and honesty. These are strengths I can now use in the wider world and in contributing to society.

I hope others with a similar upbringing can recognise the strengths they have nurtured, too. Leaving the religion does not mean we must reject everything we have become. We can carry what is valuable into the rest of our lives, use those strengths freely, and feel proud of what we have to offer.

“I, too, am allowed to live an ordinary life. I am allowed to walk a path that feels right to me, rather than living only to meet someone else’s expectations.” (Chapter 18, PDF p. 105)

If my article or my book helps even one person feel less alone, or helps a parent support a child whose beliefs have changed, I will be glad. I did not write it to persuade everyone to abandon their faith. It is one person’s account of learning to live with his past and choose what comes next.

You do not have to follow my path. I simply hope you know that your feelings matter, and that there is still a life of your own to live.
