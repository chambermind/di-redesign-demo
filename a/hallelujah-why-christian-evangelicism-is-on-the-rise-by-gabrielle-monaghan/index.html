Hallelujah ~ Why Christian Evangelicism is on the rise by Gabrielle Monaghan

EvangelicalismVictory (Faith Movement or Prosperity Gospel) Archive Abundant Life

Sunday July 1, 2012

© 2012 Times Newspapers Ltd. All rights reserved

Evangelicals…………………………..


En route to a motorcycle rally in Co Meath this weekend, no one will mistake Nigel Stephens for anything other than a biker. But the insignia on the leather jacket worn by the 41-year-old as he rides his Ducati 800 arouses curiosity. It reads: “Christian Motorcyclists Association” and “Riding for the son”. The son, in this case, is the son of God and the bike rally Stephens is organising is a booze-free event, oiled with nothing stronger than a Christian rock band whose songs eschew the swear words typical of the genre. Stephens is the national secretary of the Irish branch of the association, which was set up in America in the 1970s to spread the gospel to motorcycle enthusiasts. Stephens and his brother Graham are so passionate about the evangelical group, they opened Ireland’s first biker church, in Gort, Co Galway, last January. “If Christ were here on earth he’d be a biker,” Stephens said. “Having faith is a waste of time and nothing but hypocrisy if all you’re doing is sitting in a church once a week. When Jesus was on earth, he was out on the streets, dealing with people in trouble.” Stephens, a sales rep by day, spreads the gospel on Facebook and at biking events, where his group strives to persuade Ireland’s “unchurched bikers” to see the light. “If you go to secular bike clubs, it’s all booze and drugs,” Stephens said. “We are not there to condemn them but we want to offer a different way, to say there is an alternative faith, to say you don’t need to continue down this road of depression.” The Christian Motorcyclists Association is just one of a slew of new brands of Christianity seeking to fill the spiritual vacuum left by the dwindling devotion to a Roman Catholic church beset by scandals over the cover-up of clerical sex abuse of children.  The 2011 census shows that the vast majority of Irish people still define themselves as Catholic. The fall since the last census, in 2006, has been slight, down to 84% from 87%. However, the number of followers of new Christian churches has surged, albeit it from a low base. The number who call themselves Orthodox, Apostolic or Pentecostal increased to 14,000, up 73%. The Redeemed Christian Church of God, with roots in Nigeria, now claims 12,000 members in Ireland.

The surge in Christian alternatives to Catholicism is in sharp contrast to the declining attendances at the country’s dominant church. A survey of Irish Catholics by Amarach found just a third went to weekly mass, while church teachings on sexuality had “no relevance” for three-quarters of them. In Dublin, weekly mass attendance is down to just 14%, Archbishop Diarmuid Martin has said. When the International Eucharistic Congress opened in the capital last month, only about half of the expected 20,000 pilgrims showed up.  Evangelical and Pentecostal churches, with their charismatic leaders, colourful styles of worship and youthful congregations, offer an alternative to jaded former Catholics. So are Irish people for turning?

JUDGING by the middle-aged couples swaying self-consciously to the music at the Victory Centre on Sunday mornings, some former Irish Catholics are willing to expand their religious horizons. The Victory Christian Fellowship (1) opened this slick church near Firhouse, Dublin, in November 2009 at a cost of €12m, on foot of some generous donations.

Before church members enter the 1,000-seater amphitheatre for some US style evangelism, they can choose from large screens relaying energetic sermons, a Starbucks, a restaurant and comfortable armchairs. Inside, unlike their Irish counterparts, African and Asian church-goers dance in time with singers on stage, accompanied by an in-house band.  Parents can drop their kids off at the Victory church or their teenagers at the Hangout, a youth centre with pool and snooker tables, Nintendo Wiis and Play Stations.  It’s a far cry from a hard pew in the parish church.  Evangelicalism, (2) including the ritual of water baptisms in later life, has several followers in Irish celebrity circles, including Shane Lynch of Boyzone, a born-again Christian. Katie Taylor, Ireland’s boxing hopeful in this month’s Olympics in London, reads the Bible and listens to hymns on her iPod before she gets in the ring.

Irish interest in alternative Christian faiths has developed not just because of a drift from Catholicism but because of the recession, according to Seán Mullan, who spent 22 years as a pastor, most recently at an independent Evangelical church in Blanchardstown, west Dublin. Mullan sits on the board of Evangelical Alliance Ireland, which aims “to see the kingdom of God permeate every sphere of Irish society”. “There is definitely a sense that when the other supports that people had in life, like material wellbeing and work, were pulled from beneath them, they found themselves needing spiritual values,” he said.

The fastest-growing church in Ireland, according to Mullan, is the Redeemed Christian Church of God, with 12,000 members and 100 “parishes”. Planning files show this Pentecostal movement is bombarding Irish city and county councils with applications to set up churches in buildings vacated by recession-hit businesses. Redeemed Christian Church of God opened a church last month in a former battery company at the back of the Guinness factory in Dublin, and has applied to authorities in Cork, Kilkenny and Meath to open more outlets. The church, which has grown from modest roots in a shantytown in Lagos to having a presence in more than 100 countries, has even applied to the Department of Education to become a patron of schools* (3) planned for Navan, Co Meath and Lucan, Co Dublin.  African-led Pentecostal churches are expected to double in number in Ireland to 250 within 10 years, says Livingstone Thompson, a Jamaican academic who does cultural-awareness training in schools and government departments.   “The most popular way that these churches are formed is that an individual charismatic leader, who may have been a member of an existing church, will initiate a new church and become its leader,” he said. “This church then joins a successful brand, such as the Redeemed Christian Church of God.”

He added: “I would not regard this growth as a numerical threat to Catholicism but the vitality of these churches, coupled with the increasing number of people who prefer to define themselves as atheist, is changing the religious landscape.”  While Irish-led Christian churches such as Victory enjoy a moment, African-led Pentecostal churches are struggling to grow. Thompson said: “In Navan, where I live, I’m beginning to notice a white flight from one congregation. The main reason is the zeal of the new  immigrants, which is challenging the traditional worship style of Evangelical churches. It’s a bit much for some Irish people.”  Even by the passionate standards of African churches, the Redeemed Christian Church of God is renowned for the intensity of its prayers. So much so that in Nigeria it has been called “the weeping church”. During services, members of the congregation will clap, whoop and speak in tongues, which Pentecostals believe to be the verbal expression of the “holy spirit”. Congregations pursue extreme prayer so that gay people and even autistic children can be “saved”.  “Autism tends to be seen as an indication of a possession within the family — that there are some unclean spirits,” Thompson said. “When they pray, it is so that someone be relieved from that spirit. So they are appealing to God, for instance, that someone would be delivered from homosexuality. But it is not an elaborate event like an exorcism.”

However, Michael Kelly, deputy editor of The Irish Catholic, the religious newspaper, says evangelical movements may hold clues for how the Catholic church could reinvigorate itself. “The Catholic church, institutionally, has huge problems with Pentecostalism because it doesn’t accept that the baptisms are valid,” he said.  “But where the Catholic church can up its game is by looking at how Pentecostal and Evangelical churches build a strong sense of community. I’ve seen this firsthand in Britain, where young Christians who have to leave London and move to the north of England for work seek out a Pentecostal church. They create a ready-made community, with, for instance, motherand-baby groups. That is an attractive proposition for cities like Dublin, where it can be hard to meet people.”  While Catholic liturgy has limited room for change, some of its churches are attempting to attract a younger congregation. This is manifested in Dublin churches such as in Rathmines and on Upper Gardiner Street, where mass is held at about 10pm on Sundays to attract young crowds who would never rise early enough for morning mass. Congregations are treated to gospel music, Kelly said.  Stephens is aware that entertainment is the key to keeping the fire of enthusiasm lit at the Christian Motorcyclists Association. “There’s no organ — we sing praise and worship songs with a lively young band,” he said. “We also give them a little biker’s Bible. It has a cover picture of handlebars in front of a stretch of open road. It’s called Hope for the Highway.”
